BEFORE YOU BEGIN

About this book

This is a complete course in histology: the microscopic structure of cells, tissues and organs, the way that structure serves function, and the way it fails in disease. It is written to be read on its own, from the first page to the last. Every explanation, table, identification task and question is complete in itself.

The book is organized in seven parts. Parts I to V teach the subject in the order in which it is best learned: the cell and the techniques that make it visible; the four basic tissues; blood, circulation and defense; digestion and respiration; and finally excretion, reproduction and the special senses. Part VI condenses the whole subject into comparison tables and a rapid-review chapter. Part VII is a hundred-question examination with explained answers.

How to use the images. Histology is a visual subject. Appendix D provides nine original schematic teaching plates to show relationships among tissue structures. These are not photomicrographs, are not drawn to scale, and do not replace authentic stained sections or laboratory instruction. The chapter descriptions identify the cellular features, tissue boundaries, layers, ducts and follicles to look for in real slides. Read a chapter, compare its schematic where relevant, then test the identification on actual microscopy material.

A note on numbers. Values are typical adult human values unless stated otherwise. Ranges differ with method, fixation, species and observer, and even well-established figures vary a little between references. Where authorities differ, this book gives the range or says which value is being used. Percentages are approximate. No number in this book should be treated as a diagnostic threshold; clinical decisions require clinical sources.

Clinical material. The clinical notes show why histological structure matters. They are written for orientation and are not advice about diagnosis or treatment.

Scope and limits. This is an independent educational work. It is not a substitute for laboratory teaching, for a course in medicine, or for supervised clinical training. No third-party micrographs or textbook plates are reproduced. The original schematics in Appendix D explain patterns but are not evidence of actual slide morphology.

How to study

If you have six weeks. Work through Parts I to V at roughly one part per week. Read a chapter, then close it and write its comparison tables from memory, then answer its recall questions. In the sixth week, revise with Part VI and work through the hundred questions in Part VII.

If you have ten days. Two or three chapters a day. On the first pass read the explanation and the tables; leave the recall questions for the second pass. Concentrate on six comparisons: the epithelium types, the connective-tissue fibers, the cartilage types, the muscle types, the leukocytes, and the wall of the gut.

If you have 48 hours. Read Chapter 24 end to end, then the hundred questions, then the ten master tables. Do not attempt the prose. Histology rewards pattern recognition under time pressure, and patterns live in tables.

Whichever plan you use, alternate reading with retrieval and slide identification. Rereading can support initial comprehension, but testing yourself from memory and explaining visual evidence more clearly reveals what you can recall.

The Twelve Laws of Histology

These twelve rules of thumb hold across most of histology. Each has exceptions, and the chapters point them out. When you are unsure how to approach a structure, reason from them.

  1. Structure follows function, and function follows structure. Most visible features are a solution to a physical problem — diffusion distance, mechanical stress, surface area, water loss, conduction speed.
  2. Form is set by the apical surface, the basal surface, and the junctions between cells. Look at all three before naming a tissue.
  3. Epithelium sits on a basement membrane and is normally avascular. Nutrients reach it by diffusion from vessels in the underlying connective tissue. The exception worth knowing is the stria vascularis of the cochlea, which contains capillaries within the epithelium.
  4. Connective tissue is defined by its extracellular matrix, not its cells. The matrix tells you the mechanical job: tension, elasticity, compression, or filtration.
  5. Basophilia and eosinophilia report what a cell is doing. Blue-purple staining (hematoxylin) reflects nucleic acid — rough ER and ribosomes in a protein-synthesizing cell. Pink staining (eosin) reflects abundant protein — cytoplasm, secretory granules, collagen. The correlation is strong but not absolute: mucin and lipid droplets stain poorly with either dye.
  6. Cells that make steroid hormone are full of smooth ER and lipid droplets — hence foamy, pale, or vacuolated cytoplasm (adrenal cortex, Leydig cells, corpus luteum).
  7. Muscle is classified by nucleus position and striation, not by shape alone. Peripheral multiple nuclei = skeletal; central single nucleus with striation = cardiac; central single nucleus without striation = smooth.
  8. The number of cell layers is one clue to function, not a complete rule. Simple epithelia often specialize in exchange, absorption or secretion; stratified epithelia often resist abrasion. Cell junctions, surface specializations and location also matter.
  9. Surface area is bought with folds. Microvilli, villi, plicae circulares, basal infoldings, and cristae all exist for the same arithmetic reason.
  10. Tubes are built in concentric layers, and each layer has one job. In blood vessels: intima for exchange, media for tone, adventitia for anchorage. In the gut: mucosa for digestion, submucosa for supply, muscularis for movement.
  11. Capsules, septa, follicles, ducts and vascular organization help identify solid organs. A capsule or hilum alone is not diagnostic: distinguish lymph node, spleen, thymus and glands by their characteristic parenchyma and routes of blood, lymph or secretion.
  12. Most histological facts have a clinical counterpart. Linking a structure to a disease makes it easier to recall and easier to apply.

Exam technique

Answering a slide or image question

Never begin with a guess. Use this sequence every time:

  1. Magnification and staining. H&E at low power versus PAS versus silver tells you what the preparation was designed to show.
  2. The boundary. Is there a free surface? A lumen? A capsule? Blood vessels inside the tissue? This immediately separates epithelium-lined organs from solid organs.
  3. The dominant tissue. Note the arrangement — cords, acini, tubules, follicles, layers, bundles.
  4. The one diagnostic cell. Parietal cell, Paneth cell, podocyte, Purkinje fiber, club cell, Sertoli cell, odontoblast. Examination images often turn on a single diagnostic cell.
  5. Name it, then justify it in the same sentence. “This is thyroid gland because the parenchyma is arranged in follicles of colloid lined by a single layer of cuboidal cells.” A justified answer shows that you understand the image rather than merely recognizing it.

Answering a “describe and give the function of” essay

Use a fixed skeleton — it works for any organ:

Capsule → cortex → medulla → parenchyma arrangement → cell types → blood supply → ducts or outflow → function → clinical correlation.

Adapt the headings to the organ. Cortex and medulla are not universal; hollow organs are better described by their wall layers and luminal specializations.

Ten common errors

  • “It looks like…” without saying why it looks that way.
  • Naming a cell without its product or function.
  • Confusing “basophilic” (blue; acidic components binding a basic dye) with “eosinophilic” (pink; basic proteins binding the acidic dye eosin).
  • Saying “simple squamous” for endothelium without noting it is a type of simple squamous epithelium.
  • Using “mucous” for “mucosa” — different words, different things.
  • Describing the adrenal medulla and cortex as one tissue; they are embryologically unrelated.
  • Calling transitional epithelium “stratified squamous”; the umbrella cells are unique.
  • Forgetting that the thymus has no follicles and the spleen has no afferent lymphatics.
  • Mixing up the two adrenal cortex zones that produce cortisol and aldosterone.
  • Writing “bone is dead tissue” — osteocytes are metabolically active and connected by canaliculi.

PEARL — In a viva or OSCE-style histology station, the task is usually to connect a structure to a function and a disease. Practice every fact in a three-part sentence: “Structurally X, which allows Y, and when it fails you get Z.”

PART I — CELLS AND TECHNIQUES

1 · Histology & Its Methods of Study

In this chapter: Preparation of Tissues for Study · Light Microscopy · Electron Microscopy · Autoradiography · Cell & Tissue Culture · Enzyme Histochemistry · Visualizing Specific Molecules · Interpretation of Structures in Tissue Sections

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Trace a tissue from patient to stained slide, and explain what each step preserves.
  2. Predict what hematoxylin and eosin will color, and why.
  3. Choose the right special technique for a target molecule (carbohydrate, lipid, protein, nucleic acid, antigen).
  4. Explain how section thickness, fixation, and artifact can mislead you when you interpret a two-dimensional image.

The core idea

Histology explores the body’s tissues and how they are arranged to produce functional organs. Tissues have two interacting components: cells and extracellular matrix (ECM). The ECM is not packing material — it supports the cells, carries the fluid that brings nutrients and removes waste, and its molecules bind specific cell-surface receptors connected to structural components inside the cell, forming a continuum in which cells and matrix function together.

Organs form by an orderly combination of these tissues. Because cells and matrix components are small, histology depends on microscopes and molecular methods — and many routine bright-field images come from fixed, dehydrated, cleared, embedded, sectioned and stained tissue. Frozen sections, live-cell imaging and some specialized methods use different workflows. Knowing what each step does tells you which appearances are real and which are artifacts.

Preparation of Tissues for Study

Step What it does
Fixation Pieces of organ are placed as soon as possible in stabilizing or cross-linking compounds. Formalin — a buffered solution of formaldehyde, made by diluting the 37–40% stock to about 4% — is widely used for light microscopy; glutaraldehyde for electron microscopy. Both react with the amine groups (NH₂) of proteins, preventing degradation by proteases; glutaraldehyde also cross-links adjacent proteins. Fixative must diffuse fully through the tissue, so organs are cut into small fragments, or fixed by vascular perfusion
Dehydration Water extracted gradually through increasing ethanol solutions, ending in 100% ethanol
Clearing Ethanol replaced by an organic solvent miscible with both alcohol and the embedding medium; the tissue becomes translucent
Infiltration Tissue placed in melted paraffin at 52–60 °C in an oven, which evaporates the clearing solvent
Embedding Paraffin allowed to harden in a small container at room temperature
Sectioning Block trimmed and cut on a microtome. Paraffin sections are typically 3–10 µm; EM requires sections less than 1 µm

A concentration note. Formalin is often described as a buffered isotonic solution of 37% formaldehyde. That 37–40% solution is the stock from which working fixative is made; tissue is fixed in 10% formalin, equivalent to about 3.7–4% formaldehyde, buffered to pH 7.2–7.4 (neutral buffered formalin). Keep the two concentrations apart in practical questions.

The units you should be able to convert: 1 µm = 1/1000 mm; 1 nm = 0.001 µm = 10⁻⁶ mm; 1 Å = 0.1 nm = 10⁻⁴ µm.

Plastic resins are used for both light and electron microscopy. They are infiltrated after dehydration and harden when cross-linking polymerisers are added; some resins can be polymerized at lower temperatures than paraffin processing, but resin embedding does not eliminate processing artifacts, and some protocols use heat.

CLINICAL — Biopsies are tissue samples removed during surgery or routine procedures, fixed in vials of formalin. When the result is needed before the procedure ends — typically to know whether a growth is malignant before the patient is closed — the specimen is rapidly frozen using an appropriate cooling method and sectioned in a cryostat. Direct immersion in liquid nitrogen is not universally used and may introduce freezing artifacts. Freezing is also used for histochemistry of sensitive enzymes or small molecules, because freezing can preserve enzyme activities that routine fixation may diminish; and because routine organic solvents extract many lipids, frozen sections are often preferred for demonstrating neutral lipids.

Staining

Most cells and extracellular material are completely colorless. Dyes behave like acidic or basic compounds and form electrostatic (salt) linkages with ionisable radicals of tissue macromolecules.

Term Meaning Examples
Basophilic Anionic (net negative) components with affinity for basic dyes — nucleic acids, glycosaminoglycans Toluidine blue, alcian blue, methylene blue, hematoxylin
Acidophilic Cationic components with many ionised amino groups, staining with acidic dyes — mitochondria, secretory granules, collagen Eosin, orange G, acid fuchsin

H&E is the most common method. Hematoxylin stains DNA in the nucleus, RNA-rich cytoplasm, and cartilage matrix dark blue-purple; eosin stains other cytoplasmic structures and collagen pink and acts as the counterstain. Trichrome stains (eg, Masson) allow greater distinction among extracellular components.

The PAS reaction uses the hexose rings of polysaccharides and other carbohydrate-rich structures and stains them distinctly purple or magenta — the basis for demonstrating glycogen, mucin, and basement membranes. The Feulgen reaction is a distinct DNA-specific method: controlled acid hydrolysis exposes aldehyde groups in DNA, which then react with Schiff reagent.

Enzyme digestion identifies what a stain is binding: pretreating a section with an enzyme that digests one substrate. Pretreatment with ribonuclease greatly reduces cytoplasmic basophilia with little effect on the nucleus, proving that the cytoplasmic staining depended on RNA.

Lipid-soluble dyes such as Sudan black reveal lipid-rich structures — provided the processing steps that remove lipids (heat and organic solvents) are avoided. Metal impregnation with silver salts visualizes certain ECM fibers and specific elements in nervous tissue. The staining procedures used for most light micrographs are conventional, and the ones you will meet are summarized below.

Slide preparation, from fixation to observation, takes 12 hours to 2½ days, depending on tissue size, embedding medium, and staining method.

Light Microscopy

The bright-field microscope has three lens sets:

  • Condenser — collects and focuses a cone of light onto the slide.
  • Objective — enlarges and projects the image; routine objectives are ×4 (large field), ×10 (medium), ×40 (detail).
  • Eyepiece (ocular) — magnifies a further ×10, giving total magnifications of ×40, ×100, ×400. Total magnification = objective × ocular.

Resolving power — the smallest distance at which two structures are seen as separate — is the critical factor, and conventional bright-field light microscopy typically achieves lateral resolution of about 0.2 µm under favorable conditions. Higher magnification alone cannot recover detail below the resolution limit. Anything smaller or thinner than 0.2 µm (a single ribosome, a microfilament) cannot be distinguished, and two mitochondria closer than 0.2 µm appear as one. Resolution depends mainly on the objective lens; the ocular only enlarges and does not improve resolution. Magnification is of value only when accompanied by resolution.

Virtual microscopy converts a stained slide into high-resolution digital images captured in a grid at multiple magnifications, stored so a browser can navigate the original slide — now replacing glass slide collections in teaching laboratories.

Method Principle Use
Fluorescence Fluorophores emit longer-wavelength light when irradiated; bright on a dark background DAPI and Hoechst bind DNA (blue); acridine orange binds DNA and RNA and emits different colors, localizing them separately; fluorescein-coupled probes and labeled antibodies
Phase-contrast Light changes speed through structures of different refractive index, producing contrast in transparent objects Living, unstained cultured cells; DIC (Nomarski optics) gives a more three-dimensional image
Confocal A laser point source and a pinhole aperture optically conjugated to the focal plane block out-of-focus light; the beam scans the specimen Thin optical sections, localization with great precision, and 3-D reconstruction
Polarizing A polarizing filter passes light vibrating in one direction; oriented macromolecules rotate it and appear bright against a dark background — birefringence Collagen, cellulose, microtubules, actin filaments; only material with repetitive periodic structure is seen

Digital cameras extend all of these methods: images can be quantified, enhanced, and analyzed by software for features not visible through the eyepieces.

Electron Microscopy

The wavelength of an electron beam is far shorter than that of light, so the electron microscope can resolve structures far below the light-microscope limit — in principle, a thousandfold improvement.

Transmission EM (TEM) resolves to about 3 nm in routine biological sections — the working figure, limited by section thickness and staining rather than by the instrument alone. A modern TEM can reach a point-to-point resolution of about 0.2 nm on suitable specimens, which is why both numbers appear in teaching; use 3 nm for sectioned tissue and 0.2 nm only when the question asks for the instrument’s limit. Either way, TEM views isolated particles magnified as much as 400,000×. Routine tissue sections of 40–90 nm embedded in resin are studied at up to about 120,000×. Electrons pass through the section to produce an image in black, white, and gray: areas that transmitted electrons appear electron lucent (bright), while denser areas or those binding heavy metal ions appear electron dense (dark). The column is configured like an upside-down light microscope, with electromagnetic lenses and a tungsten filament cathode accelerating electrons at 60–120 kV.

Scanning EM (SEM) images the surface in three dimensions.

Autoradiography

A radiolabeled precursor is incorporated into newly synthesized macromolecules and detected by a photographic emulsion applied over the section — so the silver grains mark the cellular sites where those macromolecules were made. The classic application is ³H-thymidine labeling of cells in S phase.

Cell & Tissue Culture

Living cells maintained outside the body, observed with phase-contrast or DIC microscopy, allow behavior, differentiation, and drug responses to be studied directly — without fixation, which is precisely the advantage.

Enzyme Histochemistry

A substrate is converted by a specific enzyme into an insoluble, colored product deposited at the site of the enzyme, localizing enzyme activity within cells. Acid phosphatase for lysosomes is the standard example.

Visualizing Specific Molecules

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) depends on the specific reaction between an antigen and an antibody labeled with a visible marker — a fluorescent compound or peroxidase for light microscopy, gold particles for TEM.

Method How it works Why it is used
Direct IHC A labeled primary antibody binds the antigen directly Simple, fewer steps
Indirect IHC An unlabeled primary antibody is detected by labeled secondary antibodies More commonly used — the extra level of binding amplifies the signal and gives greater technical flexibility

In situ hybridization (ISH) uses labeled cDNA probes to detect specific gene sequences or mRNAs microscopically.

Interpretation of Structures in Tissue Sections

Two rules govern the interpretation of every section:

  1. Many processing, preparation, and staining steps introduce artifacts — spaces and precipitates not present in living tissue — and these must be recognized for what they are.
  2. Sections are essentially two-dimensional planes through three-dimensional structures, so what you see is a slice, not the whole object.

MICRO — Before identifying anything, decide whether it could be an artifact. A clear space around every cell usually means shrinkage; a straight edge through the tissue is a knife mark; granular debris is precipitated stain. True structure has irregular, biological boundaries.

TIP — Three numbers carry the chapter: 0.2 µm (light microscope resolution), 3–10 µm (paraffin section thickness), about 3 nm (TEM resolution of routine sections). If a question asks why the electron microscope reveals organelles that the light microscope cannot, the answer is the shorter wavelength of the electron beam, not the magnification.

PEARL — A useful chain to remember from Chapter 1: dehydration precedes clearing, clearing makes the tissue translucent, and freezing avoids both — which is why frozen sections are used for rapid diagnosis, for enzymes, and for lipids.

Laboratory Reasoning: Choosing a Method and Recognizing Artifacts

A useful histology result begins with a question, not a stain. Decide what structure or molecule must be demonstrated, what resolution is required, and what the specimen-processing method might destroy.

Question Useful starting approach Important limitation
What is the overall tissue architecture? Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded section with H&E Fine molecular identity cannot be inferred from color alone
Is a carbohydrate-rich structure present? PAS with appropriate controls; diastase digestion when testing for glycogen PAS positivity is not specific for glycogen
Where is a particular protein expressed? Validated immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence Antibody specificity, fixation and controls determine interpretability
Are neutral lipid droplets present? Suitable frozen section with a lipid stain Routine paraffin processing extracts many lipids
What is the ultrastructure of a membrane or organelle? Electron microscopy with appropriate preparation Small field of view and preparation artifacts complicate interpretation
Is a target nucleic-acid sequence present? In situ hybridization with appropriate probes and controls Signal quality depends on preservation, accessibility and probe specificity

Artifact checklist. Tissue shrinkage can produce artificial gaps between cells. Poor fixation can cause loss of cellular detail and autolysis. Compression or chatter during microtomy can distort tissue architecture. Folding may mimic an additional epithelial layer. A poorly oriented cut may make a round tubule look elongated or hide a gland’s opening.

Worked example. A pale circular space in a paraffin section is not automatically a blood vessel. Ask whether it has an endothelial lining, contains erythrocytes, and is connected to other vascular structures. An adipocyte may also appear empty because lipid was extracted during processing, but it usually has a thin rim of cytoplasm and a displaced nucleus.

Quality-control habit. Record the stain, magnification, specimen orientation and relevant positive and negative controls before drawing a molecular conclusion. Distinguish what is directly visible from what is inferred.

Chapter 1 — summary

  • H&E = the routine survey stain; hematoxylin is basic (nuclei, rough ER), eosin is acidic (collagen, cytoplasm).
  • Resolution: light microscope about 0.2 µm; TEM about 3 nm in routine sections.
  • Fixation cross-links proteins; formalin is the standard fixative, glutaraldehyde for EM.
  • Paraffin sections are 3–10 µm; frozen sections are used for urgent diagnosis and for lipid.
  • PAS stains carbohydrates magenta; it demonstrates glycogen, mucin, and basement membranes.
  • Immunohistochemistry localizes a specific antigen with an antibody; in situ hybridization localizes a specific nucleic acid sequence.
  • Artifacts — shrinkage, knife marks, precipitated stain — must be recognized before any structure is identified.
  • A 2D section samples one plane, so a tube can look like a ring and a sphere like a circle.

Active recall

Answer each from memory before checking.

  1. What does formalin do to a tissue? — Cross-links proteins, preserving structure while making the tissue firm enough to section.
  2. Which dye stains nuclei, and why? — Hematoxylin, a basic dye that binds acidic nucleic acids (DNA and RNA).
  3. What does PAS demonstrate? — Carbohydrates, glycogen, and glycoprotein-rich structures such as basement membranes.
  4. Light microscope versus electron microscope resolution? — About 0.2 µm versus about 3 nm in routine sections, roughly two orders of magnitude.
  5. Why is a 5-µm section dangerous to interpret in one glance? — It samples a single plane; a tube can look like a ring, a sphere like a circle, and a fold like a septum.

Exam-style question

A research group needs to show that a newly described protein is present in the basal layer of the epidermis. Which technique is the most direct choice?

A. Hematoxylin and eosin
B. Periodic acid–Schiff
C. Immunohistochemistry with a specific antibody
D. Sudan black

Answer: C — Immunohistochemistry localizes a specific protein by antibody binding. H&E shows general morphology, PAS shows carbohydrates, and Sudan black shows lipids.

Practical identification

Work with a real or virtual H&E section. At low power, locate the tissue edge, any free surface and any capsule; at high power, find nuclei, cytoplasm and extracellular matrix. Then look deliberately for artifacts: clear spaces around cells (shrinkage), straight knife marks, folded or torn areas, and precipitated stain. If phase-contrast, fluorescence or confocal images are available, compare what each reveals and explain why a structure may be visible in one preparation and invisible in another.

2 · The Cytoplasm

In this chapter: Cell Differentiation · The Plasma Membrane · Cytoplasmic Organelles · The Cytoskeleton · Inclusions

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Describe each membrane-bound organelle and match it to the cell type that needs it most.
  2. Explain how proteins are sorted from ribosome to final destination.
  3. Distinguish the three cytoskeletal systems by subunit, diameter, and motor protein.
  4. Predict the ultrastructure of a cell from its function (steroid synthesis, protein export, phagocytosis, movement).

The core idea

A cell is a compartmentalization machine. Each organelle is a membrane-bound solution to a specific logistics problem: making protein, making lipid, generating ATP, degrading waste, or moving cargo. Learn the job, and the morphology follows automatically.

Cell Differentiation

The chapter opens with differentiation, because it explains why one genome produces hundreds of cell shapes. Cells become specialized by differential gene expression — the same DNA, different genes switched on. Differentiation is accompanied by changes in the amount and type of organelles, which is exactly what you recognize down a microscope: an erythroblast loses its nucleus, a plasma cell fills with rough ER, a muscle cell fills with contractile filaments.

Stem cells retain the capacity to self-renew and produce differentiated progeny. They can undergo asymmetric division (one daughter retains stem-cell identity and one differentiates) or symmetric division (expanding or reducing the stem-cell pool according to context). The balance helps regulate tissue renewal in the gut, epidermis, and bone marrow (Chapter 3).

The Plasma Membrane

A 7.5–10 nm phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins — the fluid mosaic model. Amphipathic phospholipids form the bilayer spontaneously; cholesterol modulates fluidity; glycolipids and glycoproteins project outward as the glycocalyx, which mediates cell recognition, adhesion, and immune identity.

Protein class Position Function
Integral (transmembrane) Span the bilayer Channels, pumps, receptors, adhesion
Peripheral Attached to one face Signaling, cytoskeletal anchoring, enzymes

Lipid rafts are cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched microdomains that concentrate signaling molecules. Caveolae are small flask-shaped invaginations involved in uptake and signaling.

Transport across the membrane

  • Simple diffusion — small, non-polar molecules down a gradient.
  • Osmosis — water movement through the bilayer and aquaporins.
  • Facilitated diffusion — carrier or channel, down the gradient, no energy.
  • Active transport — against the gradient, ATP-dependent. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase exchanges 3 Na⁺ out for 2 K⁺ in and consumes a large fraction of the cell’s resting energy budget.
  • Endocytosis — phagocytosis (large particles, actin-driven), pinocytosis (fluid), receptor-mediated endocytosis (clathrin-coated pits; the classic example is LDL uptake, and its failure causes familial hypercholesterolemia).
  • Exocytosis — vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane; requires SNARE proteins and calcium.
  • Transcytosis — endocytosis on one face plus exocytosis on the other; used by endothelial cells and antibodies crossing epithelia.

Cytoplasmic Organelles

Mitochondria. Double membrane; the inner membrane folds into cristae carrying the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. The matrix contains circular mitochondrial DNA, mitochondrial ribosomes, and calcium granules. Mitochondria are maternally inherited and semi-autonomous. Cells with huge energy demand (cardiac muscle, brown fat, proximal tubule, sperm midpiece) are packed with them. Cells that synthesize steroid hormone have mitochondria with tubular rather than lamellar cristae, a reliable ultrastructural marker.

Ribosomes. 15–20 nm particles of rRNA plus protein, made of a large and small subunit. Free ribosomes make cytosolic protein for internal use; ribosomes bound to the rough ER make protein for secretion, membranes, or lysosomes. Polyribosomes (polysomes) are strings of ribosomes on one mRNA, and a polyribosome is the functional unit of translation.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER). An anastomosing network of intercommunicating channels and cisternae, continuous with the outer nuclear membrane. Rough ER (RER) — flattened cisternae studded with ribosomes. Sites of synthesis of secretory proteins, integral membrane proteins, and lysosomal enzymes. It is the structure responsible for basophilia — and in neurons, stacked RER plus polysomes form the Nissl bodies.

Smooth ER (SER) — tubular and ribosome-free. Three jobs: lipid and steroid synthesis, detoxification (hepatic cytochrome P450), and calcium storage (sarcoplasmic reticulum of muscle). Hepatocytes, adrenal cortical cells, Leydig cells, and the corpus luteum are rich in SER — which is why steroid-secreting cells look pale and foamy rather than blue.

Golgi apparatus. Vesicles leave the ER in coats marked by COPII and fuse with the cis (forming) face; retrograde transport back to the ER is mediated by COPI-coated vesicles. It is a stack of 4–8 flattened cisternae with a cis (forming) face receiving vesicles from the ER and a trans (maturing) face releasing vesicles. Functions: modification (glycosylation, sulfation, phosphorylation), sorting, and packaging. The trans-Golgi network is the dispatch center routing proteins to secretory granules, lysosomes, or the membrane. Cells specialized for secretion (plasma cells, pancreatic acinar cells, goblet cells) have an obvious, often supra-nuclear Golgi.

Lysosomes. 0.05–0.5 µm vesicles of acid hydrolases at pH ≈ 5 maintained by a proton pump. Primary lysosomes are newly formed; secondary lysosomes are the product of fusion with a phagosome or autophagosome; residual bodies remain after digestion and accumulate as lipofuscin, the “wear-and-tear” pigment of long-lived cells such as cardiac myocytes and neurons.

Peroxisomes. Small, membrane-bound oxidative organelles containing catalase and oxidases. They perform β-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids, detoxify ethanol, and destroy H₂O₂.

Proteasomes. Cytosolic barrel-shaped complexes that degrade ubiquitin-tagged proteins.

The Cytoskeleton

Element Diameter Protein Main roles
Microfilament 6–8 nm Actin Microvilli core, cell cortex, stress fibers, cleavage furrow, muscle contraction
Intermediate filament 10 nm Keratin, vimentin, desmin, GFAP, neurofilament, lamin Mechanical strength; tissue-specific identity
Microtubule 25 nm Tubulin (α/β dimer) Spindle, cilia and flagella, intracellular transport

Intermediate filaments are the histologist’s best friend, because each cell type keeps its own: keratin in epithelium, vimentin in mesenchyme, desmin in muscle, GFAP in astrocytes, neurofilament in neurons, lamins in the nucleus. This is why immunohistochemistry can identify the origin of a tumor that looks undifferentiated.

Microtubules are polar. Kinesin walks cargo toward the plus end (anterograde, away from the cell body); dynein walks toward the minus end (retrograde, back to the cell body). Rabies and herpes viruses exploit dynein to travel to the neuronal soma.

Cilia and flagella have a 9 + 2 arrangement with dynein arms; the basal body has 9 + 0. Defective dynein causes primary ciliary dyskinesia, of which Kartagener syndrome (situs inversus, bronchiectasis, sinusitis, male infertility) is the classic form.

Inclusions

Non-membranous accumulations of metabolites or pigments: glycogen (PAS-positive, liver and muscle), lipid droplets (Sudan-positive), melanin, lipofuscin, hemosiderin, and crystals.

CLINICAL — Lysosomal storage diseases are the direct consequence of a missing acid hydrolase. In Tay–Sachs disease hexosaminidase A is absent, so GM2 ganglioside accumulates in neurons, producing developmental regression, a cherry-red macular spot, and death in early childhood. In I-cell disease the enzyme that tags hydrolases with mannose-6-phosphate is defective, so the enzymes are secreted instead of delivered to lysosomes.

MICRO — Learn to read a cell’s job from its EM. Abundant RER and a large Golgi = protein secretion. Abundant SER and lipid droplets, mitochondria with tubular cristae = steroid secretion. Many mitochondria and few other organelles = energy transduction. Numerous lysosomes and residual bodies = phagocytosis or long life. Bundles of filaments = mechanical work.

MNEMONIC — “Keratins Keep Epithelium; Vimentin is Versatile mesenchyme; Desmin Does muscle; GFAP Glues astrocytes; Neurofilaments are Neuronal.”

TIP — Keep two distinctions straight. The Golgi modifies and sorts proteins; the RER makes them. The mitochondrion generates ATP; the lysosome degrades material.

Active recall

  1. Rough versus smooth ER — what distinguishes them functionally? — Rough ER carries ribosomes and makes membrane and secretory proteins; smooth ER makes lipids and steroids and detoxifies.
  2. Why is the Golgi described as polarized? — Cis face receives, medial cisternae modify, trans face packages and sorts.
  3. Which organelle contains catalase, and what does it do? — The peroxisome; catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide, and peroxisomes also perform β-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids.

Exam-style question

A. Abundant rough ER and apical zymogen granules
B. Abundant smooth ER, lipid droplets, and tubular mitochondrial cristae
C. Numerous lysosomes and residual bodies

Practical identification

On an electron micrograph, identify the plasma membrane, rough ER (parallel cisternae bearing ribosomes), smooth ER (tubules without ribosomes), Golgi (stacked flattened cisternae with vesicles), mitochondria (double membrane and cristae), lysosomes (dense bodies) and inclusions (glycogen rosettes, lipid droplets, pigment). On a light micrograph, use basophilia to locate rough ER and eosinophilia to locate mitochondria and secretory granules, and justify each assignment from the chemistry of the dyes.

3 · The Nucleus

In this chapter: Components of the Nucleus · The Cell Cycle · Mitosis · Stem Cells & Tissue Renewal · Meiosis · Apoptosis

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Describe the nuclear envelope, lamina, and pore complex as a selective transport system.
  2. Explain how DNA is packaged into nucleosomes, chromatin, and chromosomes.
  3. Sequence the cell cycle and name the checkpoints that guard it.
  4. Contrast mitosis, meiosis, and apoptosis, and explain what goes wrong when each fails.

The core idea

The nucleus stores the genome, transcribes it, and duplicates it before division. Its morphology is a readout of transcriptional activity: dark, condensed chromatin means silent genes; pale, dispersed chromatin means active genes.

Components of the Nucleus

Nuclear envelope. Two membranes separated by a perinuclear cisterna, continuous with the rough ER. Beneath the inner membrane lies the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of lamins A, B, and C that supports the envelope and organizes chromatin. Mutations in lamin A cause Hutchinson–Gilford progeria.

Centrosomes. Each cell has a pair of centrioles — nine microtubular triplets — surrounded by pericentriolar material: the centrosome, the microtubule-organizing center that builds the mitotic spindle, and the basal body of a cilium.

Nuclear pores. 3,000–4,000 per nucleus. Each is a nuclear pore complex about 100 nm wide, built from roughly 34 nucleoporins, and can pass up to about 1,000 macromolecules per second. Small molecules diffuse; proteins and RNA require importins and exportins and a Ran-GTP gradient.

Chromatin. Each human cell holds about 2 meters of DNA — 3.2 billion base pairs — which must be packaged into a nucleus a few micrometers across. The DNA wraps around a core of eight histones (two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3, H4) with about 150 bp of DNA per nucleosome (about 1.65 turns around the octamer), plus a larger H1 histone on the outside: that unit is the nucleosome, which in EM looks like 10-nm beads on a string. Further folding into 30-nm fibers and beyond involves nonhistone scaffold proteins and is less well understood. Heterochromatin is condensed and transcriptionally inactive; euchromatin is dispersed and active. Constitutive heterochromatin is permanently silent; facultative heterochromatin can be reactivated. The Barr body is a facultatively heterochromatic, inactivated X chromosome — one per somatic cell in a female.

Nucleolus. Not membrane-bound; a factory for ribosomal subunits, with a pars fibrosa (rRNA transcription) and a pars granulosa (ribosome assembly), organized around nucleolar organizer regions on the acrocentric chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22. Cells that make large amounts of protein have prominent nucleoli, and malignant cells often do too.

The Cell Cycle

Phase What happens Duration (typical cycling cell)
G1 Cell grows, checks nutrients and DNA integrity; restriction point ~10 h
S DNA replication; histones synthesized ~8 h
G2 Repair check, prepare for mitosis ~4 h
M Mitosis and cytokinesis ~1 h

The durations above are typical of a rapidly dividing cultured cell; most adult tissues cycle far more slowly or not at all, so do not transplant these numbers to the body. Progression is driven by cyclin–CDK complexes. p53 halts the cycle at G1 to allow repair and triggers apoptosis if repair fails — hence “guardian of the genome” — and it is mutated in about half of human cancers. Rb is the G1/S brake lost in retinoblastoma. G0 is the quiescent state of cells that have left the cycle.

Mitosis

  • Prophase — chromosomes condense, centrioles move to opposite poles, the nuclear envelope begins to break down.
  • Metaphase — chromosomes align at the equatorial plate; the spindle attaches to kinetochores.
  • Anaphase — sister chromatids separate and are pulled poleward.
  • Telophase — nuclear envelopes re-form, chromosomes decondense.
  • Cytokinesis — an actin contractile ring pinches the cell in two.

Stem Cells & Tissue Renewal

  • Totipotent — can form the whole organism (zygote, early blastomeres).
  • Pluripotent — can form all three germ layers (inner cell mass; embryonic stem cells; induced pluripotent stem cells reprogrammed with the Yamanaka factors Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc).
  • Multipotent — form a restricted set of cell types (hematopoietic stem cell).
  • Unipotent — one cell type.

Tissues are labile (continuous turnover — epidermis, gut, marrow), stable (low turnover, can be triggered — liver, kidney, fibroblasts), or permanent (most neurons, cardiac myocytes, lens fibers), with little or no regenerative capacity under normal conditions.

Meiosis

Two divisions produce four haploid cells from one diploid cell, with crossing over between homologous chromosomes during pachytene of prophase I, held together by the synaptonemal complex. Meiosis generates genetic diversity and halves the chromosome number; non-disjunction produces aneuploid gametes, the mechanism behind trisomy 21.

Apoptosis

Programmed cell death: caspases are activated, cytochrome c escapes mitochondria, the Bcl-2 family decides survival versus death, chromatin condenses, the cell shrinks and buds into apoptotic bodies that are phagocytosed without inflammation.

Feature Apoptosis Necrosis

The contrast is a teaching simplification. Necroptosis and pyroptosis are regulated lytic deaths, and apoptotic cells that are not cleared promptly can provoke inflammation, so the presence or absence of inflammation is a strong rule of thumb rather than an absolute distinction.

CLINICAL — Lamin A mutation causes progeria with premature aging. Changes in nuclear shape and chromatin texture are also one of the oldest cancer-screening tools: the irregular, hyperchromatic nucleus of a dysplastic cervical cell is what a Pap smear is looking for.

MICRO — An active cell has a pale nucleus with a prominent, reticulated nucleolus and lots of euchromatin. A quiescent cell — a small lymphocyte, a spermatozoon, a superficial keratinocyte — has dense heterochromatin and little visible nucleolus. Nuclear texture is a functional readout.

TIP — One Barr body in a normal female somatic cell, zero in a male, one in Klinefelter syndrome (47, XXY), none in Turner syndrome (45, X). Count Barr bodies and you have counted X chromosomes minus one.

  1. What is a nucleosome? — About 150 bp of DNA wrapped around an octamer of histones (two each of H2A, H2B, H3, H4), with H1 on the outside.
  2. Heterochromatin versus euchromatin? — Heterochromatin is condensed and transcriptionally silent; euchromatin is dispersed and active.

Exam-style question

A cell is blocked in metaphase with colchicine. Its chromosomes number 46. Which statement is correct?

A. It is a haploid gamete
B. It is a diploid somatic or pre-meiotic germ cell
C. It has completed meiosis II

Practical identification

Find a cell with a large, pale nucleus and a prominent nucleolus, such as a neuron or a plasma cell, and contrast it with a small lymphocyte, whose dense nucleus almost fills the cell. Identify mitotic figures by their condensed chromosomes and absent nuclear envelope, and distinguish them from apoptotic bodies, which are fragmented, membrane-bound and lack a spindle. In a buccal smear from a female, look for the Barr body at the nuclear margin.

4 · Epithelial Tissue

Learning objectives

  1. Classify any epithelium by the number of layers and the shape of its surface cells.
  2. Match each junctional complex to its molecular components and its function.
  3. Explain how microvilli, stereocilia, and cilia differ in structure and purpose.
  4. Predict where a given epithelium will be found from the mechanical or transport job it must do.

The core idea

Epithelial tissues are composed of closely aggregated polyhedral cells adhering strongly to one another and to a thin layer of ECM, forming cellular sheets that line the cavities of organs and cover the body surface. Epithelia (Gr. epi, upon + thele, nipple) line all external and internal surfaces of the body, and many exchanges between an organ and its environment occur across epithelial barriers. Everything else — the number of layers, the shape of the cells, the specializations on the apex — is a solution to one of four problems: protection, absorption, secretion, or transport.

The four basic tissues (the framework for the whole subject):

Tissue Cells Extracellular matrix Main functions
Epithelial Aggregated polyhedral cells Small amount Lining of surface or body cavities; glandular secretion
Connective Several types of fixed and wandering cells Abundant amount Support and protection of tissues/organs
Muscle Elongated contractile cells Moderate amount Strong contraction; body movements
Nervous Elongated cells with extremely fine processes Very small amount Transmission of nerve impulses

Most organs are divided into the parenchyma — the cells responsible for the organ’s specialized functions — and the stroma, which plays a supporting role. In most organs, stroma includes supporting connective tissue and vessels. The brain and spinal cord have specialized neural supporting tissue (notably glia) and also contain connective tissue associated with blood vessels and coverings.

Characteristic Features of Epithelial Cells

The principal functions of epithelial tissues are covering, lining, and protecting surfaces (eg, epidermis), absorption (eg, the intestinal lining), and secretion (eg, parenchymal cells of glands). Specific cells of certain epithelia may be contractile (myoepithelial cells) or specialized sensory cells, such as those of taste buds or the olfactory epithelium.

Cell shape and nuclear shape correspond: columnar cells have elongated nuclei, squamous cells flattened nuclei, and cuboidal or pyramidal cells spherical nuclei. This is the fastest identification rule in the whole subject.

The defining features:

  1. Cells are closely apposed with minimal intercellular space.
  2. There is a free (apical) surface facing air, a lumen, or a cavity.
  3. Cells rest on a basement membrane.
  4. The tissue is avascular — nourishment arrives by diffusion from vessels in the underlying connective tissue.
  5. Cells are polarized: apical, lateral, and basal domains differ in structure and function.

The basement membrane is often demonstrated by PAS or silver staining. Electron microscopy identifies a basal lamina, usually described by its lamina densa and sometimes an electron-lucent zone; the classical lamina lucida can be influenced by preparation. An associated reticular lamina is not uniformly present beneath every epithelium:

A Practical Approach to Epithelial Identification

Epithelial classification becomes reliable when three separate observations are recorded: layering, shape of the luminal cells, and apical or basal specialization. Begin at the free surface, then trace individual cells toward the basement membrane. A tangential cut may make a simple epithelium appear stratified, while nuclei at different heights in pseudostratified epithelium do not imply that every cell fails to reach the basal lamina.

Observation Interpretation Common pitfall
One layer of very flat cells Simple squamous epithelium, as in endothelium or alveolar lining Thin cytoplasm may be almost invisible on H&E
One layer of cells about as tall as wide Simple cuboidal epithelium, common in small ducts and tubules An oblique section changes apparent cell height
One layer of tall cells Simple columnar epithelium, often absorptive or secretory Goblet cells and cilia are not present at every site
Nuclei at different heights, cells sharing the basal lamina Pseudostratified epithelium It is functionally one epithelial layer, not truly multilayered

Superficial dome-shaped cells, variable thickness with distension Urothelium Flattened umbrella cells in a distended specimen may obscure the classic dome shape

Decision sequence. (1) Find the luminal boundary. (2) Determine whether the tissue is genuinely multilayered. (3) Describe the surface cells. (4) Look for cilia, microvilli, keratin, goblet cells or umbrella cells. (5) Use surrounding tissue and organ architecture to confirm the identification.

Clinical reasoning. Epithelial metaplasia changes a tissue’s differentiation program, but the new lining is not automatically malignant. Dysplasia describes disordered growth and cytologic atypia; invasive carcinoma requires evidence of invasion through the relevant tissue boundary. Routine histology must be interpreted in clinical context.

Check your understanding. A respiratory lining has nuclei at several levels, cilia and mucus-producing cells, yet its cells contact the basal lamina. Answer: pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium. The critical evidence is basal attachment, not the apparent number of nuclear rows.

Specializations of the Apical Cell Surface

Microvilli. Finger-like extensions with a core of actin filaments cross-linked by actin-bundling proteins (villin, fimbrin) and anchored in a terminal web. They multiply surface area for absorption. In the proximal tubule and small intestine they form a brush border visible as a fuzzy eosinophilic rim. In the epididymis and inner ear, unusually long, stiff microvilli are called stereocilia — a misnomer, because they contain no microtubules.

Cilia. Motile, 9 + 2 microtubule axoneme with dynein arms, anchored to a basal body (9 + 0). They move mucus, cerebrospinal fluid, and the ovum. A primary cilium is a single non-motile 9 + 0 cilium acting as a sensory antenna — its failure underlies polycystic kidney disease.

Basal infoldings. Deep folds of the basal membrane packed with mitochondria — the “striated” appearance in the ducts of salivary glands and the distal tubule of the kidney. They are the morphological signature of active ion transport.

Two naming traps to avoid. Endothelium and mesothelium are both simple squamous epithelium — one lines blood vessels, the other body cavities; they are not separate tissue types. And pseudostratified epithelium is truly a single layer in which every cell touches the basement membrane, even though the nuclei sit at different levels.

Transport Across Epithelia

An epithelium that moves material from one side to the other is built like a machine: entry at the apical face, exit at the basal face, with the tight junction preventing back-leak and mitochondrial-rich basal infoldings powering the pumps. In the intestinal enterocyte, glucose enters apically via Na⁺-glucose cotransport (SGLT1) and exits basolaterally via GLUT2; the Na⁺ gradient is maintained by basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. This is transcellular transport. Paracellular transport passes between cells and is limited by tight junctions.

Renewal of Epithelial Cells

Epithelia wear out. They are replaced from stem cells whose location varies with the tissue: the basal layer of stratified squamous epithelium, the crypt base of the intestine, the isthmus of the gastric gland, and the basal layer of the epidermis. Renewal rate is a diagnostic index: the intestinal epithelium turns over in days, the epidermis in weeks. Because the stem cells rest on the basement membrane, an epithelium can regenerate completely after superficial injury — but not after the basement membrane itself is destroyed.

Glands

  • Exocrine — connected to a surface by a duct. Endocrine — ductless, secreting into the blood.
  • Unicellular — the goblet cell (mucin; empty cup-shaped apex after secretion).
  • Simple — one unbranched duct (simple tubular = intestinal crypt; simple coiled tubular = eccrine sweat gland; simple branched acinar = sebaceous gland).
  • Compound — branched duct (salivary glands, pancreas, mammary gland).
  • Mechanism: merocrine (exocytosis — pancreas, sweat glands), apocrine (membrane and cytoplasm bud off — the lipid component of mammary secretion), holocrine (whole cell disintegrates — sebaceous gland).

Myoepithelial cells sit between secretory cells and the basement membrane and contract to expel product — visible in mammary and salivary glands.

CLINICAL — Epithelial junctions are the target of autoimmune blistering. In pemphigus vulgaris, antibodies against desmoglein break desmosomes, so the epidermis detaches within the epithelium and thin-walled blisters form. In bullous pemphigoid, antibodies against hemidesmosomal antigens split the epidermis from the basement membrane and the blister is thick-walled and tense. The level of the split is dictated by which junction fails.

MICRO — Count layers, not nuclei. If every cell rests on the basement membrane and only the nuclei sit at different heights, the epithelium is pseudostratified — tall, ciliated, respiratory. If cells are stacked on one another, it is stratified. Then the top layer: flat and anucleate = keratinized; flat with nuclei = non-keratinized; dome-shaped and large = transitional; tall with cilia = respiratory.

MNEMONIC — “Simple for exchange, stratified for stress, transitional for stretch.” For cilia, remember 9 + 2 moves, 9 + 0 anchors.

TIP — Where is ciliated simple columnar epithelium found? The uterine tube — cilia move the ovum — and parts of the respiratory tract, where the epithelium is pseudostratified. Students often wrongly assign all ciliated epithelium to the lung.

Chapter 4 — summary

  • Epithelium sits on a basal lamina, is avascular, and is normally adjacent to connective tissue.
  • Simple = one layer (exchange, absorption, secretion); stratified = protection; pseudostratified = all cells touch the basement membrane.
  • Squamous = flat; cuboidal = height ≈ width; columnar = height > width.
  • Keratinized stratified squamous = dry, abrasive surfaces; nonkeratinized = wet surfaces (mouth, esophagus, vagina).
  • Urothelium (transitional) = dome-shaped umbrella cells; stretches.
  • Tight junction (zonula occludens) = seal and fence; it creates apical–basal polarity.
  • Zonula adherens anchors actin; desmosomes anchor intermediate filaments; gap junctions couple cells.
  • Hemidesmosomes attach the basal cell to the basal lamina.
  • Basal lamina = lamina lucida + lamina densa (type IV collagen, laminin); the reticular lamina (type III) belongs to connective tissue.
  • Microvilli = actin core, increase surface; stereocilia = long, stiff microvilli; cilia = 9 + 2 microtubules, motile.
  • Basal bodies anchor cilia and have a 9 + 0 arrangement.
  • Apical specializations serve absorption or movement; basal specializations anchor and receive signals.
  • Epithelia renew from stem cells: labile (gut, skin) versus stable (liver, kidney).

Active recall

  1. How do you classify an epithelium in one pass? — First count the layers (simple, stratified, pseudostratified), then name the surface cell shape (squamous, cuboidal, columnar).
  2. What does a tight junction do besides sealing? — It fences membrane proteins, creating and maintaining apical–basal polarity.
  3. Which junction attaches a cell to the basal lamina? — The hemidesmosome.
  4. Microvillus, stereocilium, cilium — core structure? — Microvillus: actin core. Stereocilium: long, stiff actin bundle. Cilium: 9 + 2 microtubules anchored by a 9 + 0 basal body.
  5. Why is urothelium suited to the bladder? — Umbrella cells with uroplakin plaques and tight junctions form an osmotic barrier, and the epithelium thins as the bladder fills.

Exam-style question

An epithelium lines a dry, abrasion-prone surface. Which description fits it?

A. Simple columnar with goblet cells
B. Stratified squamous keratinized
C. Simple squamous with fenestrations
D. Pseudostratified ciliated columnar

Practical identification

Classify any epithelium with two questions: how many layers, and what shape is the surface cell? Then find the basal lamina, the underlying connective tissue and the apical specialization — microvilli, cilia, stereocilia or keratin. Distinguish pseudostratified from stratified by checking whether every cell reaches the basement membrane. In a gland, decide whether the secretory unit is serous, mucous or mixed, and whether the duct is intercalated, striated or excretory.

The core idea

Connective tissue is the tissue whose defining feature is abundance of extracellular matrix. The cells are widely separated because the matrix — not the cells — does the mechanical work. Name the matrix and you have named the tissue’s job: collagen for tension, elastin for recoil, ground substance for compression and diffusion.

Collagen synthesis in five steps — a reliable structured-question answer:

  1. Translation on the RER as preprocollagen.
  2. Hydroxylation of proline and lysine — requires vitamin C.
  3. Glycosylation in the ER and Golgi.
  4. Procollagen is secreted and the propeptides cleaved → tropocollagen.
  5. Fibril assembly with cross-links formed by lysyl oxidase, which requires copper.

This pathway explains two diseases. Without vitamin C, hydroxylation fails and the result is scurvy — bleeding gums, poor wound healing, perifollicular hemorrhage. Without copper, cross-linking fails, as in Menkes disease. In Ehlers–Danlos syndrome the defect is in collagen processing or type; in Marfan syndrome the defect is in fibrillin, so the aorta dilates and the lens dislocates.

Ground Substance

An amorphous, hydrated gel of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), proteoglycans, and glycoproteins, which resists compression, lubricates, and controls diffusion.

  • GAGs (also called mucopolysaccharides) are long polymers of repeating disaccharide units, usually with many negative charges that attract water: hyaluronic acid (the only unsulfated one, not attached to protein, and the backbone of the gel) and the sulfated GAGs — chondroitin sulfate, keratan sulfate, dermatan sulfate, and heparan sulfate.
  • Proteoglycans are a core protein with GAG side chains — the classic is aggrecan, a very large molecule with a 250-kDa core protein heavily bound with chondroitin and keratan sulfate and joined to hyaluronan by a link protein. Its water-binding is why cartilage resists compression.
  • Perlecan is the key proteoglycan of all basal laminae — the one name that links ground substance to the basement membrane.
  • Glycoproteins — fibronectin (binds cells to matrix via integrins, important in wound healing) and laminin (basement membrane).

Types of Connective Tissue

  • Loose (areolar) — abundant ground substance, scattered fibers, many cells. Fills spaces, surrounds vessels, underlies epithelia.
  • Dense irregular — thick collagen bundles in many directions; dermis, organ capsules. Resists stress from all directions.
  • Dense regular — parallel collagen bundles; tendon and ligament. Resists stress in one direction.
  • Elastic — many elastic fibers; elastic ligament, vocal cord, large arteries.
  • Reticular — type III collagen network; lymphoid organs and marrow.
  • Mucous (Wharton’s jelly) — gelatinous, in the umbilical cord.
  • Embryonic — mesenchyme.

Besides fibroblasts, the resident population includes adipocytes, macrophages, mast cells, and the leukocytes that migrate in (especially lymphocytes and plasma cells in chronic inflammation) — which is why older descriptions divided them into fixed and wandering cells.

Repair. Inflammation → granulation tissue (proliferating capillaries, fibroblasts, macrophages) → collagen deposition → scar. Primary intention occurs in clean apposed wounds; secondary intention in larger, open wounds with more granulation and contraction.

CLINICAL — Scurvy is the purest histology-to-clinic link in the chapter: inadequate hydroxylation produces defective collagen, which produces fragile capillaries (bleeding gums, petechiae) and failed wound healing. Scleroderma is the opposite failure — excess collagen in skin and internal organs. Keloid is an overgrowth of dense collagen beyond the original wound margin, common in darker skin.

MICRO — Collagen is pink, wavy, and in bundles; elastin is sparse, often darker, and best shown by orcein. A plasma cell has an eccentric clock-face nucleus with a pale perinuclear halo; a mast cell’s granules stain metachromatically; the eosinophil’s bilobed nucleus is unmistakable.

MNEMONIC — “1 bone, 2 cartilage, 3 reticulin, 4 basement membrane.”

TIP — The distinction that matters most here is dense regular versus dense irregular, and the answer is one word: direction. Regular = parallel = tendon = one axis of force. Irregular = random = dermis = many axes.

  • Type I collagen = bone, tendon, dermis, scar. Type II = hyaline and elastic cartilage. Type III = reticulin. Type IV = basal lamina.
  • Collagen synthesis needs vitamin C for proline and lysine hydroxylation; deficiency = scurvy.
  • Elastic fibers = elastin core + fibrillin microfibrils; orcein stains them.
  • Ground substance = GAGs and proteoglycans; hyaluronan is the only unsulfated GAG.
  • Fibroblast makes collagen; myofibroblast contracts (wound closure).
  • Plasma cell = eccentric clock-face nucleus, perinuclear Golgi halo, antibody factory.
  • Mast cell = heparin, histamine, proteases; metachromatic granules; IgE receptor.

  • Loose (areolar) tissue = cells and vessels; dense irregular = dermis; dense regular = tendon.
  • Reticular tissue = type III collagen framework of lymphoid organs and marrow.

6 · Adipose Tissue

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish white and brown adipocytes by light and electron microscopy.
  2. Explain how white adipose tissue works as an endocrine organ.
  3. Explain the mechanism of non-shivering thermogenesis and where brown fat is found.
  4. Relate adipose storage limits to obesity, lipotoxicity, and lipodystrophy.

The core idea

Adipose tissue is an endocrine and metabolic organ disguised as a filling tissue. White fat stores energy; brown fat burns it for heat. Both are built from the same cell with a completely different internal architecture.

White Adipose Tissue

Unilocular. Each white adipocyte is a very large cell, 50–150 µm in diameter, containing one enormous lipid droplet that displaces the cytoplasm to a thin rim and flattens the nucleus against the plasmalemma — the signet-ring appearance. Adipose tissue is normally 15–20% of body weight in men and somewhat more in women. The lipid dissolves during routine processing, so on an H&E slide the cell appears as an empty circle with a thin border.

Functions: energy storage, insulation, cushioning of organs (retro-orbital, perirenal, mesenteric), and endocrine signaling.

Where the stored fat comes from — three sources: dietary fat carried as chylomicrons; triglycerides made in the liver and carried as VLDLs; and fatty acids and glycerol synthesized by the adipocytes themselves. Mobilization is by hormone-sensitive lipase, activated by norepinephrine from the adrenal gland and sympathetic nerves, and by growth hormone; insulin inhibits that lipase and stimulates lipid synthesis. The cells are supported by reticular fibers, with connective tissue septa dividing the tissue into lobules.

Adipose-derived factors — the ones worth knowing (the last is an enzyme, not an adipokine):

  • Leptin — from adipocytes, acts on the hypothalamus to reduce appetite.
  • Adiponectin — increases insulin sensitivity; low in obesity.
  • Resistin, TNF-α, IL-6 — pro-inflammatory, part of why obesity is a chronic inflammatory state.
  • Aromatase — an enzyme, not a cytokine: it converts androgens to estrogens, making adipose tissue a peripheral estrogen source (the reason obesity raises estrogen levels).

Brown Adipose Tissue

Multilocular. Many small lipid droplets, a central round nucleus, and — the diagnostic feature — numerous mitochondria with abundant cristae containing uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1, thermogenin), which short-circuits the proton gradient so energy is released as heat instead of ATP.

Found in the fetus and newborn (interscapular, axillary, perirenal, along the great vessels), much reduced in adults. Sympathetic innervation drives it. “Beige” adipocytes appear within white fat after cold exposure.

CLINICAL — In lipodystrophy fat is absent or redistributed, and the metabolic consequences are paradoxical: severe insulin resistance with hypertriglyceridemia, because the remaining fat cannot store lipid safely. TIP — Do not confuse adipose tissue with steatosis. Adipocytes are a normal tissue with a capsule and vessels; steatosis is lipid accumulating inside hepatocytes or myocytes as vacuoles.

Chapter 6 — summary

  • White adipocyte: one large lipid droplet, nucleus flattened at the rim, few mitochondria.
  • Brown adipocyte: many small droplets, round central nucleus, many cristae-rich mitochondria.
  • White fat = energy storage plus an endocrine organ: leptin, adiponectin, and other adipokines.
  • Brown fat = non-shivering thermogenesis through UCP1, which uncouples the proton gradient.
  • Brown fat is richest in the newborn (neck, back, mediastinum) and persists in adult neck and supraclavicular depots.
  • Beige/brite adipocytes are an inducible, brown-like population within white fat.
  • Adipose tissue is vascular and innervated; white fat’s sympathetic supply is sparse, brown fat’s is rich.
  • Obesity is expansion by hypertrophy and hyperplasia; the storage limit produces lipotoxicity.
  • Lipodystrophy = absent or redistributed fat with severe insulin resistance.
  • Leptin signals energy stores to the hypothalamus; adiponectin increases insulin sensitivity.

  2. What is UCP1 and where does it act? — Uncoupling protein 1 in the inner mitochondrial membrane; it lets protons leak back without ATP synthesis, releasing heat.
  3. Name two adipokines. — Leptin (signals stored energy) and adiponectin (increases insulin sensitivity).
  4. When is brown fat most abundant? — In the newborn (neck, back, mediastinum); it persists in adult neck and supraclavicular depots.
  5. What is lipotoxicity? — When adipose storage capacity is exceeded, lipid accumulates in liver, muscle, and pancreas, impairing their function.

7 · Cartilage

In this chapter: Hyaline Cartilage · Elastic Cartilage · Fibrocartilage · Cartilage Formation, Growth, & Repair

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish hyaline, elastic, and fibrocartilage by matrix, cells, and perichondrium.
  2. Explain why cartilage heals so poorly and what tissue replaces it.
  3. Describe appositional and interstitial growth and when each is possible.
  4. Identify cartilage types in a micrograph and relate each to its mechanical role.

The core idea

Cartilage is a tough, resilient connective tissue in which the extracellular matrix — collagen, proteoglycans, and water — is the bulk of the tissue; chondrocytes occupy only a small fraction of its volume, sitting in lacunae. Aggrecan binds a large amount of water, giving the matrix its compressive resilience. Cartilage supports soft tissues, notably in the respiratory tract, and provides cushioned, low-friction surfaces in joints. It is avascular and aneural — no blood vessels, lymphatics, or nerves — and is usually surrounded by a vascularized perichondrium of dense connective tissue whose fibroblasts form its outer fibrous layer. Its extracellular matrix provides mechanical resilience; the absence of vessels contributes to its limited capacity for repair.

The shared architecture

  • Cells: chondroblasts (immature, at the surface) and chondrocytes (mature, in lacunae).
  • Matrix: territorial matrix immediately around the lacuna (rich in proteoglycan, basophilic) and interterritorial matrix between cells (more collagen, eosinophilic).
  • Isogenous groups — clusters of 2–8 chondrocytes from one parent cell, indicating recent interstitial growth.
  • Perichondrium — fibrous outer layer plus chondrogenic inner layer; present on most cartilage, absent on fibrocartilage and on articular surfaces.

Hyaline Cartilage

The commonest type and the template for most of the fetal skeleton. Glassy, homogeneous matrix; collagen makes up 40% of hyaline cartilage’s dry weight, all of it embedded in a hydrated gel of proteoglycans and structural glycoproteins, with more proteoglycan and less collagen immediately around the lacunae (the territorial matrix). Perichondrium is present except on articular surfaces and the epiphyses of growing long bones, where the cartilage itself forms the smooth, low-friction bearing surface of joints. Found in the trachea, bronchi, nose, ribs, epiphyseal plate, and articular surfaces.

Elastic Cartilage

Type II collagen plus a network of elastic fibers, giving flexibility with recoil. Found in the epiglottis, pinna, external auditory canal, and Eustachian tube — that is, everywhere something must bend and spring back.

Fibrocartilage

Type I and type II collagen in coarse bundles, with chondrocytes arranged in parallel rows between them. No perichondrium. Found where compressive and tensile forces meet: intervertebral disc, symphysis pubis, menisci, temporomandibular joint. The disc is the perfect example, with an inner nucleus pulposus (notochord remnant, rich in hyaluronic acid) and an outer annulus fibrosus of concentric fibrocartilage lamellae.

Structure–function question. Why does the meniscus contain fibrocartilaginous tissue rather than a uniform glassy matrix? Answer: its collagen organization helps resist tensile forces while its hydrated matrix participates in load distribution and compression resistance. Mechanical behavior depends on the complete tissue architecture, not a single collagen type.

Repair question. Why does a focal articular-cartilage defect often persist? Answer: articular cartilage has no direct blood supply and lacks a perichondrium; mature chondrocytes have limited capacity to replace a substantial defect. Repair responses depend on lesion depth and whether subchondral bone is involved.

Cartilage Formation, Growth, & Repair

  • Appositional growth — new cartilage added at the periphery by chondroblasts of the perichondrium. Increases width.
  • Interstitial growth — chondrocytes divide within lacunae and secrete matrix, separating daughter cells. It is prominent during development and in growth plates; some interstitial matrix turnover continues in mature cartilage, although its capacity for growth is limited.

Because cartilage is avascular, nutrition depends on diffusion from the perichondrium or synovial fluid. This limits repair: some defects may fill with fibrocartilaginous repair tissue, particularly after procedures that access subchondral bone; many untreated full-thickness articular defects heal poorly or incompletely.

CLINICAL — Osteoarthritis is a whole-joint disorder involving cartilage, subchondral bone, synovium and other joint tissues; a central feature is disruption of chondrocyte-mediated matrix maintenance: proteoglycans are depleted, water content rises, collagen fibrils fragment, and the surface fissures. Articular cartilage itself is aneural, so pain arises from innervated structures such as subchondral bone, capsule, ligaments and synovium; pain can occur at different stages and does not correlate perfectly with cartilage loss. Rheumatoid arthritis is different: an autoimmune synovitis destroys cartilage from the surface inward, producing a pannus.

MICRO — Identify cartilage by the lacuna: a cell alone in a clear space within a solid matrix. Then the matrix: glassy purple with cells in clusters = hyaline; wavy dark fibers = elastic; thick parallel pink bundles with cells in rows = fibrocartilage.

TIP — Two commonly confused points: hyaline cartilage contains type II collagen, not type I, and fibrocartilage has no perichondrium, which is why disc injuries persist.

Chapter 7 — summary

  • Cartilage = chondrocytes in lacunae + solid matrix; no blood vessels, no nerves.
  • Hyaline cartilage = type II collagen + aggrecan; glassy; perichondrium present except at articular surfaces.
  • Elastic cartilage = type II collagen + elastic fibers; perichondrium present; epiglottis, auricle, auditory tube.
  • Fibrocartilage = type I + type II collagen, rows of chondrocytes, no perichondrium; discs, menisci, pubic symphysis.
  • Territorial matrix surrounds each lacuna; interterritorial matrix lies between groups.
  • Isogenous groups = daughter cells from recent division, still in one lacuna.
  • Appositional growth = from perichondrium, adds width.
  • Interstitial growth = division inside lacunae, adds length, young cartilage only.
  • Articular cartilage is hyaline without perichondrium and heals poorly.
  • Cartilage repair fills with fibrocartilage, not hyaline.
  • Osteoarthritis = matrix failure; rheumatoid arthritis = synovial inflammation with pannus.
  • Chondroblasts make matrix; chondrocytes maintain it.

  2. Which collagen types are in hyaline and fibrocartilage? — Hyaline: type II. Fibrocartilage: type I and type II.
  3. What is an isogenous group? — Daughter chondrocytes from a recent division, still sharing one lacuna.
  4. Appositional versus interstitial growth? — Appositional adds cartilage at the periphery and increases width; interstitial growth divides chondrocytes inside lacunae, increases length, and occurs only in young cartilage.
  5. Why is articular cartilage a poor repairer? — It is avascular, aneural, and lacks perichondrium, so nutrition depends on synovial fluid diffusion.

  2. Distinguish woven from lamellar bone, and compact from spongy bone.
  3. Sequence endochondral and intramembranous ossification.

The core idea

Bone is the main constituent of the adult skeleton: it gives solid support, protects vital organs of the cranial and thoracic cavities, encloses medullary cavities where blood cells form, and acts as a reservoir of calcium and phosphate, storing or releasing them to keep body fluid concentrations constant. It also forms a system of levers that multiply the force of muscle contraction into movement. Its stiffness comes from mineral, its toughness from collagen, and its capacity to adapt from four cell types that continuously demolish and rebuild it — and most skeletal disease reflects failure of one of those cells, or of the balance between them.

As osteoid calcifies and hardens it entraps some osteoblasts, which then differentiate into osteocytes sitting in lacunae between the lamellae, with cytoplasmic processes in canaliculi. Osteocytes maintain the calcified matrix and are fed from microvasculature in the central canals of osteons through those canaliculi.

The osteoclast is the exception. Every other bone cell is mesenchymal; the osteoclast is monocyte-derived and is the cell required for bone remodeling. It dissolves mineral by pumping H⁺ and digests collagen with cathepsin K.

The RANK–RANKL–OPG axis: osteoblasts express RANKL, which binds RANK on osteoclast precursors and drives differentiation; they also secrete osteoprotegerin (OPG), a decoy receptor that blocks RANKL. PTH shifts the balance toward resorption; estrogen suppresses it.

Bone Matrix

  • Inorganic (~50% of dry weight) — calcium phosphate as hydroxyapatite crystals, with bicarbonate, citrate, magnesium, potassium, and sodium; compressive strength.
  • Organic (~50% of dry weight) — type I collagen (90% of the organic matter), osteocalcin, osteonectin, osteopontin, proteoglycans, growth factors; tensile strength. Some references give a 65:35 ratio instead; both conventions are in current use, so state which one you are using.

Remove the mineral and bone becomes flexible; remove the collagen and it becomes brittle. Bone is strong because it is both at once.

Periosteum & Endosteum

Periosteum — dense connective tissue with a mainly fibrous layer over a more cellular layer; small vessels penetrate the matrix from it. Absent on articular surfaces and at tendon insertions. Endosteum covers all trabeculae around the marrow cavities — not merely the shaft. Both are sources of osteoprogenitor cells for growth and repair.

Types of Bone

Compact (cortical) bone — dense, on the surface; built of osteons (Haversian systems): concentric lamellae around a central Haversian canal containing vessels and nerves, with osteocytes in lacunae linked by canaliculi, and Volkmann canals running transversely.

Spongy (cancellous) bone — a network of trabeculae with marrow between them, oriented along lines of mechanical stress. No true osteons.

Woven versus lamellar. Woven bone is disorganized and produced rapidly in the fetus, fracture callus, and disease (Paget, osteosarcoma). Lamellar bone is ordered and mechanically superior; adult bone is lamellar but always being remodelled through basic multicellular units (BMUs).

Osteogenesis

Intramembranous ossification — mesenchyme condenses and differentiates directly into osteoblasts, depositing bone in a fibrous membrane. Produces the flat bones of the skull, mandible, and clavicle. No cartilage template.

Endochondral ossification — a hyaline cartilage model is replaced by bone. This is how long bones form and grow in length. The epiphyseal plate has five zones:

Zone (from epiphysis toward diaphysis) What happens
1. Reserve (resting) Small, inactive chondrocytes
2. Proliferative Chondrocytes divide in columns — the actual growth in length
3. Hypertrophic Chondrocytes enlarge and the matrix calcifies
4. Calcified cartilage Chondrocytes die; matrix calcifies
5. Ossification Osteoblasts deposit bone on the calcified spicules

Growth in width is appositional, by periosteal osteoblasts. The primary ossification center appears in the diaphysis; secondary centers in the epiphyses. When the plate ossifies, an epiphyseal line remains and length growth stops.

Bone Remodeling & Repair

Fracture → hematoma → inflammation → soft callus (fibrocartilage) → hard callus (woven bone) → remodeling to lamellar bone. Immobilization and blood supply decide the outcome. Wolff’s law — bone is laid down where it is loaded and resorbed where it is not — explains both trabecular architecture and the bone loss of bed rest.

Joints

Fibrous joints (synarthroses) — bones united by dense connective tissue, no cavity (sutures of the skull, gomphosis of a tooth).

Cartilaginous joints (amphiarthroses) — bones united by cartilage: primary by hyaline cartilage (epiphyseal plate, first sternocostal joint); secondary by fibrocartilage (intervertebral disc, symphysis pubis).

Synovial joints (diarthroses) — a joint cavity between hyaline articular cartilage on each bone, enclosed by a capsule of dense connective tissue lined by synovial membrane, which secretes synovial fluid for lubrication and nutrition of the avascular cartilage. Some joints add a meniscus (fibrocartilage), a labrum, or a fat pad. Because articular cartilage has no perichondrium and no vessels, it heals hardly at all — the anatomical basis of degenerative joint disease.

CLINICAL — Six to learn as a set. Osteoporosis — reduced bone mass with normal mineralization; estrogen deficiency drives it, trabeculae are lost first, fragility fractures follow. Rickets/osteomalacia — unmineralized osteoid; in children the growth plate widens and deforms (rachitic rosary, bowed legs). Paget disease — chaotic excessive remodeling with mosaic woven bone. Osteopetrosis — osteoclast failure, dense but brittle bone with a crowded marrow cavity. Achondroplasia — failure of chondrocyte proliferation in the plate, short limbs with normal trunk. Osteogenesis imperfecta — defective type I collagen, brittle bones, blue sclerae.

MICRO — On a ground section, concentric lamellae around a central canal = an osteon = compact bone. On a decalcified H&E section, osteoblasts are the row of cuboidal cells on the surface and osteocytes sit in lacunae. A multinucleated cell in a scalloped pit is an osteoclast resorbing.

MNEMONIC — “OsteoBlast Builds, OsteoClast Chews, OsteoCytes Communicate.” For the epiphyseal plate, “Rest, Proliferate, Hypertrophy, Calcify, Ossify” — read from the joint outward.

TIP — The commonest error is saying osteoclasts are mesenchymal. They are hematopoietic, from the monocyte–macrophage line. The second is reversing the epiphyseal zones: growth happens at the epiphyseal side, ossification at the diaphyseal side.

Chapter 8 — summary

  • Bone matrix is type I collagen plus hydroxyapatite; osteoid must mineralize before it is rigid.
  • Osteoblasts make osteoid; osteocytes maintain matrix; osteoclasts resorb bone with a ruffled border.
  • Osteoclasts are multinucleated and derive from the monocyte–macrophage lineage, not from osteoblasts.
  • Compact bone is built of osteons; spongy bone is a trabecular lattice without osteons.
  • Endochondral ossification replaces a cartilage model; intramembranous ossification forms directly in mesenchyme.
  • The growth plate runs reserve → proliferative → hypertrophic → calcified → ossification zones.
  • Osteogenesis imperfecta = type I collagen; achondroplasia = FGFR3; Marfan = fibrillin.
  • PTH raises serum calcium; calcitonin lowers it; vitamin D increases absorption.
  • Fracture repair: hematoma → fibrocartilaginous callus → bony callus → remodeling.

Active recall

  1. Which cell makes bone matrix, and which resorbs it? — Osteoblasts make osteoid; osteoclasts resorb bone using a ruffled border and acid.
  2. What does osteoid contain, and what mineralizes it? — Type I collagen and proteoglycans; hydroxyapatite crystals deposit along the collagen.
  3. How do osteocytes stay alive inside lacunae? — Their processes run through canaliculi and contact processes of neighboring cells and the central canal, allowing diffusion of nutrients.
  4. Endochondral versus intramembranous ossification? — Endochondral replaces a cartilage model (long bones, base of skull); intramembranous forms directly in mesenchyme (flat skull bones, mandible).
  5. What happens at a growth plate, zone by zone? — Reserve → proliferative → hypertrophic → calcified matrix → ossification, with chondrocyte death and vascular invasion.

9 · Nerve Tissue & the Nervous System

In this chapter: Development of Nerve Tissue · Neurons · Glial Cells & Neuronal Activity · Central Nervous System · Peripheral Nervous System · Neural Plasticity & Regeneration

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Draw a neuron and label its parts, and classify neurons by shape and function.
  2. Name the six glial cell types and give each one’s job.
  3. Explain how a synapse transmits a signal, and how myelin speeds conduction.
  4. Compare regeneration in the peripheral and central nervous systems.

The core idea

Nervous tissue is built for rapid signaling over distance. The neuron is a polarized cell with a receiving end (dendrites and soma), a conducting cable (axon), and a transmitting end (synapse). Everything else — myelin, glia, barriers — exists to make that signaling faster, safer, and better supplied.

Development of Nerve Tissue

The nervous system arises from the neural plate, which folds into the neural tube (giving the CNS) while neural crest cells migrate away to form the peripheral neurons, Schwann cells, satellite cells, adrenal medulla, melanocytes, and much of the facial skeleton. This embryology explains three exam facts: the CNS is lined by ependyma derived from the tube; Schwann cells are neural crest; and the adrenal medulla is neural crest while the cortex is mesoderm.

Neurons

  • Perikaryon (soma) — large, pale nucleus with a prominent nucleolus and abundant Nissl bodies (stacked RER and polysomes). Nissl substance is absent from the axon hillock — a useful landmark.
  • Dendrites — short, branched, receptive; contain Nissl bodies.
  • Axon — one per neuron, arises at the axon hillock, contains no Nissl bodies, specialized for conduction.
  • Axonal transport — kinesin anterograde; dynein retrograde. Rabies, herpes, and tetanus toxin exploit retrograde transport.

Classification: multipolar (most CNS neurons and motor neurons), bipolar (retina, olfactory epithelium), pseudounipolar (sensory ganglia), anaxonic. Also by function — sensory, motor, interneuron.

The synapse. A chemical synapse has a presynaptic terminal with synaptic vesicles and mitochondria, a synaptic cleft (~20–30 nm), and a postsynaptic membrane with receptors. Transmission is unidirectional and modifiable. Electrical synapses (gap junctions) are fast and bidirectional.

There are six major glial types: oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, ependymal cells, and microglia in the CNS; Schwann cells and satellite cells in the PNS. Myelination: in the PNS a Schwann cell wraps one axon segment; in the CNS an oligodendrocyte wraps several. The gaps are nodes of Ranvier, where voltage-gated Na⁺ channels cluster, so the impulse jumps by saltatory conduction. Velocity rises with axon diameter and myelin thickness.

Central Nervous System

  • Cerebral cortex — six layers; pyramidal cells are the output neurons; gray matter outside.
  • Cerebellar cortex — three layers: molecular, Purkinje (large flask-shaped cells, the only output), and granular.
  • Spinal cord — gray matter in an H-shaped core, white matter outside; anterior horn motor, posterior horn sensory. This is the reverse of the cerebral cortex.
  • Meninges — dura, arachnoid, pia; CSF in the subarachnoid space.
  • Choroid plexus — produces CSF.
  • Blood–brain barrier — tight junctions between endothelial cells, reinforced by astrocyte end-feet and pericytes. Absent in the circumventricular organs (area postrema, median eminence, pineal, posterior pituitary), which is how circulating hormones reach the brain.

Peripheral Nervous System

  • Sensory ganglia — pseudounipolar neurons with satellite cells; no synapses inside the ganglion.
  • Autonomic ganglia — multipolar neurons, synapses present.
  • Peripheral nerve — axons in fascicles surrounded by perineurium, the whole nerve by epineurium, individual fibers by endoneurium.

Neural Plasticity & Regeneration

After axotomy the distal stump undergoes Wallerian degeneration; the soma shows central chromatolysis; Schwann cells form bands of Büngner to guide regrowth; regeneration proceeds at about 1 mm/day. CNS axons regenerate poorly because of inhibitory molecules (Nogo, MAG) and glial scarring. Plasticity — the remodeling of synapses with use — is the basis of learning and of rehabilitation after injury.

CLINICAL — Multiple sclerosis is autoimmune demyelination in the CNS. Guillain–Barré syndrome is demyelination in the PNS, ascending from the legs. Parkinson disease is loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra with Lewy bodies. Alzheimer disease shows neurofibrillary tangles and amyloid plaques. Poliomyelitis destroys anterior horn motor neurons and produces flaccid paralysis.

MICRO — Identify neurons by the large pale nucleus, prominent nucleolus, and clumped Nissl bodies. The axon hillock is the pale cone where Nissl substance stops. In peripheral nerve, look for wavy bundles of axons with myelin rings and connective tissue sheaths. In the cerebellar cortex, the single row of enormous Purkinje cells is unmistakable.

TIP — Oligodendrocyte = CNS, several axons. Schwann cell = PNS, one segment of one axon. And microglia are the only glia of mesodermal origin.

Chapter 9 — summary

  • Neurons are classified by shape (multipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar) and by function (sensory, motor, interneuron).
  • Nissl bodies are rough ER plus polysomes; their position marks the soma and proximal dendrites.
  • Oligodendrocytes myelinate the CNS (several axons each); Schwann cells myelinate the PNS (one segment each).
  • Astrocytes form end-feet, contribute to the blood–brain barrier, and scar the CNS.
  • Microglia are the resident CNS macrophages. Under usual physiological conditions they arise mainly from embryonic yolk-sac myeloid progenitors, colonize the developing CNS, and largely maintain themselves by local self-renewal; they are not simply replenished from circulating adult monocytes.
  • Ependymal cells line the ventricles and produce cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Myelin allows saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.
  • The blood–brain barrier is sealed by tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells.
  • PNS axons regenerate through bands of Büngner; CNS axons do not regenerate.

Active recall

  1. Which glial cell myelinates the CNS, and which myelinates the PNS? — Oligodendrocyte (CNS, several axons); Schwann cell (PNS, one segment of one axon).
  2. Which CNS glia act as resident macrophages, and where do they originate? — Microglia; mainly from embryonic yolk-sac myeloid precursors rather than the adult circulating monocyte pool.
  3. What forms the blood–brain barrier? — Tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells, reinforced by astrocyte end-feet and pericytes.
  4. How is a signal carried along an axon? — An action potential; myelin allows saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.
  5. Why does CNS injury scar rather than regenerate? — Oligodendrocytes and myelin inhibit axon growth, astrocytes form a glial scar, and there is no permissive sheath like the PNS bands of Büngner.

Practical identification

Identify a neuron by its large euchromatic nucleus, prominent nucleolus and Nissl bodies; find its dendrites and its single axon hillock. Distinguish astrocytes (star-shaped), oligodendrocytes (small, round, myelinating in the CNS), microglia (small, elongated, perivascular), ependymal cells (ciliated, lining ventricles), and, in peripheral nerve, Schwann cells and satellite cells. In a nerve trunk, find the myelin sheath, node of Ranvier, endoneurium, perineurium and epineurium.

A second section has parallel bundles of nerve fibers enclosed by a distinct perineurium, but no large neuronal somata: this favors a peripheral nerve. A third section contains ependymal lining along a fluid-filled cavity, surrounded by nervous tissue: look for the ventricular or central-canal region rather than a ganglion.

10 · Muscle Tissue

In this chapter: Skeletal Muscle · Cardiac Muscle · Smooth Muscle · Regeneration of Muscle Tissue

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle by light microscopy.
  2. Explain the sarcomere and the sliding-filament mechanism.
  3. Compare excitation–contraction coupling in the three muscle types.
  4. Predict which muscle regenerates and which is replaced by scar.

The core idea

Muscle tissue consists of cells that optimize the universal cell property of contractility. Most muscle cells originate from embryonic mesoderm; the intrinsic muscles of the iris and some head muscles are the standard exceptions, deriving from neural crest and other sources. Muscle differentiates by elongation and abundant synthesis of the myofibrillar proteins actin and myosin. All three types use the same actin–myosin interaction but arrange it differently: striated and voluntary (skeletal), striated and involuntary (cardiac), unstriated and involuntary (smooth).

Skeletal Muscle

Organization, large to small: muscle → fascicles → fibers → myofibrils → myofilaments. Each fiber is a long, cylindrical, multinucleated syncytium with peripheral nuclei, produced by fusion of myoblasts. Satellite cells persist beneath the basal lamina and mediate limited regeneration.

The sarcomere, bounded by two Z lines:

Band Contents Behavior in contraction
A band Thick filaments (myosin) Constant length
I band Thin filaments (actin) only Shortens
H band Thick filaments only, no overlap Shortens
Z line Anchor for actin Moves closer
M line Center of the A band —

Sliding filament mechanism: myosin heads bind actin, hydrolyze ATP, and pivot, pulling thin filaments toward the M line. The A band never changes length.

Control. The sarcoplasmic reticulum stores Ca²⁺; T-tubules are invaginations of the sarcolemma that encircle each myofibril near the aligned A- and I-band boundaries, where each T-tubule associates with two terminal cisternae — a triad. Depolarization spreads along the T-tubules to trigger simultaneous Ca²⁺ release throughout the fiber. An action potential releases Ca²⁺, which binds troponin C, moves tropomyosin off the myosin-binding site, and contraction begins. Relaxation requires Ca²⁺ reuptake.

Innervation. The motor end plate — acetylcholine binds nicotinic receptors, depolarizes the sarcolemma, and triggers contraction. One motor neuron plus all the fibers it supplies is a motor unit.

I (slow, red) Slow Oxidative Resistant Red
IIa (fast, oxidative-glycolytic) Fast Mixed Intermediate Intermediate
IIx/IIb (fast, glycolytic) Fast Glycolytic Fast fatigue White

Sensory receptors. Muscle spindles (intrafusal fibers, Ia afferents, gamma motor neurons) sense length and stretch; Golgi tendon organs sense tension.

Cardiac Muscle

  • Striated, branched fibers, one or two central nuclei.
  • Intercalated discs — the diagnostic feature — contain fascia adherens (anchors actin), desmosomes (mechanical strength), and gap junctions (electrical coupling), making the heart a functional syncytium.
  • Diads at the Z line (one T-tubule + one cisterna), rather than the triads at the A–I junction of skeletal muscle.
  • Abundant mitochondria and lipid droplets; highly oxidative; resistant to fatigue.
  • Purkinje fibers — specialized conducting cells, larger and paler, with fewer myofibrils; they conduct rapidly through the ventricles.
  • Regeneration is essentially absent; damage is repaired by fibrous scar.

Smooth Muscle

  • Spindle-shaped cells with a single central nucleus and no striations.
  • Contractile apparatus: dense bodies (analogous to Z lines) linked by desmin and actin.
  • No troponin. Calcium binds calmodulin, which activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK).
  • Caveolae replace T-tubules; the SR is sparse and Ca²⁺ entry across the membrane matters more.
  • Unitary (single-unit) smooth muscle — gap junctions, spontaneous rhythmicity, in gut, uterus, bladder. Multiunit — each cell separately innervated, in the iris and vas deferens.
  • Capable of hypertrophy and hyperplasia (uterine myometrium in pregnancy).

Regeneration of Muscle Tissue

Skeletal muscle can regenerate to a limited extent from satellite cells; preserved basal-lamina and extracellular-matrix scaffolds favor aligned repair, whereas extensive destruction promotes fibrosis. Cardiac muscle does not regenerate — infarcted myocardium is replaced by scar. Smooth muscle can both hypertrophy and, in the uterus, proliferate.

CLINICAL — Duchenne muscular dystrophy is an X-linked defect of dystrophin, which links the sarcolemma to the extracellular matrix; without it, contraction tears the membrane, fibers die, and fat and fibrous tissue replace muscle (pseudohypertrophy). Cardiac hypertrophy is an adaptation to pressure overload; beyond a point the myocytes outgrow their blood supply and fail.

  • Skeletal muscle: long cylinders, many peripheral nuclei, triads at the A–I junction.
  • Cardiac muscle: branched cells, one or two central nuclei, intercalated discs, diads at the Z line.
  • Smooth muscle: spindle cells, one central nucleus, no striations, no T-tubules (caveolae).
  • Sarcomere = Z line to Z line; A band constant, I band and H zone shorten.
  • Thin filament = actin + troponin + tropomyosin; thick filament = myosin.
  • Ca²⁺ binds troponin C in striated muscle and calmodulin in smooth muscle.
  • Smooth muscle contraction uses myosin light-chain kinase; it has no troponin.
  • Intercalated discs contain fasciae adherentes, desmosomes, and gap junctions — not tight junctions.
  • Skeletal muscle regeneration depends on satellite cells and is more effective when extracellular-matrix scaffolds are preserved.
  • Cardiac muscle does not regenerate; infarction heals by scar.
  • Type I fibers = slow, oxidative, fatigue-resistant. Type II = fast, glycolytic.
  • Duchenne dystrophy = dystrophin defect; myasthenia gravis = antibody to the nicotinic receptor.
  • Muscle spindle = stretch receptor; Golgi tendon organ = tension receptor.

  1. Which band does not change length during contraction? — The A band, because it is the length of the thick filaments.
  2. What hides the myosin-binding site at rest, and what moves it? — Tropomyosin; calcium binds troponin C and shifts tropomyosin.
  3. Cardiac versus skeletal muscle at the T-tubule level? — Cardiac diads lie at the Z line; skeletal triads lie at the A–I junction.
  4. What is the calcium sensor in smooth muscle? — Calmodulin, which activates myosin light-chain kinase; smooth muscle has no troponin.
  5. Which muscle types regenerate? — Skeletal (limited, from satellite cells), smooth (hyperplasia/hypertrophy); cardiac does not.

The core idea

The circulatory system pumps and directs blood cells and substances to all tissues. The enormous capillary network provides a large exchange surface, although estimates of total capillary length and area vary with the method and assumptions used. Almost every vessel is a variation on one three-layered tube. What changes between an aorta and a capillary is how much muscle and elastic tissue each layer contains — reflecting local hemodynamic forces, tissue requirements and developmental specialization.

Heart

  • Endocardium — endothelial lining and underlying connective tissue, continuous with the intima of vessels. A deeper subendocardial layer, particularly in the ventricles, may contain Purkinje fibers; they are not a universal layer of the endocardium.
  • Myocardium — the thickest layer; cardiac muscle in complex spirals, atria thinner than ventricles, left ventricle thickest.
  • Epicardium — connective tissue containing many adipocytes, covered by mesothelium; the visceral layer of the pericardium.
  • Cardiac skeleton — dense irregular connective tissue rings that anchor the valves and electrically insulate atria from ventricles.
  • Valves — endocardium folded over a connective-tissue core; valve leaflets are largely avascular, although small vessels may occur near their bases. The mitral and tricuspid have chordae tendineae and papillary muscles.

Microcirculation. Arterioles feed capillary networks that drain into venules. Metarterioles and discrete precapillary sphincters are described in some vascular beds but are not universal features of all tissues. Arteriovenous anastomoses shunt blood directly, important in skin thermoregulation.

Lymphatic Vascular System

Begins as blind-ended lymphatic capillaries with overlapping endothelial flaps and anchoring filaments, which open when interstitial pressure rises. They converge into collecting vessels with valves, then nodes, trunks, and the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct. Initial lymphatic capillaries have a discontinuous or sparse basal lamina and specialized overlapping endothelial junctions that permit interstitial fluid and cells to enter under appropriate conditions. Microbial spread and tumor-cell entry depend on additional biological mechanisms, not simply on the absence of fenestrations.

CLINICAL — Atherosclerosis begins with endothelial dysfunction: LDL crosses the endothelium, is oxidized, and provokes monocyte recruitment. Macrophages become foam cells, a fatty streak forms, smooth muscle migrates into the intima, and a fibrous cap covers a necrotic core. Rupture of a thin cap exposes thrombogenic material and produces the acute coronary syndrome. Aneurysm follows destruction of medial elastic fibers. Varicose veins follow valve failure. Lymphedema follows lymphatic obstruction — classically after axillary node clearance.

MICRO — Arteries: round lumen, thick wall, corrugated internal elastic lamina. Veins: irregular collapsed lumen, thin media. In elastic arteries the media shows wavy pink bands with no orderly muscle coat; in muscular arteries the media is uniform smooth muscle.

TIP — The three capillaries are distinguished by continuity, not size: continuous (tight), fenestrated (pores), sinusoidal (gaps wide enough for a red cell). Liver, spleen, marrow = sinusoidal. Kidney glomerulus and intestine = fenestrated. Brain = continuous with tight junctions.

The core idea

Blood is connective tissue with a liquid matrix. Its cells all derive from one stem cell, and their morphology is a direct readout of their job: red for oxygen, granular white for killing, agranular white for immunity, and fragments for clotting.

Composition of Plasma

About 55% of blood volume. Plasma consists mainly of water and dissolved proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste. The liver produces most major plasma proteins, including albumin and fibrinogen, while plasma cells produce immunoglobulins; some complement and other circulating proteins also have extrahepatic sources.

On clotting, some proteins are consumed and others are released from platelets, leaving serum, whose biological properties differ from plasma. Serum is plasma minus fibrinogen (i.e., after clotting).

Blood Cells

Erythrocytes

  • Biconcave disc, 7.5 µm in diameter, 2.6 µm thick at the rim but only 0.75 µm at the center — flexible enough to pass small capillaries; no nucleus, no organelles, so the cell is packed with hemoglobin.
  • Lifespan about 120 days; removed by splenic macrophages; the globin is recycled and the heme converted to bilirubin.
  • Shape maintained by spectrin and ankyrin; defects produce hereditary spherocytosis.
  • Hemoglobin — four globin chains, each with a heme group carrying Fe²⁺. Adult HbA (α₂β₂) and fetal HbF (α₂γ₂, higher affinity for O₂).
  • Reticulocytes are young red cells with residual RNA, appearing polychromatic; a raised count signals active marrow output.
  • Hematocrit — the sedimented erythrocyte column — is about 44% of total blood volume in healthy adults; the thin layer between cells and plasma is the buffy coat of leukocytes and platelets. An average adult has 5–6 L of blood.

The leukocytes — broadly grouped as granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and agranulocytes (lymphocytes, monocytes). The granulocytes are 12–15 µm, with eosinophils about the same size as neutrophils or slightly larger. Lymphocytes range from 6 to 15 µm — small, medium, and large forms — and monocytes are 12–20 µm, the largest of the leukocytes in a smear. (Estimates differ: 12–15 µm in some descriptions, 12–20 µm in measurements made on smears. Both are recorded here rather than one being silently preferred.)

Neutrophil granules: azurophilic (primary) granules contain myeloperoxidase and defensins; specific (secondary) granules contain lysozyme and lactoferrin. The neutrophil dies after phagocytosis; the accumulation of dead neutrophils and debris is pus. Each phagocyte kills ingested bacteria inside a phagolysosome.

Platelets are 2–4 µm anucleate fragments of megakaryocyte cytoplasm, with a peripheral clear zone (hyalomere) and a central granular zone (granulomere). They number 150,000–400,000/µL and circulate for about 10 days. They adhere, activate, and aggregate to form the primary hemostatic plug; von Willebrand factor links them to collagen.

Hemostasis in four steps (the standard physiology sequence; this chapter describes only the platelet plug and clotting): (1) Vascular spasm — the injured vessel constricts, slowing flow. (2) Primary hemostasis — platelets bind exposed collagen (directly and through von Willebrand factor), degranulate, and aggregate into a soft plug. (3) Coagulation — exposed tissue factor and platelet phospholipid trigger the clotting cascade, converting prothrombin to thrombin, which cleaves fibrinogen into fibrin; the fibrin mesh stabilizes the plug as a clot. (4) Retraction and repair — the clot contracts, and endothelial cells and fibroblasts rebuild the wall.

CLINICAL — The differential count is a diagnostic instrument. Neutrophilia suggests bacterial infection or steroid use; eosinophilia parasites or allergy; lymphocytosis viral infection or chronic lymphocytic leukemia; pancytopenia marrow failure. In sickle cell disease a single glutamic acid → valine substitution in β-globin allows deoxygenated HbS to polymerize; the cell sickles, occludes small vessels, and is removed by the spleen.

MICRO — Count lobes, not granules. Neutrophil: 3–5 lobes. Eosinophil: exactly two, orange granules. Basophil: granules so dark the nucleus is hidden. Monocyte: kidney-shaped nucleus, largest cell. Lymphocyte: round dense nucleus, almost no cytoplasm.

MNEMONIC — “Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas” = Neutrophil, Lymphocyte, Monocyte, Eosinophil, Basophil — in descending order of abundance.

TIP — Eosinophil granules stain with eosin because they are basic proteins — the naming is the reverse of what students expect. The basophil’s granules contain heparin and histamine, so the “basic” cell is the one full of allergy mediators.

  • Plasma is the fluid phase; serum is plasma after clotting and lacks fibrinogen.
  • Erythrocytes are biconcave, 7.5 µm, anucleate, and live about 120 days.
  • Hemoglobin is the gas carrier; the biconcave shape maximizes surface-to-volume ratio.
  • Neutrophils are the most numerous leukocyte and the first responder to acute inflammation.
  • Eosinophils have bilobed nuclei and orange-red granules (major basic protein); they attack parasites.
  • Basophils carry histamine and heparin and mediate immediate hypersensitivity.
  • Lymphocytes are the second most numerous; monocytes are the largest and become macrophages.
  • Platelets are anucleate fragments of megakaryocytes and form the primary hemostatic plug.
  • Hemostasis = vascular spasm → platelet plug → coagulation → clot retraction and repair.

The core idea

All blood cells descend from one hematopoietic stem cell, and the pathway from that cell to a mature erythrocyte, granulocyte, or platelet is a sequence of named stages you can recognize by decreasing nucleus, increasing cytoplasm, and changing color. It is the lineage with the most named stages in histology, so it rewards systematic learning.

Sites of hemopoiesis

Period Site
First trimester Yolk sac (mesoblastic phase)
Second trimester Liver and spleen (hepatic phase)
Third trimester Bone marrow — marrow becomes the major organ in the third trimester as skeletal elements ossify

In the adult, active red marrow persists in the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis, skull, and proximal femur. The rest is yellow (fatty) marrow, which can revert to red marrow under demand — why chronic anemia produces marrow expansion and frontal bossing.

Stem Cells, Growth Factors, & Differentiation

The hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) is multipotent, not pluripotent: it self-renews and can give rise to the major blood-cell lineages. Human HSC-enriched populations commonly express CD34, but CD34 alone does not uniquely identify a functional stem cell. HSC behavior is regulated by a bone-marrow niche involving stromal cells, vascular cells, and extracellular matrix. Committed progenitor cells — also called CFUs (colony-forming units) — proliferate and differentiate under growth factors, which are also called CSFs (colony-stimulating factors) or cytokines. Bone Marrow

Between the trabeculae lie hemopoietic cords and sinusoids with a discontinuous endothelium, so mature cells can enter the circulation. The myeloid:erythroid (M:E) ratio is normally 2:1 to 4:1; it shifts toward erythroid in hemolytic anemia and toward myeloid in infection or leukemia.

Maturation of Erythrocytes

Proerythroblast → basophilic → polychromatophilic → orthochromatophilic erythroblast → reticulocyte → erythrocyte, developing in erythropoietic islands or cords. The names track the cytoplasm’s transition from RNA-rich to hemoglobin-filled; at the last stage the nucleus is extruded, producing a reticulocyte that still contains some polyribosomes when released into the circulation.

The cell shrinks, the nucleus condenses and is extruded, hemoglobin accumulates so the cytoplasm shifts from blue to pink, and the nucleolus disappears. The sequence takes about a week. EPO drives it; iron, vitamin B₁₂, and folate are required.

Maturation of Granulocytes

Myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band cell → segmented neutrophil (or eosinophil/basophil).

The suffixes carry the meaning: -blast = dividing precursor (myeloblast, lymphoblast, proerythroblast), -cyte = the later, maturing cell (myelocyte, lymphocyte, reticulocyte); the suffix does not by itself mean post-mitotic, because myelocytes still divide; thrombocyte is another name for the platelet.

Primary (azurophilic) granules appear at the promyelocyte stage, specific granules at the myelocyte stage, the nucleus indents at the metamyelocyte stage, and becomes a C-shaped band before segmenting. A left shift — more band forms and younger cells in the peripheral blood — indicates accelerated production: immature neutrophils, chiefly band (stab) cells, are released prematurely when the circulating neutrophil pool is depleted by bacterial infection.

Maturation of Agranulocytes

Lymphopoiesis begins in the marrow for B cells and in the thymus for T cells. Monopoiesis produces monocytes that become tissue macrophages. Both pass through stages in which the nucleus becomes less lobated, the cytoplasm more abundant, and the chromatin pattern characteristic of the mature cell.

Origin of Platelets

Megakaryoblasts → megakaryocytes, which become polyploid and develop demarcation membranes that delineate future platelet territories; platelets bud from the cytoplasm under TPO control.

CLINICAL — Aplastic anemia is marrow failure with an empty, fatty marrow and pancytopenia. Leukemia is the opposite: marrow packed with malignant blasts, crowding out normal lineages and producing anemia, infection, and bleeding. Megaloblastic anemia (B₁₂ or folate deficiency) shows nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony — the nucleus matures more slowly than the cytoplasm, so cells are large and hypersegmented. Iron deficiency produces microcytic hypochromic cells because hemoglobin synthesis lags behind cell division.

MICRO — Identify the lineage by nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and granule color. Early cells have a large nucleus, a thin rim of basophilic cytoplasm, and a nucleolus. As cells mature, the ratio falls, granules appear, and the nucleus condenses. Maturation moves in one direction: nucleus shrinks, cytoplasm grows, color shifts from blue to pink or from blue to granular.

TIP — Two facts that are easy to invert: EPO comes from the kidney (peritubular interstitial cells), and TPO comes mostly from the liver. Students routinely invert these.

Chapter 13 — summary

  • Hemopoiesis shifts from yolk sac → liver and spleen → bone marrow; the adult site is red marrow of the axial skeleton.
  • The marrow stroma (reticular cells, macrophages, adipocytes, endothelial cells) forms the hematopoietic niche.
  • Erythropoiesis: proerythroblast → basophilic → polychromatophilic → orthochromatophilic normoblast → reticulocyte → erythrocyte.
  • The nucleus is extruded at the orthochromatophilic erythroblast stage, producing the reticulocyte, which still contains residual RNA.
  • Granulopoiesis: myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band → segmented cell.
  • Specific granules appear at the myelocyte stage; azurophilic granules at the promyelocyte stage.
  • Megakaryocytes are polyploid and shed platelets from their cytoplasm.
  • Erythropoietin from the kidney drives erythroid production; G-CSF drives granulocytes.
  • The normal myeloid-to-erythroid ratio is about 2:1 to 4:1.

The core idea

The immune system is a set of organs arranged to bring antigen to lymphocytes. Each organ is a different solution to the same problem: how to filter a fluid (blood, lymph, or air/food), trap antigen, and present it to the right cell.

Innate & Adaptive Immunity

Feature Innate Adaptive

A working definition is worth memorizing: innate immunity is present from birth and uses leukocytes (mainly granulocytes) plus soluble proteins; adaptive immunity develops more slowly and is based on antigen presentation to lymphocytes.

Cytokines

Polypeptide hormones of the immune system, secreted by immune cells to regulate one another. The ones worth naming: interleukins (IL-1, IL-2 for T-cell proliferation, IL-4 for IgE and Th2 responses, IL-5 for eosinophils, IL-6 for acute-phase response), interferons (antiviral; IFN-γ activates macrophages), TNF-α (inflammation, fever, cachexia), chemokines (directed leukocyte migration), and colony-stimulating factors (hemopoiesis, Chapter 13). Cytokines act locally (paracrine) or on the secreting cell (autocrine), and their excessive release produces a cytokine storm and the systemic inflammatory response.

Antigens & Antibodies

Antigens are molecules recognized by the adaptive immune system; epitopes are the specific parts bound by a receptor. Antibodies are immunoglobulins:

Antigen Presentation

T cells cannot see free antigen. They recognize peptide displayed on MHC molecules:

  • MHC class I — on the surface of all nucleated cells, bearing fragments of that cell’s own proteins (and viral proteins if infected); recognized by CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells.
  • MHC class II — expressed chiefly by professional antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B lymphocytes) and also by specialized cells such as thymic epithelial cells; expression can be induced in other cells during inflammation. It commonly presents peptides from extracellular proteins processed in endosomal compartments, including self as well as foreign antigens, to CD4⁺ T cells. The distinction from MHC I is useful but not absolute, because cross-presentation and other processing routes exist.

This pairing — CD4/MHC II, CD8/MHC I — is the one to know cold.

Cells of Adaptive Immunity

T lymphocytes mature in the thymus and are responsible for cell-mediated immunity. Their T-cell receptor (TCR) is usually an αβ heterodimer; each T cell also carries one CD marker that fixes its class:

T-cell class Marker Job
Helper T cell CD4⁺ Recognizes MHC II; secretes cytokines that activate B cells and macrophages
Cytotoxic T cell CD8⁺ Recognizes MHC I; kills infected or tumor cells
Regulatory T cell CD4⁺ CD25⁺ Suppresses immune responses and prevents autoimmunity
γδ T cell γδ TCR Mainly in epithelia; early defense at body surfaces

B lymphocytes mature in bone marrow and carry a B-cell receptor (BCR) — surface IgM or IgD — that binds antigen directly, without MHC. On activation they become plasma cells (antibody factories) and memory cells. NK cells kill without prior sensitization and without MHC restriction.

How a Lymphoid Nodule Forms

This sequence explains every secondary lymphoid organ in the chapter:

  1. Lymphocytes are packed in a meshwork of reticulin made by fibroblastic reticular cells; most APCs among them are dendritic cells with many processes.
  2. In a secondary organ, follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) display antigen–antibody complexes on their large surface. A B cell whose BCR binds that antigen is held there.
  3. With cytokines from helper T cells, that B cell proliferates clonally, producing a large transient nodule with a pale germinal center.
  4. Selected B cells can exit as antibody-secreting plasma cells or memory B cells. T follicular helper cells support the germinal-center reaction; the germinal center is not a production site for memory T cells. An active secondary follicle has a pale germinal center, whereas a resting primary follicle lacks one.

Thymus

  • Capsule with trabeculae dividing it into lobules, each with a cortex and a medulla.
  • No follicles. The cortex is densely packed with developing thymocytes and epithelial reticular cells; the medulla is paler and contains Hassall corpuscles — concentric whorls of epithelial cells, unique to the thymus.
  • Blood–thymus barrier — formed by squamous thymic epithelial cells joined by desmosomes and occluding junctions, together with vascular endothelium and pericytes — prevents unregulated exposure of thymocytes to antigens.
  • Cortex is packed with T lymphoblasts (thymocytes), macrophages, and thymic epithelial cells (TECs). Stellate TECs form a cytoreticulum to which lymphocytes attach instead of reticulin fibers; these TECs are antigen-presenting cells expressing MHC class II and secreting cytokines — which is why the thymus is also counted among endocrine glands.
  • Hassall corpuscles — aggregates of medullary TECs, concentrically arranged, up to 100 µm, unique to the medulla; their cells secrete cytokines that control local dendritic cells and promote regulatory T cells for peripheral tolerance.
  • Function: T-cell maturation and selection. Positive selection occurs in the cortex: only thymocytes whose TCR recognizes the individual’s own MHC molecules survive. Negative selection occurs in the medulla: thymocytes that bind self-antigen too tightly are deleted, leaving tolerance. Medullary TECs express Aire, which lets them display a wide sample of tissue self-antigens for this test.
  • Involutes after puberty, replaced by adipose tissue.

Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue

Tonsils (palatine, lingual, pharyngeal/adenoid, tubal) — a partial capsule (hemicapsule), no afferent lymphatics, epithelium invaginates into crypts. Peyer’s patches in the ileum; appendix; MALT throughout the gut, respiratory tract, and genitourinary tract.

Spleen

  • No afferent lymphatics. It filters blood.
  • White pulp — lymphocytes around a central arteriole: the periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS) is the T-cell zone; follicles at its periphery are the B-cell zone.
  • Red pulp — cords of Billroth and sinusoids whose elongated endothelial stave cells are separated by narrow slits.
  • Two routes through the red pulp. In the closed circulation, capillaries from the penicillar arterioles connect directly to sinusoids. In the open circulation (about half of the penicillar arterioles), capillaries are open-ended and dump blood into the splenic cords, so plasma and cells must squeeze between stave cells to re-enter the vasculature. Platelets, motile leukocytes, and thin flexible erythrocytes pass; stiff or swollen erythrocytes at the end of their 120-day life are blocked and removed by macrophages.
  • Iron recycling. Splenic and hepatic macrophages break down senescent erythrocyte hemoglobin. Heme oxygenase releases iron for storage in ferritin or export through ferroportin; circulating iron binds transferrin for reuse in erythropoiesis. The heme ring is catabolized to biliverdin and then bilirubin for hepatic processing and biliary excretion. Hemopexin binds free heme in plasma to limit heme toxicity; it is not the carrier required for every intracellular heme-degradation reaction.
  • Functions: blood filtration, removal of old erythrocytes, immune response to blood-borne antigen, and extramedullary hemopoiesis in fetal life and marrow failure.

The core idea

The gut is one tube with four concentric layers, and every region is a modification of those four layers for a specific job: grinding, acidifying, absorbing, or dehydrating. Learn the four layers and their regional variations and you can describe any segment from esophagus to anus.

General Structure of the Digestive Tract

Layer Contents Function
Mucosa Epithelium + lamina propria + muscularis mucosae Digestion, absorption, secretion, barrier
Submucosa Loose connective tissue, vessels, lymphatics, Meissner (submucosal) plexus, glands where present Supply and control of secretion
Muscularis Inner circular + outer longitudinal smooth muscle, Auerbach (myenteric) plexus between them Peristalsis
Serosa or adventitia Mesothelium + connective tissue (intraperitoneal) or connective tissue only (retroperitoneal) Suspension and protection

This is the skeleton of every answer about the gut. Two rules organize it:

  • In the digestive tract the mucosa has three components — a lining epithelium, a lamina propria of loose connective tissue, and smooth muscle from the muscularis mucosae. The epithelium changes; the other two do not.
  • Serosa = suspended in the abdomen; adventitia = embedded in surrounding tissue. The esophagus develops within the mediastinum and therefore has an adventitia along almost its whole length.

Both plexuses belong to the enteric nervous system: Meissner’s controls secretion and blood flow, Auerbach’s controls motility. The lymphocytes and nodules scattered through mucosa and submucosa are MALT (Chapter 14).

Oral Cavity

The lining is stratified squamous everywhere, but the degree of keratinization tracks wear: keratinized on the hard palate and gingiva, partly keratinized on the lips, and nonkeratinized over most of the soft oral mucosa.

Lips — three zones: cutaneous (skin with hair and sebaceous glands), vermilion (thin keratinized epithelium over vascular connective tissue — the red of the lip), and mucosal (nonkeratinized, wet). A core of striated muscle makes the lips mobile.

Tongue — skeletal muscle in bundles, covered by stratified squamous epithelium with four papilla types:

Papilla Shape Taste buds Notes
Filiform Cone-shaped, heavily keratinized No Most numerous; mechanical; gives the whitish dorsal appearance
Fungiform Mushroom-shaped, lightly keratinized Yes Scattered among filiform
Foliate Leaf-like folds Yes Lateral margins
Vallate (circumvallate) Largest, 1–3 mm wide, in a trench Yes, ~250 per papilla 8–12 in a V in front of the terminal sulcus

Taste buds are ovoid intraepithelial organs of 50–100 cells, about half of them elongated gustatory cells with a 7–10-day turnover. Their microvilli project toward a taste pore in the epithelial surface; support cells and basal stem cells complete the bud. Filiform papillae never carry taste buds — a standard trap.

Teeth. An adult has 32 permanent teeth. Each has a crown, a constricted neck, and roots in dental alveoli.

  • Enamel — hardest material in the body: 96% calcium hydroxyapatite, only 2–3% organic, no collagen. It is built of enamel rods ~5 µm wide running the full ~2 mm thickness. Ameloblasts secrete a collagen-free matrix rich in amelogenin (20 kDa), which initiates and guides crystal growth. Enamel is acellular; ameloblasts disappear at eruption, so enamel cannot regenerate, and it is lost from decalcified sections.
  • Dentin — 70% hydroxyapatite in a type I collagen matrix, harder than bone. Odontoblasts (cranial neural crest) secrete predentin, which mineralizes within about a day. Odontoblasts can persist for many years, and their processes extend into dentinal tubules, although their extent varies. Sensory nerve fibers enter the inner dentin in some regions; they do not accompany every odontoblast process throughout the full dentin thickness. Movement of fluid within dentinal tubules is a leading explanation for dentin sensitivity (the hydrodynamic theory).
  • Cementum — bone-like covering of the roots. Pulp — vascular, innervated loose connective tissue entering through apical foramina.
  • Periodontium = cementum + periodontal ligament + alveolar bone + gingiva. The ligament’s collagen bundles (Sharpey fibers) bind cementum to bone; it is 150–350 µm thick.

Esophagus

A muscular tube about 25 cm long. Nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium, a thick muscularis mucosae, esophageal glands in the submucosa (mucus-secreting, lubricating), and small cardiac glands in the lamina propria near the junction.

The muscularis shows the progression to know: skeletal muscle at the superior end → mixed in the middle → exclusively smooth in the lower third. The outer layer is adventitia except for the distal 1–2 cm, which lies in the peritoneal cavity and has a serosa. At the esophagogastric junction the epithelium changes abruptly from stratified squamous to simple columnar, and the adventitia gives way to serosa.

Practical Histology: Identifying the Esophagus–Stomach Transition

At low magnification, begin with the luminal epithelium rather than the thickness of the muscle wall. The esophagus has nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium designed to resist abrasion. The stomach has simple columnar mucous epithelium that descends into gastric pits. The transition between the two epithelial patterns is usually abrupt.

Next, inspect the mucosal glands. Esophageal submucosal glands are mucus-secreting and sit beneath the muscularis mucosae; the stomach’s gastric pits and glands belong to the mucosa. A gland’s position relative to the muscularis mucosae is often more informative than its color alone.

Finally, assess the muscularis externa. Esophageal muscle changes from skeletal to smooth along its length, while the stomach’s muscular wall is predominantly smooth muscle. Do not use muscle type alone to identify the junction: a small or obliquely cut specimen may not show the full wall.

Observation Favors esophagus Favors stomach

Mucosal glands Usually absent except near the ends Region-specific gastric glands
Submucosal glands May be present Not a defining feature

Clinical connection. Chronic reflux may cause replacement of the distal esophageal squamous lining by intestinal-type columnar epithelium (Barrett esophagus). Identification and grading require proper biopsy interpretation; the mere presence of columnar cells at a junction is not sufficient by itself.

Stomach

Four regions: the superior cardia and inferior pylorus resemble each other histologically (branching mucous glands, few parietal or chief cells), while the intervening fundus and body contain the main gastric glands. Cardiac and pyloric glands are predominantly mucous; parietal cells may be present in small numbers, especially near transitions. They should not be described as categorically absent. The surface is perforated by gastric pits, which are lined by the same surface mucous cells as the stomach lumen and lead into branching glands. No goblet cells, no villi.

The gland epithelium contains surface mucous cells, mucous neck cells, parietal cells, chief cells, and enteroendocrine cells, plus epithelial stem and progenitor populations concentrated in the isthmus/neck region:

Parietal (oxyntic) Upper half of gland HCl + intrinsic factor Large cell, many mitochondria, intracellular canaliculi; intrinsic factor is required for vitamin B₁₂ uptake; eosinophilic
Chief (zymogenic) Deeper half of gland Pepsinogen Activated to pepsin by luminal acid; basophilic with apical zymogen granules
Mucous neck Neck Mucus (less alkaline than surface mucus) Secretion; the proliferative stem-cell zone lies among them
Surface mucous Surface and pits Viscous mucus + bicarbonate The barrier that protects the mucosa

Parietal cells are controlled both by parasympathetic innervation and by paracrine histamine and gastrin from enteroendocrine cells — the pharmacological basis of proton-pump inhibitors and H₂ blockers. The muscularis has three layers (oblique, circular, longitudinal), an exception to the four-layer template that allows churning.

Small Intestine

The small intestine is several metres long in adults; measured length varies with whether it is examined in vivo or after removal and with the degree of muscular contraction. Its absorptive surface is amplified at three levels: plicae circulares (mucosa + submucosa, about a 3-fold increase), villi (mucosa, about 10-fold), and microvilli (cell surface, a further 20-fold), with a large cumulative increase in absorptive surface; published total-area estimates vary considerably with the measurement method — the clearest “structure follows function” example in the body.

Each villus is a finger of lamina propria covered by simple columnar epithelium, containing a lacteal and smooth muscle. Between villi, simple tubular crypts (of Lieberkühn) are the proliferative compartment. Smooth muscle of the lamina propria and muscularis mucosae, controlled by Meissner’s plexus, moves the villi and propels lymph; the muscularis, controlled by Auerbach’s plexus, produces peristalsis.

Cell types and what each absorbs or secretes:

  • Enterocytes — brush border; disaccharidases and peptidases in the glycocalyx complete digestion, and the resulting monosaccharides and amino acids are absorbed across the brush border and pass to capillaries.
  • Goblet cells — mucus.
  • M cells — specialized epithelium over the lymphoid nodules, which takes up antigen from the lumen and hands it to the lymphocytes below.
  • Paneth cells — base of crypts, eosinophilic granules, secrete defensins (and lysozyme).
  • Enteroendocrine cells — CCK, secretin, GIP, motilin.
  • Stem cells — crypt base, replacing the epithelium every few days.

Lipid absorption is different and worth knowing: lipid products associate with bile salts, enter enterocytes, are re-esterified to triglycerides and packaged as chylomicrons, and leave through the lacteal rather than the capillary.

Region Defining feature
Duodenum Duodenal (Brunner) glands in the submucosa; leaf-like villi
Jejunum Tallest villi; no Brunner glands; Peyer’s patches belong to the ileum
Ileum Peyer’s patches (mucosal and submucosal); shortest villi

Large Intestine

Three regions: the short cecum with the appendix; the long colon (ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid); and the rectum.

No villi, straight tubular crypts lined by colonocytes (absorptive, water and electrolyte uptake) and abundant goblet cells. The outer longitudinal muscle is gathered into three teniae coli, which produce the bulging haustra; the serosa carries hanging fat masses, the omental appendages (appendices epiploicae). Function: water and electrolyte absorption, mucus secretion, bacterial fermentation. The appendix is a blind diverticulum of the cecum with abundant lymphoid tissue in its lamina propria and submucosa — part of MALT.

The rectum continues as the anal canal. There the mucosa is highly vascular, folded into anal columns (of Morgagni) with intervening anal sinuses, and the columnar lining changes to stratified squamous epithelium at the rectoanal junction. Swollen mucosal or submucosal veins here are hemorrhoids.

CLINICAL — Peptic ulcer — H. pylori breaches the mucous barrier; acid and pepsin digest the wall, classically in the duodenal bulb or gastric antrum. Pernicious anemia — loss of parietal cells means no intrinsic factor, so B₁₂ cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum. Celiac disease — villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and intraepithelial lymphocytes in the proximal small intestine, driven by gluten. Barrett esophagus — chronic acid reflux causes metaplasia from stratified squamous to intestinal-type columnar epithelium with goblet cells, a premalignant change. Diverticulosis — mucosa and submucosa herniate between the teniae coli; trapped feces cause diverticulitis.

MICRO — Identify the segment by the surface, not the glands. Villi + crypts = small intestine. No villi + deep pits + thick gland mass = stomach. No villi + straight crypts + many goblet cells = colon. Then find the regional marker: Brunner glands = duodenum; Peyer’s patches = ileum; three muscle layers = stomach.

MNEMONIC — “Duodenum has Brunner Below; Ileum has Peyer’s Patches.” And “Filiform = Feel only; Vallate = Carry most taste.”

PEARL — The numbers that appear in questions: enamel 96% mineral and 2 mm thick; dentin 70%; esophageal length 25 cm; 8–12 vallate papillae with about 250 taste buds each. The stomach has no goblet cells and no villi; the intestine has both.

Practical Histology: Distinguishing Duodenum, Jejunum, Ileum, and Colon

Identify the intestinal segment from multiple tissue layers, not from a single villus or lymphoid nodule. Orientation, distension, and the depth of the biopsy can hide useful landmarks.

Segment Mucosal pattern Most discriminating supporting feature
Duodenum Villi and intestinal crypts Brunner glands in the submucosa; a superficial biopsy may miss them
Jejunum Often prominent villi and plicae circulares No defining Brunner glands or extensive aggregated Peyer patches in a representative full-thickness section
Ileum Villi and crypts; often more goblet cells distally Aggregated lymphoid nodules (Peyer patches), typically extending into the submucosa
Colon Straight crypts rich in goblet cells; no normal villi Continuous crypt-rich mucosa without small-intestinal villi

Worked example 1. A specimen contains villi and crypts, plus pale mucous glands beneath the muscularis mucosae. Best answer: duodenum. Confirm that the glands lie in the submucosa, rather than confusing them with ordinary intestinal crypts.

Worked example 2. A section shows numerous straight goblet-rich crypts and a flat luminal surface without villi. Best answer: colonic mucosa. A biopsy alone may not reliably distinguish individual colonic segments.

Worked example 3. A villous small-intestinal specimen has a large aggregate of lymphoid follicles extending toward the submucosa. Best answer: ileum, especially if the aggregate is characteristic of a Peyer patch. Isolated lymphoid follicles can occur elsewhere, so use the complete pattern.

Boundary of inference. Villus height, goblet-cell abundance and fold prominence vary along the intestine and with sampling. If submucosa is absent and the distinguishing landmarks are not seen, report small-intestinal mucosa rather than guessing a segment.

Self-test. Why can a routine mucosal biopsy fail to establish duodenal identity even when the patient has duodenal tissue? Answer: the biopsy may not include the submucosa, where Brunner glands are located.

  • Auerbach lies between the muscle layers (motility); Meissner lies in the submucosa (secretion, blood flow).
  • Keratinized oral epithelium: hard palate and gingiva. Everywhere else, mostly nonkeratinized.
  • Filiform papillae are the only type without taste buds.
  • Vallate papillae: 8–12, largest, ~250 taste buds each.

  • Dentin is 70% hydroxyapatite in type I collagen; odontoblast processes stay in dentinal tubules.
  • Esophageal muscle: skeletal above, mixed in the middle, smooth below.
  • Esophagogastric junction: squamous → columnar, and adventitia → serosa.

16 · Organs Associated with the Digestive Tract

In this chapter: Salivary Glands · Pancreas · Liver · Biliary Tract & Gallbladder

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Compare the secretory units and ducts of the three major salivary glands.
  2. Explain how the exocrine pancreas makes and delivers digestive enzymes and bicarbonate.
  3. Trace blood and bile through a hepatic lobule in opposite directions.
  4. Explain how the gallbladder concentrates bile and what controls its release.

The core idea

Four gland systems share one duct system and one embryological origin: the salivary glands lubricate and begin digestion, the pancreas digests, the liver processes, and the gallbladder stores. Each is built around its job, and each has one histologic signature that identifies it.

Salivary Glands

The three major pairs produce about 90% of saliva; minor glands scattered through the oral mucosa contribute the rest. Total output is 0.75–1.5 L/day.

Gland Secretory units Secretion
Parotid Serous acini only Watery, enzyme-rich
Submandibular Mixed, mainly serous acini Mixed
Sublingual Mixed, mainly mucous tubules, some with serous demilunes (partly a fixation artifact) Viscous

Serous cells are protein-secreting and form round or oval acini; mucous cells secrete mucin and form elongated tubules. Myoepithelial cells sit between the secretory cells and the basal lamina and squeeze the secretion onward.

The duct system is a three-step sequence, and each step has a different epithelium:

  1. Intercalated ducts — simple cuboidal; the smallest.
  2. Striated ducts — simple columnar, with mitochondria-lined basolateral folds that reabsorb Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and add HCO₃⁻. This is why saliva is hypotonic and why the striated duct is the histologic signature of a salivary gland.
  3. Interlobular (excretory) ducts — unusually, lined by stratified cuboidal or columnar epithelium.

Pancreas

A compound acinar gland, entirely exocrine except for the scattered islets of Langerhans. It secretes about 1.5 L/day of alkaline juice.

  • Acinar cells — pyramidal, with a basal nucleus, abundant rough ER and Golgi in the basal cytoplasm, and eosinophilic zymogen granules apically. The cell is polarized for export: make protein at the base, package it at the apex.
  • Centroacinar cells — pale cells inserted into the acinar lumen; they begin the duct system inside the acinus.
  • Ducts — intercalated → intralobular → interlobular → main pancreatic duct. Centroacinar and intercalated duct cells secrete HCO₃⁻ under secretin, neutralizing chyme and establishing the pH at which pancreatic enzymes work. The high pH also keeps the enzymes inactive until they reach the duodenum.
  • Endocrine islets — pale, round clusters between acini (details in Chapter 20).

The pancreas has only a very thin capsule, whose septa carry the larger vessels and ducts and separate the parenchyma into lobules; each acinus is surrounded by a basal lamina and a delicate reticular sheath — a useful contrast with the parotid, which has striated ducts and no islets.

Liver

The liver is both an endocrine gland (plasma proteins into blood) and an exocrine gland (bile into ducts). That dual output explains its unusual architecture: two fluid streams run in opposite directions through the same lobule.

Blood supply. About 75% of hepatic blood is nutrient-rich, O₂-poor portal vein blood; 25% is oxygenated hepatic artery blood. Both enter at the portal areas and flow toward the central vein.

  • Classic lobule — a polygon of plates of hepatocytes radiating toward a central vein, with portal areas at the corners. Connective tissue between lobules is sparse, becoming more abundant in the portal areas.
  • Portal area (triad + one) — a portal venule, a hepatic arteriole, a bile ductule, and a small lymphatic.
  • Hepatocytes — large polygonal epithelial cells with large central nuclei; about 50% are polyploid and many are binucleated. They contain abundant rough and smooth ER, many small Golgi complexes, glycogen, and peroxisomes.
  • Sinusoids — discontinuous, fenestrated endothelium. Between endothelium and hepatocytes is the perisinusoidal space (of Disse), where plasma exchanges with hepatocytes.
  • Kupffer cells — stellate macrophages in the sinusoidal lining; remove effete erythrocytes and release iron and bilirubin.
  • Hepatic stellate (Ito) cells — in the perisinusoidal space, storing vitamin A and other fat-soluble vitamins in lipid droplets; they become myofibroblasts in fibrosis.
  • Bile canaliculi — grooves between adjacent hepatocytes, sealed by tight junctions. Bile flows toward the portal area while blood flows away from it.
  • Canals of Hering — where canaliculi join the bile ductules; all ducts eventually form the left and right hepatic ducts.

Functions: carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism; albumin and clotting factor synthesis; detoxification by SER and peroxisomes; bile production; bilirubin conjugation; storage of glycogen, iron, and vitamins A, D, and B₁₂.

Zonation. Zone I (periportal) receives the most oxygen and nutrients and performs oxidative metabolism, such as protein synthesis; zone III (pericentral) receives the least, and its cells are the first to accumulate fat and undergo ischemic necrosis. Zone III is also where drug biotransformation predominates — the basis for many patterns of hepatic injury.

Biliary Tract & Gallbladder

Every bile-conducting structure after the canaliculi is lined by cholangiocytes — simple cuboidal or columnar epithelium.

Route: canaliculi → canals of Hering → bile ductules → interlobular ducts → left and right hepatic ducts → common hepatic duct → cystic duct → gallbladder → cystic duct → common bile duct → hepatopancreatic ampulla (of Vater) → duodenum.

The gallbladder is a sac with a highly folded mucosa, a well-vascularized lamina propria, no muscularis mucosae or submucosa, and a muscularis of interwoven bundles. Its columnar cholangiocytes absorb water, concentrating bile; the muscle contracts under cholecystokinin (CCK) released by duodenal enteroendocrine cells when food arrives.

CLINICAL — Cirrhosis is the end result of hepatocyte injury: fibrosis with regenerative nodules, portal hypertension, and synthetic failure. Acute pancreatitis is autodigestion by prematurely activated enzymes; chronic pancreatitis destroys acini and islets, producing diabetes and steatorrhea. Carcinoma of the head of the pancreas obstructs the common bile duct and produces painless obstructive jaundice. Gallstones form when bile is supersaturated with cholesterol or pigment; impaction in the cystic or common bile duct produces biliary colic and obstructive jaundice.

MICRO — The liver is recognized by cords of eosinophilic cells with central veins and portal areas. The pancreas by dark purple acini with pink apical granules and pale islets. Pancreas versus parotid: the pancreas has islets and no striated ducts; the parotid has striated ducts and no islets.

TIP — Bile flows toward the portal triad; blood flows away from it. The common error is to memorize “everything flows to the center”.

17 · The Respiratory System

In this chapter: Nasal Cavities · Pharynx · Larynx · Trachea · Bronchial Tree & Lung · Lung Vasculature & Nerves · Pleural Membranes · Respiratory Movements

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Follow the airway from nose to alveolus and name what changes at each level.
  2. Identify respiratory epithelium and its cell types.
  3. Explain the blood–air barrier, surfactant, and the mechanics of ventilation.
  4. Distinguish conducting from respiratory airways in a micrograph.

The core idea

The respiratory tract is a conducting series (nose to terminal bronchiole) followed by a respiratory series (respiratory bronchiole to alveolus). The transition is marked by the disappearance of cartilage and the appearance of alveoli. Walking down the tree, each level loses one structural support and gains a thinner epithelium.

Nasal Cavities

Each nasal cavity has a vestibule where air enters, and three conchae projecting from the medial wall that create turbulence and force air over a large mucosal surface.

  • Vestibule — stratified squamous epithelium with vibrissae (stiff hairs) that filter large particles.
  • Respiratory region — respiratory epithelium: pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells, over a highly vascular lamina propria containing seromucous glands. It warms, humidifies, and cleans air; the cilia sweep mucus toward the pharynx.
  • Olfactory region — the roof and part of the superior concha: olfactory epithelium, about 10 cm² and up to 100 µm thick, containing bipolar olfactory neurons, supporting cells, and basal stem cells. Each neuron has about 20 long nonmotile cilia bearing odorant receptors. Olfactory neurons are replaced every 1–2 months — unusual for neurons. No goblet cells, but Bowman glands in the lamina propria.

The respiratory epithelium is a mixed population, and the proportions are worth remembering:

Cell Share Function
Ciliated columnar cells Majority Move the mucus blanket
Goblet cells — Secrete mucus
Brush cells ~3% Chemosensory; short blunt microvilli
Small granule (Kulchitsky) cells ~3% DNES; dense-core granules 100–300 nm
Basal cells ~30% Stem cells

Pharynx

Nasopharynx — respiratory epithelium with lymphoid tissue (pharyngeal tonsil) in the lamina propria. Oropharynx and laryngopharynx — stratified squamous epithelium, continuous with the oral cavity and esophagus. The change from respiratory to stratified squamous epithelium marks where the airway is exposed to abrasion from food.

Larynx

A short air passage between pharynx and trachea, with skeletal cartilages and intrinsic muscles.

  • Epiglottis — elastic cartilage, with stratified squamous epithelium on its lingual surface and respiratory epithelium on its laryngeal surface; the switch is abrupt.
  • Vocal folds (true cords) — stratified squamous epithelium, avascular, with the vocal ligament and the vocalis muscle. Tension and vibration produce sound.
  • False cords (vestibular folds) — respiratory epithelium and glands.

Trachea

  • Epithelium — respiratory epithelium (pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells); the trachea is 10–12 cm long and supported by C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage.
  • Lamina propria — elastic fibers, lymphocytes, seromucous glands.
  • Cartilage — C-shaped hyaline rings, incomplete posteriorly.
  • Trachealis muscle — smooth muscle bridging the posterior gap, allowing the esophagus to expand.

Bronchial Tree & Lung

Bronchi — the primary bronchus enters each lung and branches repeatedly. Cartilage breaks into irregular plates rather than rings, a complete ring of smooth muscle appears, and elastic fibers and glands persist. Each tertiary (segmental) bronchus with its branches forms a bronchopulmonary segment — a pyramidal portion of lung with its own blood supply, separated from its neighbors by connective tissue septa.

Bronchioles — airways of 1 mm or less, formed after about the tenth generation of branching. They have no cartilage and no glands, a simple ciliated columnar or cuboidal epithelium, circular smooth muscle, and elastic fibers.

  • Club cells (formerly Clara cells) replace goblet cells as the main secretory cell; they secrete surfactant-like material, help innate immunity, and their smooth ER P450 system detoxifies inhaled compounds. They are the progenitor cells of the bronchiolar epithelium.
  • Terminal bronchiole — the last conducting airway, lined mainly by club cells, with no alveoli.

Respiratory region: terminal bronchiole → respiratory bronchiole (simple cuboidal, interrupted by alveoli) → alveolar duct (a linear series of alveoli) → alveolar sac (a cluster). Gas exchange begins at the respiratory bronchiole.

Cell Shape Function Share of alveolar lining
Type I pneumocyte Squamous, extremely thin Gas exchange; forms the blood–air barrier Lines almost the entire alveolar surface
Type II pneumocyte Cuboidal, lamellar bodies Surfactant; innate immunity; progenitor for type I A small part of each alveolus
Alveolar macrophage Large, free (“dust cell”) Phagocytosis —

Numbers to know:

  • Each alveolus is about 200 µm across. Each adult lung has about 200 million alveoli, with a total internal surface area of about 75 m².
  • The blood–air barrier = surfactant film + type I pneumocyte + fused basal laminae + capillary endothelium. Its total thickness is 0.1–1.5 µm, and the attenuated type I cytoplasm can be as thin as 25 nm.
  • Surfactant is an oily mixture of cholesterol, phospholipids, and surfactant proteins secreted by type II cells and club cells. It lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse at end-expiration.
  • Pores of Kohn — 10–15 µm openings that connect adjacent alveoli and equalize air pressure.

Lung Vasculature & Nerves

The lung has a double blood supply: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood for gas exchange and forms a dense capillary network around alveoli, while the bronchial arteries (from the aorta) supply the conducting airways and pleura. Pulmonary arteries are unusual in carrying deoxygenated blood and in having thin walls suited to low pressure. Autonomic nerves control bronchial smooth muscle and gland secretion — parasympathetic (vagal) constriction, sympathetic relaxation.

Pleural Membranes

A serous membrane of simple squamous mesothelium over connective tissue: visceral pleura covers the lung and is continuous with parietal pleura lining the thoracic wall. A thin fluid film lets the layers slide while surface tension holds them together, so the lung follows the chest wall.

Respiratory Movements

Ventilation is pressure-driven: the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles enlarge the thoracic cavity, pleural pressure falls, the lung expands passively, and air enters. Elastic recoil of the lung and surface tension at the alveolar air–liquid interface drive expiration — precisely why surfactant deficiency causes collapse.

CLINICAL — Respiratory distress syndrome of the newborn — immature type II pneumocytes produce insufficient surfactant, so alveoli collapse; treatment is exogenous surfactant and antenatal steroids. Emphysema — destruction of alveolar walls enlarges air spaces and reduces surface area. Asthma — bronchoconstriction, mucosal edema, eosinophilic inflammation, thickened basement membrane, and smooth muscle hypertrophy. Chronic bronchitis — mucus hypersecretion and gland hypertrophy. Cystic fibrosis — defective CFTR thickens secretions, causing bronchiectasis and recurrent infection. Lung cancer — three main types are described here: squamous cell carcinoma (smoking-related, arises in segmental bronchi), adenocarcinoma (commonest in non-smokers, arises peripherally in bronchioles and alveoli), and small cell carcinoma (less common, highly malignant, arising from small granule Kulchitsky cells of the bronchial epithelium).

MICRO — Walk down the tree and count what disappears. Cartilage rings + glands = trachea. Cartilage plates + no glands = bronchus. No cartilage, no glands, muscular wall = bronchiole. Then look for alveoli in the wall: if present, it is a respiratory bronchiole.

MNEMONIC — “Rings, Plates, None” for cartilage from trachea → bronchus → bronchiole. And “Type I for gas, Type II for surfactant, macrophage for garbage.”

TIP — Club cells are found in bronchioles, not alveoli, and are the progenitor cells of the bronchiolar epithelium. A question that places them in the alveolus is testing whether you know the level of the airway.

Chapter 17 — summary

  • The conducting airway is nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles.
  • Respiratory epithelium is pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells.
  • Cartilage changes down the tree: C-shaped rings → irregular plates → none.
  • The respiratory bronchiole is the first airway with alveoli in its wall.
  • Type I pneumocytes line almost the entire alveolar surface and form the thin gas-exchange barrier.
  • Type II pneumocytes secrete surfactant and divide to replace type I cells.
  • The blood–air barrier is surfactant, type I cell, fused basal laminae, and capillary endothelium (0.1–1.5 µm).
  • Alveolar macrophages (dust cells) clear particles and debris.
  • The pleura is a serous membrane: visceral and parietal layers with a fluid-filled cavity.

18 · Skin

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Name the epidermal layers in order and the defining feature of each.
  2. Match each epidermal cell type to its origin and function.
  3. Identify the dermis, hypodermis, and each skin appendage.
  4. Explain the stages of skin repair, and recognize healing by first and second intention.

The core idea

Skin is a stratified, keratinizing, self-renewing barrier with appendages embedded in it. The epidermis is a factory that moves cells outward and turns them into keratin; the dermis provides mechanical and nutritional support; the appendages provide temperature control, sensation, and protection. Every fact in the chapter is a consequence of one of those three jobs.

Epidermis

Thickness: 75–150 µm in thin skin, 400–600 µm in thick skin. Full skin (epidermis + dermis) is about 4 mm on the back and 1.5 mm on the scalp. The epidermis is avascular — nutrients and O₂ arrive by diffusion from the dermal papillae. It turns over about every 15–30 days.

Melanocytes — about one per five or six keratinocytes (600–1200/mm² of thin skin), with processes reaching 10–30 keratinocytes. Tyrosinase converts tyrosine to DOPA and then to eumelanin (brown-black) or pheomelanin (red-yellow). Mature melanosomes are ellipsoid, 0.5 × 1 µm, and are transferred to keratinocytes, which hold more pigment than the cells that make it. The pigment sits over the nucleus and protects nuclear DNA from UV damage — the reason tanning exists and the reason melanoma risk tracks sun exposure. One melanocyte plus its keratinocytes is an epidermal-melanin unit. Melanocytes are about four times more numerous and active in thin skin than in thick palmar and plantar skin.

The melanin rule: densities of epidermal-melanin units are similar across populations; what differs is how rapidly melanin is made and how abundantly it accumulates in keratinocytes. Dark skin is not skin with more melanocytes.

Dermis

  • Papillary dermis — loose connective tissue forming dermal papillae that interdigitate with the epidermis; contains the subpapillary microvascular plexus, which sends capillary loops into the papillae.
  • Reticular dermis — dense irregular connective tissue with thick collagen bundles and elastic fibers; up to 4 mm thick on the back.

Dermal vasculature has two jobs: nutrition (two plexuses, subpapillary and deep) and thermoregulation through arteriovenous anastomoses between them.

Thick versus thin skin. Thick skin (palms, soles) has a thick stratum corneum, a stratum lucidum, and no hair follicles; it has many sweat glands. Thin skin covers most of the body, has hair follicles and sebaceous glands, and a thinner epidermis.

Subcutaneous Tissue

The hypodermis — loose connective tissue and adipose tissue (panniculus adiposus) binding skin loosely to underlying organs, so the skin can slide. It insulates, cushions, stores energy, and determines how a wound behaves and how an injection is absorbed.

Hair

Hairs are keratinized structures formed in hair follicles, epidermal invaginations extending into the dermis.

  • The hair bulb at the base contains the matrix (rapidly proliferating keratinocytes) and is invaginated by a vascular dermal papilla that supplies it.
  • The hair root has three concentric layers — medulla, cortex, cuticle — surrounded by internal and external root sheaths continuous with the epidermis, a glassy membrane (partly basal lamina), and a connective tissue sheath.
  • The arrector pili smooth muscle raises the hair; a root plexus provides sensation.
  • Density: about 600 hairs/cm² on the face and 60/cm² elsewhere.
  • Growth cycles: anagen (growth), catagen (involution), telogen (rest).

Nails

Hard keratin plates on the dorsal surface of the distal phalanx. The nail root lies under a skin fold whose cornified layer forms the cuticle (eponychium); keratinocytes proliferate in the matrix and differentiate into the nail plate, which slides forward over the nail bed (which does not contribute). The lunula is the visible part of the matrix.

Skin Glands

Sebaceous glands — holocrine: sebocytes accumulate lipid, die, and discharge sebum into the hair follicle (pilosebaceous unit). Absent from palms and soles.

Eccrine sweat glands — merocrine, coiled tubular, with a secretory portion of cuboidal cells and a duct of stratified cuboidal cells; abundant on palms and soles; watery sweat for thermoregulation.

Apocrine sweat glands — axillae and perineum, much wider lumens, develop after puberty, and secrete protein-rich sweat onto hair follicles; odor follows bacterial action.

Skin Repair

Repair runs through overlapping stages: a clot forms and platelets release growth factors; neutrophils and macrophages remove bacteria and debris (inflammation lasts 2–3 days); fibroblasts proliferate under growth-factor and macrophage signals and produce new collagen while capillaries grow into the site, forming granulation tissue; keratinocytes from the epidermal basal layer migrate beneath and through the clot and restore epithelial continuity; and excess collagen is left as scar. Epithelialization is complete before the collagen of the scar remodels.

Standard clinical terminology: healing by first intention — clean, apposed edges needing minimal granulation tissue; second intention — an open wound with abundant granulation tissue, wound contraction by myofibroblasts, and a larger scar. Burns are graded by depth: first-degree (epidermis), second-degree (into dermis, blistering), third-degree (full thickness, including appendages, so it cannot re-epithelialize).

CLINICAL — Psoriasis — T-cell-driven accelerated production and differentiation of keratinocytes, with thickening, scaling, and a defective barrier. Skin cancer — about one-third of all cancers begin in skin; most arise from basal or spinous cells (basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma), are diagnosed early, and are rarely lethal; incidence rises with fair skin and solar exposure. Melanoma — moles can show dysplasia and progress to malignant melanoma, whose cells penetrate the basal lamina and metastasize through blood and lymph. Merkel cell carcinoma is 40 times less common than melanoma but has twice its mortality. Albinism — defective tyrosinase or another melanin-pathway component; vitiligo — loss or reduced activity of melanocytes. Pemphigus (intercellular junctions) and bullous pemphigoid (dermal–epidermal junction) are the blistering diseases. Cystic fibrosis — salty sweat, because CFTR fails to reabsorb salt in the sweat duct.

MICRO — Count layers and look for appendages. Thick skin: five layers, thick keratin, no hair. Thin skin: four layers, hair follicles and sebaceous glands. A dark condensed nucleus at the basal layer with a clear halo suggests a melanocyte.

TIP — “Melanocytes are more numerous in dark skin” is false. Unit density is similar in all people; melanin production and transfer differ. Also remember that the nail bed makes no contribution to the nail plate — the matrix does.

MNEMONIC — “Come Let’s Get Some Beer” — Corneum, Lucidum, Granulosum, Spinosum, Basale (superficial to deep) — and remember lucidum exists only in thick skin.

  1. Trace filtrate from glomerulus to ureter, naming each segment and its epithelium.
  2. Explain the three layers of the filtration barrier and how each contributes to selectivity.
  3. Describe the juxtaglomerular apparatus and its role in blood-pressure control.

The core idea

The kidney is a countercurrent filtration and reabsorption machine built from millions of identical tubes. Each segment of the tubule has a distinct epithelium matched to a distinct transport job, so identifying the segment from its histology is the same as naming its function.

Kidneys

Each kidney is bean-shaped, about 12 cm long, 6 cm wide, and 2.5 cm thick in adults, with a convex lateral border and a concave medial hilum, which admits nerves and vessels and lets the ureter and lymphatics exit. Inside the hilum the ureter expands as the renal pelvis, which divides into two or three major calyces, then minor calyces.

  • Capsule → cortex (renal corpuscles + convoluted tubules) → medulla (loops of Henle, collecting ducts, vasa recta) organized into 8–15 pyramids (some descriptions give 8–12; both counts are in use).
  • Renal columns (of Bertin) — cortical tissue between pyramids.
  • Lobe = pyramid + overlying cortex; lobule = a medullary ray + the cortical tissue around it.
  • Papilla → minor calyx → major calyx → renal pelvis → ureter.
  • The cortex receives more than 10 times the blood flow of the medulla.

The nephron — about 1.25 million per kidney — begins with the renal corpuscle: the glomerulus (a tuft of fenestrated capillaries) inside the glomerular (Bowman) capsule.

  • Parietal layer — simple squamous epithelium, continuous with the proximal tubule.
  • Visceral layer — podocytes with primary processes and interdigitating pedicels that embrace the capillaries. Between pedicels are filtration slit pores, bridged by a zipper-like slit diaphragm of P-cadherin, nephrins, and the negatively charged glycoprotein podocalyxin.
  • Mesangial cells — between capillaries, covering surfaces not covered by podocytes; contractile (like pericytes) and produce external lamina.

The filtration barrier has three layers: fenestrated endothelium → glomerular basement membrane (GBM), about 330 nm thick, of type IV collagen and other proteins made by endothelial cells and podocytes → podocyte slit diaphragms. The barrier restricts macromolecules through its structural organization and molecular interactions; size selectivity is well established, while the independent contribution of charge selectivity is more complex than a simple two-filter model suggests.

Functional numbers to know:

  • Total filtration area ≈ 500 cm².
  • GFR ≈ 125 mL/min, or about 180 L/day.
  • Plasma volume ≈ 3 L, so the kidneys filter a volume equal to the whole plasma volume about 60 times a day.

Juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA) — at the vascular pole: juxtaglomerular (JG) cells in the afferent arteriole (modified smooth muscle, contain renin), the macula densa (a plaque of tall, closely packed cells in the distal straight tubule sensing NaCl), and extraglomerular mesangial (lacis) cells.

Reading a Renal Biopsy: A Step-by-Step Method

A renal section becomes easier to interpret when you separate location, compartment, and cellular detail rather than guessing a diagnosis from a single structure.

  1. Orient the specimen. Renal corpuscles establish that cortex is present. Medullary tissue lacks corpuscles and contains parallel loops, vessels and collecting ducts. A small biopsy may not sample every compartment.
  2. Examine the glomeruli. Locate the capillary tuft, Bowman’s space, parietal epithelium and mesangial regions. Ask whether the tuft is open and delicate or crowded by cells or matrix. Do not diagnose a specific glomerular disease from routine light microscopy alone.
  3. Compare tubules. Proximal tubules generally have strongly eosinophilic cytoplasm and a prominent apical brush border, so their lumina often look irregular or partly obscured. Distal tubules usually have clearer lumina and less conspicuous apical microvilli. Section angle and fixation can alter both appearances.
  4. Assess the interstitium and vessels. Note the amount of connective tissue between tubules, inflammatory cells if present, and arterial or arteriolar wall changes. These compartments can be as important as the glomeruli.
  5. Connect appearance to function. A damaged filtration barrier may permit albumin to enter urine; tubular injury can impair reabsorption and concentration. Neither finding establishes a diagnosis without clinical information and appropriate laboratory tests.

Microscopy caution. Standard renal-biopsy interpretation often integrates light microscopy with immunofluorescence and electron microscopy. For example, immune deposits and podocyte foot-process changes may not be characterized adequately on a routine H&E slide.

Blood Circulation

Renal artery → segmental → interlobar (between pyramids) → arcuate (at the corticomedullary junction) → interlobular (radiating into the cortex) → afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries in the cortex and vasa recta in the medulla. The efferent arteriole, not a venule, drains the glomerulus — an arrangement that keeps glomerular hydrostatic pressure high. Efferent arterioles of cortical glomeruli branch into a diffuse peritubular network; those of juxtamedullary glomeruli form the long hairpin vasa recta that run alongside the loops of Henle and participate in countercurrent exchange, helping preserve the medullary osmotic gradient. The loops of Henle, especially their thick ascending limbs, are central to countercurrent multiplication.

Size contrast: the straight proximal tubule is about 60 µm in outer diameter and narrows abruptly to about 30 µm in the thin limbs of the loop.

The visual contrast that answers most questions: the proximal tubule is eosinophilic with a ragged brush border and a star-shaped lumen; the distal tubule is pale with a clean lumen and no brush border.

Collecting ducts. Connecting tubules from several nephrons join to form cortical collecting ducts, which enter the medulla parallel to the loops and vasa recta and become larger with more columnar cells. The largest (papillary) ducts — ducts of Bellini — open into a minor calyx, where filtrate undergoes no further modification and is called urine.

Ureters, Bladder, & Urethra

Ureter — urothelium (transitional epithelium), a folded mucosa, and a thick muscularis that moves urine by peristalsis; inner longitudinal and outer circular layers, with a third layer added near the bladder. Bladder — urothelium, a highly folded mucosa, and the detrusor muscle in three poorly delineated interlacing layers. It holds 400–600 mL, with the urge to void at about 150–200 mL.

Urothelium is a specialized barrier, not just a stratified epithelium:

  • Umbrella cells — large superficial cells up to 100 µm in diameter, with uroplakin-containing membrane plaques, hinged to allow unfolding as the bladder fills.
  • The epithelium is organized in three layers: superficial umbrella cells, an intermediate layer of cuboidal or low columnar cells (one to several deep), and a basal layer. Emptying folds the mucosa and lets umbrella cells retract folded plaques; filling flattens the cells and thins the epithelium.
  • Tight junctions plus plaques make it an osmotic barrier against hypertonic urine.

Urethra — in both sexes lined initially by urothelium. In men there are three parts: prostatic (3–4 cm, urothelium), membranous (short, through the urogenital diaphragm; stratified or pseudostratified columnar), and penile (long; stratified columnar/pseudostratified proximally, stratified squamous distally). In women the urethra is 3–5 cm, urothelium proximally, then nonkeratinized stratified squamous; the middle part is surrounded by the external striated sphincter. The short female urethra explains the frequency of cystitis.

CLINICAL — Polycystic kidney disease — inherited cysts arising from nephron epithelium destroy cortical organization. Glomerulonephritis — immune-complex or anti-glomerular antibody injury; proteinuria means the filter has become permeable to protein. Diabetic glomerulosclerosis — thickening and loss of function of the GBM; the leading cause of end-stage kidney disease in the United States. Sickle cell nephropathy — sickling in the vasa recta causes papillary infarcts. Kidney stones — calculi form in the pelvis or calyces, usually from calcium oxalate/phosphate or uric acid. Pyelonephritis — bacteria reach the papilla from a calyx; cystitis — the commonest bladder problem, favored in women by the short urethra.

MICRO — Start at the renal corpuscle: the glomerulus is a tuft of capillaries inside a capsule. Then compare tubules: eosinophilic + brush border = proximal; pale + clean = distal; large pale cells in a row = collecting duct. The macula densa is the patch of tall cells on the distal tubule facing the vascular pole.

TIP — The proximal tubule is the most eosinophilic structure in the renal cortex, packed with mitochondria for active transport. Students often call it “basophilic” because the brush border looks fuzzy — the cytoplasm is pink.

The core idea

Endocrine glands release hormone into the blood, so their histology is organized around storage and release rather than ducts. The three patterns to recognize are cords (pituitary, adrenal, parathyroid), follicles (thyroid), and clusters (pancreatic islets). Each pattern is dictated by how the hormone is stored.

Pituitary Gland (Hypophysis)

Two embryologically distinct parts in one sella turcica.

Adenohypophysis (oral ectoderm — Rathke’s pouch). The pars distalis accounts for 75% of the adenohypophysis and is covered by a thin fibrous capsule:

Region Cells Hormones
Pars distalis Acidophils GH, prolactin
Basophils ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH
Chromophobes Inactive or degranulated
Pars intermedia Basophils, colloid cysts MSH (rudimentary in humans)
Pars tuberalis — Wraps the infundibulum

Reading the stain. Routine H&E divides the pars distalis into acidophils (pink), basophils (blue), and chromophobes (poorly stained, the least numerous). The modern names describe the hormone, not the color: somatotrophs (GH), lactotrophs (prolactin), corticotrophs (ACTH), thyrotrophs (TSH), and gonadotrophs (FSH, LH). Acidophils are the somatotrophs and lactotrophs; basophils are the corticotrophs, thyrotrophs, and gonadotrophs.

Neurohypophysis (neuroectoderm): the pars nervosa stores oxytocin and ADH (arginine vasopressin) — both 9-amino-acid peptides — synthesized in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus and carried down axons. In the axon terminals they are bound to 10-kDa carrier proteins, neurophysin I and II, and accumulate in dilatations called neurosecretory (Herring) bodies, visible as faintly eosinophilic structures. Pituicytes are the glial cells of the lobe. The neurohypophysis makes no hormone of its own — it stores and releases hypothalamic hormones.

Control: the hypothalamus releases releasing and inhibiting hormones into the hypophyseal portal system, carrying them directly to the pars distalis — which is why a tiny hypothalamic signal controls a large pituitary output.

Adrenal Glands

Two glands in one organ, with entirely different embryology and function:

Each adrenal gland is a flattened half-moon about 4–6 cm long, 1–2 cm wide, and 4–6 mm thick, weighing about 8 g in total. The fasciculata is the thickest zone, and its cells are pale and vacuolated because they are full of lipid droplets (cholesterol esters), tubular mitochondrial cristae, and smooth ER for steroid synthesis. The medulla is functionally a sympathetic ganglion without axons: chromaffin cells contain electron-dense granules 150–350 nm in diameter, storing catecholamines with Ca²⁺, ATP, and 49-kDa chromogranins. About 80% of the catecholamine released is epinephrine. Mnemonic for the zones: “GFR — Salt, Sugar, Sex” from outside in.

Scattered hormone-secreting cells throughout the body — gut enteroendocrine cells, respiratory neuroendocrine cells, and similar — sharing the ability to take up amine precursors (APUD cells). They are the reason a “pure” endocrine organ is not the only source of hormones: the gut alone produces more regulatory peptides than any single gland.

Thyroid Gland

  • Follicles of varying size filled with colloid (thyroglobulin), lined by a simple epithelium whose height ranges from squamous to low columnar.
  • Thyrocytes (follicular cells) — synthesize T4 and T3. Activity is read from the epithelium: active gland = more follicles of tall columnar cells with scalloped colloid; hypoactive gland = squamous cells with dense colloid.
  • Parafollicular (C) cells — large, pale cells in the follicular epithelium or between follicles, of neural crest origin, secreting calcitonin.
  • Folliculogenesis: thyroglobulin is synthesized, iodinated at the apical surface, stored as colloid, taken back by endocytosis, and digested to release T3/T4.

Parathyroid Glands

Four small ovoid glands, each about 3 × 6 mm, with a total weight of about 0.4 g, usually embedded in the thyroid capsule.

  • Principal (chief) cells — small, pale cells with round central nuclei, arranged in dense cords around capillaries; they secrete PTH.
  • Oxyphil cells — much larger, intensely eosinophilic cells packed with abnormally shaped mitochondria. They are degenerated derivatives of principal cells, some still showing low-level PTH synthesis, and they accumulate with age.
  • With age, adipocytes replace secretory cells and may constitute more than 50% of the gland.
  • Cords and capillaries only — no follicles.

Pineal Gland

A pine-cone-shaped organ about 5–8 × 3–5 mm, attached to the third ventricle and covered by pia mater.

  • Pinealocytes — modified neurons in cords and lobules, with pale nuclei and long processes ending near capillaries.
  • Corpora arenacea (brain sand) — concretions of calcium and magnesium salts formed on extracellular protein deposits; they appear in childhood and increase with age without affecting function. Their density is why the gland is radio-opaque and easy to identify on imaging.
  • Melatonin — a tryptophan derivative released in darkness under sympathetic control; it sets diurnal rhythms.
  • Interstitial (glial) cells lie between pinealocytes; unmyelinated sympathetic fibers run in the septa.

CLINICAL — Pituitary adenoma — benign tumors of acidophils or basophils; a somatotroph adenoma causes gigantism before epiphyseal closure and acromegaly afterwards. Diabetes insipidus — deficient ADH (mutation, tumor compression, or head trauma) produces copious dilute urine. Addison disease — autoimmune destruction of the adrenal cortex with loss of glucocorticoid, mineralocorticoid, and androgen output. Pheochromocytoma — a benign chromaffin-cell tumor that discharges catecholamines episodically, causing swings between hypertension and hypotension. Diabetes mellitus — type 1 from autoimmune β-cell loss; type 2 from insulin resistance with relative insulin deficiency. Goiter — dietary iodine deficiency lowers thyroid hormone output, so TSH rises and the gland enlarges. Graves disease — autoantibodies chronically stimulate follicular cells, producing hyperthyroidism. Hypoparathyroidism — low PTH leaves bone over-mineralized and causes tetanic muscle contraction; hyperparathyroidism — excess PTH drives osteoclasts and hypercalcemia. Pineal tumors are rare but may be benign or highly malignant.

MICRO — Three patterns, three organs. Follicles of colloid = thyroid. Cords of pale foamy cells under a capsule = adrenal cortex; the medulla is paler and central. Small pale chief cells in dense cords = parathyroid. Pituitary: acidophils and basophils in sheets (anterior); pale fibrillary tissue without epithelial cells (posterior).

TIP — The adrenal cortex has three zones and the medulla has none of the cortex’s function. Catecholamines are medullary.

The core idea

The testis does two jobs that require opposite conditions: producing sperm, which needs a temperature below core (hence the scrotum), and producing testosterone, which needs a rich blood supply. The architecture keeps them apart by separating the seminiferous tubules from the interstitium.

Testes

  • Tunica albuginea — thick capsule; the mediastinum testis sends septa dividing the gland into about 250 lobules, each holding 1–4 convoluted seminiferous tubules. Each testis has 250–1000 tubules; each tubule is 150–250 µm wide and 30–70 cm long, for a total of about 250 m per testis.
  • Tunica vaginalis — the serous sac the testis drags with it during descent: parietal layer lines the scrotum, visceral layer covers the albuginea.
  • Seminiferous tubules — lined by a stratified epithelium of germ cells plus Sertoli cells, on a basal lamina, surrounded by peritubular myoid cells.
  • Interstitium — Leydig cells, blood vessels, connective tissue.

Temperature is part of the anatomy. Sperm formation cannot proceed at 37 °C, so the scrotum holds the testis at about 34 °C by three mechanisms: evaporation of sweat from scrotal skin; a countercurrent heat exchanger in the spermatic cord, where the pampiniform plexus of veins cools the incoming testicular artery; and the dartos and cremaster muscles, which move the testis toward or away from the body.

Sertoli cells extend from base to lumen; their nuclei are pale, oval, and have a prominent nucleolus. They are not germ-line cells, and each one supports 30–50 developing germ cells. Functions:

  • Physical support and nutrition of developing germ cells.
  • Blood–testis barrier — tight junctions between adjacent Sertoli cells near the base, creating basal and adluminal compartments. This is why the immune system does not reject sperm.
  • Phagocytosis of residual bodies.
  • Secretion of androgen-binding protein (ABP), inhibin (a 39-kDa glycoprotein that suppresses FSH), Müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS, 140 kDa, which regresses the paramesonephric ducts in the fetus), transferrin, and fluid that carries sperm out of the testis.
  • Response to FSH — which acts on Sertoli cells, not on germ cells.

Spermatogenesis: spermatogonia → primary spermatocyte → secondary spermatocyte → spermatid → spermatozoon. Meiosis I halves the chromosome number and meiosis II separates chromatids, giving four spermatids per primary spermatocyte. The germ cells do not divide in isolation: cytokinesis is incomplete, so clones stay joined by cytoplasmic bridges — a syncytium that lets a haploid cell use products of the full diploid genome, including genes on X or Y. The whole sequence from the last spermatogonial mitosis to spermatid formation takes about 2 months, and the testis produces about 200 million sperm per day.

Spermiogenesis is the metamorphosis of a round spermatid into a sperm, and it is conventionally divided into four phases:

  1. Golgi phase — proacrosomal vesicles from the Golgi coalesce into a single acrosomal cap; centrioles migrate to the opposite pole and one becomes the basal body of the flagellum.
  2. Cap phase — the acrosome spreads over about half the nucleus. It is a specialized lysosome containing hyaluronidase and the trypsin-like protease acrosin.
  3. Acrosome phase — the nucleus condenses and elongates, its histones replaced by protamines; a microtubule manchette shapes the head; mitochondria gather around the proximal flagellum to form the middle piece.
  4. Maturation phase — excess cytoplasm is shed as a residual body, intercellular bridges are lost, and the sperm is released into the lumen.

Because the barrier makes the adluminal compartment immunologically privileged, sperm antigens are not tolerized — which is why vasectomy can produce anti-sperm antibodies.

Leydig (interstitial) cells — large, polygonal, eosinophilic cells with central nuclei, abundant SER, mitochondria, and lipid droplets, lying singly or in small groups near capillaries. They secrete testosterone under LH, also called interstitial cell-stimulating hormone (ICSH) — the same hormone named LH in females. Fetal Leydig cells are active in the third and fourth months of pregnancy, then become quiescent until puberty.

Intratesticular Ducts

Sperm leave each tubule through a short straight tubule, lined at first only by Sertoli cells and then by simple cuboidal epithelium. It enters the rete testis, a labyrinth of cuboidal-lined channels in the mediastinum testis. From there 10–20 efferent ductules carry sperm to the head of the epididymis. Their epithelium has a characteristic scalloped profile in section — patches of absorptive, nonciliated cuboidal cells with microvilli alternating with patches of taller ciliated cells. Absorption of Sertoli-cell fluid plus ciliary beating creates the flow that moves sperm out of the testis.

Accessory Glands

  • Seminal vesicles — paired, highly tortuous tubes about 15 cm long, with thin complex mucosal folds and inner circular plus outer longitudinal smooth muscle. Their viscid yellowish secretion makes up about 70% of the ejaculate and supplies fructose (sperm energy), inositol, citrate, prostaglandins (which stimulate the female tract), and fibrinogen (which lets semen coagulate).
  • Prostate — a 20-g organ of 30–50 branched tubuloacinar glands in a dense fibromuscular stroma; corpora amylacea are small spherical concretions (up to 2 mm, partly calcified, made of glycoproteins and keratan sulfate) that become more numerous with age but have no clinical significance. The gland has three zones around the urethra:
    • Peripheral zone — about 70% of the tissue, with the longest ducts; the site of most carcinomas.
    • Central zone — about 25%, with submucosal glands.
    • Transition zone — about 5%, with mucosal glands around the upper urethra; the site of benign prostatic hyperplasia.
  • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) — a 34-kDa serine protease that liquefies the coagulated ejaculate; it leaks into blood, so a raised serum PSA points to carcinoma or inflammation.
  • Bulbourethral (Cowper) glands — paired, 3–5 mm mucous glands in the urogenital diaphragm; their clear secretion lubricates the urethra before ejaculation.

Penis

Three erectile bodies: two dorsal corpora cavernosa and one ventral corpus spongiosum containing the urethra and expanding distally as the glans. Each is a sponge of endothelium-lined cavernous spaces separated by trabeculae of smooth muscle and connective tissue, with a dense tunica albuginea around the corpora cavernosa. Most of the penile urethra is pseudostratified columnar, becoming stratified squamous in the glans.

Erection is a vascular event with a molecular explanation worth memorizing: parasympathetic acetylcholine makes the endothelium release nitric oxide, which activates guanylate cyclase and raises cGMP in smooth muscle; the muscle relaxes, helicine arteries dilate, cavernous spaces fill, and the swollen corpora compress the dorsal veins against the tunica albuginea. Sildenafil blocks the phosphodiesterase that degrades cGMP and therefore sustains an erection. Sympathetic discharge at ejaculation constricts the helicine arteries and trabecular muscle, emptying the spaces.

CLINICAL — Cryptorchidism — failure of descent, present in about 4% of male neonates; most descend in the first year, and bilateral cases cause infertility if not corrected by 2–3 years. An undescended testis is exposed to core temperature, so spermatogenesis fails and malignancy risk rises. Hydrocele — serous fluid in the tunica vaginalis, the commonest cause of scrotal swelling. Varicocele — dilation of the pampiniform plexus raises testicular temperature and impairs spermatogenesis. Prostatic carcinoma — arises in the peripheral zone, is palpable on rectal examination, and metastasizes to bone. BPH — nodular hyperplasia of the transition zone, compressing the urethra. Testicular tumors — 95% are germ-cell tumors, appearing after puberty and more often in untreated cryptorchidism; a painless mass is malignant until proven otherwise.

MICRO — Identify the two compartments instantly. Inside the tubule: Sertoli cells (pale, tall) and germ cells at various stages. Outside the tubule: Leydig cells (eosinophilic, polygonal, in clusters). If asked for the cell that forms the blood–testis barrier, the answer is Sertoli.

TIP — FSH acts on Sertoli cells; LH acts on Leydig cells. One sentence, and the two are hard to confuse.

  1. Sequence follicular development from primordial follicle to corpus albicans.
  2. Explain the two-cell, two-gonadotropin mechanism of estrogen production.
  3. Correlate ovarian and endometrial cycles and identify each phase histologically.

The core idea

The ovary and the endometrium are rebuilt through a monthly cycle. Every histological feature of the ovary, tube, uterus, and vagina is a stage in one of two cycles — ovarian (follicle to corpus luteum) and endometrial (proliferative to secretory) — locked together by the same four hormones.

Ovaries

Each ovary is an almond-shaped body about 3 cm long, 1.5 cm wide, and 1 cm thick.

  • Germinal (surface) epithelium — simple cuboidal mesothelium (a misnomer; it does not produce germ cells).
  • Tunica albuginea — dense connective tissue.
  • Cortex — follicles at all stages, plus corpus luteum and corpus albicans.
  • Medulla — loose connective tissue with the vessels entering at the hilum. Cortex and medulla have no sharp border.

The oocyte supply is fixed before birth and only falls. Primordial germ cells reach the gonads in the first month; oogonia peak at about 7 million in the fifth month of gestation. About 2–2.5 million primordial follicles are present at birth and about 400,000 at puberty; only about 450 are ovulated in a lifetime, and the rest undergo atresia. Menopause arrives when the reserve falls below about 1000 follicles.

The corpus luteum is a temporary gland with two cell populations:

  • Granulosa-lutein cells — the old granulosa cells; they enlarge to 20–35 µm without dividing, make up about 80% of the gland, and secrete progesterone (their SER expands, and they retain some aromatase).
  • Theca-lutein cells — from the theca interna; about half the size, darker staining, lying in the peripheral folds, and secreting androstenedione and estrogens.

The LH surge sustains progesterone secretion for 10–12 days. (A common error is to reverse the two populations: the granulosa-lutein cells make progesterone, and the theca-lutein cells make androgens and estrogens.) Without pregnancy, both populations undergo apoptosis, macrophages clear the debris, and fibroblasts lay down a collagen scar — the corpus albicans. Note that involution is not atresia: atresia is the death of an unovulated follicle.

The zona pellucida is a 5–10 µm glycoprotein coat between the oocyte and the granulosa cells, secreted by the oocyte. Its glycoproteins ZP1, ZP2, and ZP3, all made by the oocyte, form the sperm-binding receptor; binding of a capacitated sperm to this coat begins the acrosomal reaction. In humans the family also includes ZP4.

Ovulation, step by step. The LH surge triggers: completion of meiosis I with extrusion of the first polar body; granulosa production of fluid rich in prostaglandins, proteoglycans, and proteases; hyaluronan release that loosens the cumulus; activation of plasmin, which digests collagen at the stigma (the ischemic bulge on the ovarian surface); and contraction of theca externa smooth muscle. The follicle ruptures, releasing the secondary oocyte, arrested in metaphase II, wrapped in the corona radiata — viable for about 24 hours. If a sperm arrives, the oocyte completes meiosis II and emits the second polar body; the two pronuclei fuse to form the zygote.

A mature follicle takes about 90 days to develop from a primordial follicle, and only one per cycle reaches that stage while its neighbors become atretic.

Two-cell, two-gonadotropin theory: LH stimulates theca interna cells to make androstenedione; it crosses the basement membrane (the granulosa layer is avascular) and FSH stimulates granulosa cells to aromatize it to estradiol. The estrogen returns to the theca capillaries and reaches the whole body. This division of labor explains why both gonadotropins are needed for normal estrogen production — and why the granulosa layer must stay avascular.

Uterine Tubes

About 10–12 cm long, in four parts — infundibulum with fimbriae → ampulla (longest; fertilization happens here) → isthmus → intramural portion. The mucosa has complex branching folds, tallest in the ampulla, and is lined by ciliated columnar cells that sweep fluid toward the uterus and peg (secretory) cells that make the nutritive fluid. The muscularis has interwoven circular and longitudinal layers and provides most of the transport — women with immotile cilia still carry the embryo to the uterus.

Major Events of Fertilization

Fertilization normally occurs in the ampulla, and only a few hundred of the millions of ejaculated sperm reach it. The sequence:

  1. Capacitation in the female tract makes sperm able to fertilize.
  2. The sperm releases hyaluronidase to pass between the corona radiata cells.
  3. At the zona, binding to the ZP1–ZP3 complex triggers the acrosomal reaction: acrosin and other proteases digest a path through the zona.
  4. The first sperm to cross fuses with the oolemma. Membrane depolarization and a Ca²⁺ wave trigger the cortical reaction — proteases from cortical granules modify the zona glycoproteins and convert the zona into the impenetrable perivitelline barrier, a permanent block to polyspermy.
  5. The oocyte completes meiosis II, emits the second polar body, and forms the female pronucleus; the sperm head decondenses into the male pronucleus. Fusion gives the diploid zygote.

Cleavage follows as the embryo travels to the uterus — about 5 days from fertilization to the blastocyst in the uterine cavity.

Uterus

The uterus is pear-shaped: body, fundus, isthmus, and cervix, with the cervical canal opening at the internal and external os.

  • Perimetrium (serosa), myometrium (thick smooth muscle in interlacing bundles), endometrium.
  • Endometrium — stratum functionalis (shed each month) and stratum basalis (retained, regenerates it). The basal layer has a distinctive stroma: fibroblasts, ground substance, fine type III collagen, and little or no adipose tissue. Spiral arteries supply the functionalis and constrict to cause shedding.

Thickness is a second discriminator. At the end of the proliferative phase the endometrium is 2–3 mm; in the secretory phase it reaches 5–6 mm, and the functional layer becomes about four times thicker than the basal layer.

How menstruation happens. Progesterone and estrogen fall 8–10 days after ovulation. The spiral arteries contract and relax intermittently, then constrict under local prostaglandins, producing ischemia. Hypoxic cells release cytokines that attract leukocytes, whose collagenases and matrix metalloproteinases digest the matrix. The functional layer — epithelium, most of each gland, stroma, and blood-filled lacunae — separates from the basal layer and is shed. The basal layer, which does not depend on the spiral arteries, survives and regenerates the next functionalis under estrogen.

Embryonic Implantation, Decidua, & the Placenta

The blastocyst reaches the uterine cavity about 5 days after fertilization and floats in the glandular secretion for about 2 days. Implantation (nidation) takes about 3 days and is complete by about day 9: trophoblast receptors bind glycoproteins on the endometrial epithelium, the syncytiotrophoblast digests the stroma, and the embryo embeds. The trophoblast has two layers — cytotrophoblast (mitotic, inner) and syncytiotrophoblast (invasive, multinucleated, outer, and the source of hCG).

Chorionic villi project into maternal blood in the intervillous space and develop in three stages: primary villi (cytotrophoblast covered by syncytiotrophoblast) about 2 days after implantation; secondary villi (mesenchymal cores) about day 15; tertiary villi (with capillary loops) a few days later. By the end of the first month there are thousands of tertiary villi.

Decidua — the modified endometrium of pregnancy: decidua basalis (under the embryo, forms the maternal placenta), decidua capsularis (over it), decidua parietalis (the rest).

Placental hormones: hCG (maintains the corpus luteum early; the basis of pregnancy tests), a lactogen, relaxin, growth factors, progesterone, and estrogen. hCG keeps the corpus luteum of pregnancy alive for 4–5 months, by which time the placenta itself makes enough progesterone.

Cervix

The cervix is the lower cylindrical part of the uterus and differs from the body: its mucosa is 2–3 mm thick, has no spiral arteries, and does not shed with the cycle.

  • Endocervix — simple columnar mucus-secreting cells with large branched cervical glands.
  • Ectocervix — stratified squamous nonkeratinized epithelium, continuous with the vagina.
  • Transformation zone — the area just outside the external os where columnar epithelium is replaced by squamous; the site of HPV-related dysplasia and the target of the Pap smear.

Cervical mucus changes with hormones: watery and abundant at ovulation (helps sperm), viscous in the luteal phase (hinders them), and a thick mucus plug in pregnancy. Before birth, effacement dissolves collagen so the cervix softens and dilates.

Vagina

Stratified squamous nonkeratinized epithelium 150–200 µm thick and rich in glycogen, with no glands (lubrication comes from cervical and Bartholin glands) and a lamina propria with elastic fibers and venous plexuses. Lactobacilli metabolize glycogen to lactic acid, keeping the pH low and protecting against pathogens.

External Genitalia

The vulva: the labia majora (skin with hair and sebaceous glands, containing the homologue of the scrotum), labia minora (thin skin without hair, with sebaceous glands), the clitoris (erectile tissue homologous to the penis), and the vestibule containing the urethral and vaginal openings and the openings of the greater vestibular (Bartholin) glands, which provide lubrication.

Mammary Glands

A modified apocrine sweat gland whose functional unit is the terminal duct lobular unit (TDLU).

  • Non-lactating — mostly dense connective tissue and fat with small ducts and lobules.
  • Pregnancy — lobules proliferate under estrogen and progesterone.
  • Lactation — alveoli distend with secretion; protein is released by exocytosis and lipid droplets by apocrine secretion, so milk fat is wrapped in apical plasma membrane. Prolactin drives synthesis, oxytocin the ejection reflex.
  • Duct system — intralobular → interlobular → lactiferous ducts → lactiferous sinuses beneath the areola → nipple.

Hormonal control: estrogen for ductal growth, progesterone for lobuloalveolar development, prolactin for milk synthesis, oxytocin for milk ejection.

CLINICAL — Polycystic ovary syndrome — multiple arrested antral follicles, anovulation, hyperandrogenism. Endometriosis — functional endometrial tissue outside the uterus, bleeding cyclically. Leiomyoma (fibroid) — a benign smooth-muscle tumor of the myometrium. Endometrial carcinoma — unopposed estrogen, with atypical hyperplasia. Cervical carcinoma — HPV in the transformation zone. Ectopic pregnancy — implantation outside the uterine cavity, usually in the ampulla. Breast carcinoma — arises from ductal or lobular epithelium; the terminal duct lobular unit is the functional unit to remember.

MICRO — Place the ovary in the cycle by the dominant structure: many small primordial follicles = inactive/early; a large antral follicle = late follicular; a corpus luteum with folded, pale, luteinized cells = secretory phase; a hyaline scar = post-ovulatory. In the uterus, look at the glands, not the stroma.

MNEMONIC — “Proliferative = Pencil-thin glands; Secretory = Saw-tooth and swollen.”

COMMON ERROR — Some summaries reverse the two lutein cell populations, giving estrogen to the granulosa-lutein cells. The correct assignment, and the one that follows from the two-cell mechanism, is progesterone from the granulosa-lutein cells and estrogens from the theca-lutein cells.

TIP — The corpus luteum secretes progesterone, and it is maintained by LH (and, in pregnancy, by hCG). Do not say estrogen maintains the corpus luteum.

Learning objectives

  3. Describe the layers of the retina and the path of a light signal.

The core idea

Both organs convert a physical signal into a nerve impulse through a specialized epithelium — the retina for light, the organ of Corti for sound. Their histology is therefore about transduction, not merely structure.

Numbers to anchor the chapter: the adult eyeball is about 22 mm in diameter; the sclera is 0.5 mm thick (1 mm posteriorly); the cornea is the anterior one-sixth and is avascular; the retina has nine neural layers plus a pigmented layer; the cochlea is about 35 mm long with 2¾ turns.

Eyes: The Photoreceptor System

Three tunics:

The cornea has five layers, and the order is a standard question:

  1. Corneal epithelium — stratified squamous, nonkeratinized, 5–6 cells thick, about 10% of corneal thickness; its stem cells sit in the limbus, and it has one of the richest sensory innervations in the body.
  2. Bowman’s membrane — a thick (8–10 µm) basement membrane.
  3. Stroma — about 90% of the thickness, some 60 layers of type I collagen at right angles to each other, with flattened keratocytes between them; avascular.
  4. Descemet’s membrane — the thick basement membrane of the endothelium.
  5. Corneal endothelium — simple squamous, the most metabolically active cells of the cornea; their Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pumps control stromal hydration and therefore transparency.

The cornea is transparent because it is avascular and its collagen is regularly arranged. When it becomes edematous or scarred, it loses transparency — the anatomical explanation for corneal blindness.

  • Choroid — pigmented, vascular; two layers: the inner choroidocapillary lamina (nourishes the outer retina) and Bruch’s membrane, an elastin sheet between two collagen layers, next to the retinal pigment epithelium.
  • Ciliary body — ciliary muscle (fibers in three complementary orientations; accommodation) and numerous ciliary processes covered by a bilaminar epithelium: a pigmented layer next to the stroma and a nonpigmented superficial layer. The nonpigmented cells pump aqueous humor and also secrete the fibrillin microfibrils of the ciliary zonule that suspends the lens.
  • Iris — a stroma with melanocytes and, posteriorly, two pigmented epithelial layers. The anterior surface has no epithelium, only fibroblasts and melanocytes. The dilator pupillae is a layer of myoepithelial cells (sympathetic); the sphincter pupillae is a circular smooth-muscle bundle near the pupil (parasympathetic).

Aqueous humor is secreted by the ciliary processes into the posterior chamber, flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber, and drains at the iridocorneal angle through the trabecular meshwork into the scleral venous sinus (canal of Schlemm), then into scleral veins. It has the ions of plasma but almost no protein. Blockage raises intraocular pressure — glaucoma; a narrow angle that closes when the pupil dilates gives angle-closure glaucoma.

The lens: capsule (10–20 µm, type IV collagen and proteoglycans) → subcapsular epithelium (simple cuboidal, anterior surface only) → lens fibers (7–10 mm long, 2 × 8 µm in cross-section, packed with crystallins, their organelles and nuclei removed by autophagy). New fibers are added at the equator throughout life, so the lens grows and its nucleus becomes denser. It is avascular and suspended by the zonule.

Accommodation: at rest the ciliary muscle is relaxed, the zonule is taut, and the lens is flat for distant vision. To focus close, the ciliary muscle contracts, the ciliary body moves forward, tension on the zonule falls, and the lens becomes rounder. Loss of lens elasticity in the fourth decade is presbyopia; loss of transparency is cataract.

The retina — the neural retina has nine layers, and with the pigment epithelium there are ten. From the vitreous inward-outward (following the path of light): inner limiting membrane → nerve fiber layer → ganglion cell layer → inner plexiform layer → inner nuclear layer → outer plexiform layer → outer nuclear layer → outer limiting layer → rod and cone layer → pigmented layer.

Layer Contents
Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) Cuboidal, pigmented; absorbs stray light, forms the blood–retina barrier with tight junctions, phagocytoses shed rod discs, regenerates 11-cis-retinal, and secretes trophic factors
Photoreceptor layer Rods (rhodopsin, dim light, ~92 million) and cones (iodopsin, color, ~4.6 million). Rod discs are membrane stacks; cone outer segments are conical.
Outer nuclear layer Photoreceptor nuclei
Inner nuclear layer Bipolar, horizontal, amacrine, and Müller cell nuclei
Ganglion cell layer Ganglion cells whose axons form the optic nerve

Rods versus cones at the level of the disc — a classic distinction: in cones the membranous discs of the outer segment are continuous with the plasma membrane; in rods they are separate, pinched-off discs. A single rod contains about one billion rhodopsin molecules, and each day it loses and replaces about 90 discs in a cycle that takes 10 days.

Phototransduction in one paragraph. In darkness, cGMP keeps cGMP-gated Na⁺ channels open, so the photoreceptor is depolarized and steadily releases glutamate. A photon isomerizes 11-cis-retinal to all-trans-retinal within a picosecond; the opsin changes shape and activates transducin, a heterotrimeric G protein whose α subunit stimulates phosphodiesterase to hydrolyze cGMP. The channels close, the cell hyperpolarizes, and transmitter release falls — which depolarizes the bipolar cells and sends signals to the ganglion cells. The free all-trans-retinal is carried to the RPE, re-isomerized to 11-cis, and returned; this bleaching cycle takes a minute or more and underlies slow dark adaptation.

The retina is inverted: light passes through the ganglion cell and inner layers before reaching the photoreceptors, which sit against the pigment epithelium. The fovea centralis, within the macula lutea, has an exceptionally high central cone density. Its central foveola is rod-free, while a few rods can occur toward the peripheral fovea; inner retinal layers are displaced laterally, improving light access to the central cones. That is why acuity is highest there, but only in good light. The optic disc is the blind spot: ganglion-cell axons converge there to form the optic nerve, and photoreceptors are absent.

Müller cells are the principal glia — modified astrocytes whose cell bodies lie in the inner nuclear layer and whose processes span the whole retina. They provide trophic support, buffer ions and water, regulate blood flow, form the inner limiting membrane, and join photoreceptors by zonulae adherentes to form the outer limiting layer.

Ears: The Vestibuloauditory System

External ear — the auricle is a plate of elastic cartilage covered by tightly adherent skin; the external acoustic meatus is lined by skin with hairs, sebaceous glands, and modified apocrine ceruminous glands. Their product, cerumen, contains proteins, saturated fatty acids, and shed keratinocytes and is antimicrobial. Elastic cartilage supports the outer third of the meatus; the temporal bone encloses the inner part. The tympanic membrane is fibroelastic connective tissue covered by epidermis outside and simple cuboidal mucosa inside.

Middle ear — an air-filled tympanic cavity lined by simple cuboidal epithelium, containing the ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) linked by synovial joints, with two small muscles (tensor tympani on the malleus, stapedius on the stapes) that damp loud sounds. The auditory (eustachian) tube connects to the pharynx and equalizes pressure; the oval window receives the stapes and the round window relieves the pressure wave.

Inner ear — bony labyrinth containing the membranous labyrinth:

  • Cochlea — a 35-mm canal with 2¾ turns around a bony core, the modiolus. It has three compartments: scala vestibuli (perilymph) above, scala media / cochlear duct (endolymph) in the middle, and scala tympani (perilymph) below. Vestibuli and tympani communicate at the apex through the helicotrema. The vestibular (Reissner) membrane separates vestibuli from media with tight junctions that keep the two fluids apart; the basilar membrane separates media from tympani.
  • The organ of Corti sits on the basilar membrane: about 12,000 outer hair cells in three rows (five near the apex) with V-shaped stereocilia bundles, and a single row of inner hair cells with a linear bundle. The inner row is far more heavily innervated. Phalangeal cells support the hair cells and, with them, form the apical plate; pillar cells enclose the stiff inner tunnel. The tallest stereocilia of the outer cells are embedded in the acellular tectorial membrane (collagens II, V, and IX, proteoglycans, and tectorin).
  • The stria vascularis in the lateral wall secretes endolymph, pumping K⁺ into the lumen.
  • Vestibule — utricle and saccule, each with a macula containing hair cells and otoliths (gravity and linear acceleration).
  • Semicircular canals — cristae ampullares with hair cells in a cupula (rotational acceleration).

Endolymph fills the membranous labyrinth: K⁺ about 150 mM, Na⁺ about 16 mM — like intracellular fluid, and the source of the driving force for transduction. Perilymph fills the bony labyrinth and resembles cerebrospinal fluid. Perilymph drains through the perilymphatic duct to the subarachnoid space; endolymph drains through the endolymphatic duct to dural venous sinuses.

Transduction is the same in the macula, crista, and organ of Corti: bending the stereocilia toward the tallest opens mechanically gated K⁺ channels, K⁺ enters, the cell depolarizes, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open at the base, and transmitter release rises. Bending the other way closes the channels and silences the cell.

CLINICAL — Glaucoma — raised intraocular pressure from impaired drainage through the trabecular meshwork, damaging the optic nerve. Cataract — lens opacity from crystallin denaturation. Age-related macular degeneration — degeneration of the RPE and photoreceptors at the macula. Retinal detachment — separation of the neural retina from the RPE, which starves the photoreceptors. Diabetic retinopathy — microvascular damage with hemorrhage and neovascularization. Otosclerosis — ossification of the stapes footplate, producing conductive deafness. Presbycusis — age-related loss of hair cells and neurons. Ménière disease — endolymphatic hydrops with vertigo, tinnitus, and fluctuating deafness.

Eyelids and tear film — the inner surface of the eyelid and the anterior sclera are covered by conjunctiva, a stratified columnar epithelium with goblet cells that is continuous with the corneal epithelium. Lacrimal glands produce the tear film, which drains through the lacrimal apparatus into the nasal cavity.

MICRO — The cornea is identified by five layers and a regular, avascular stroma. The retina by its ten layers with a pigmented layer at the outer border and ganglion cells at the inner border. The organ of Corti by the tunnel of Corti between inner and outer hair cells on the basilar membrane, with the tectorial membrane above.

TIP — Rods detect dim light, cones detect color and detail; the central foveola contains only cones, helping explain peak visual acuity; rod density increases outside the foveal center.

The 60-second organ identification algorithm

Step 1 — Is there a lumen? - No obvious large lumen → look for follicles, cords, sinusoids, lobules or lymphoid tissue. Small ducts and vessels may still be present; lack of a visible lumen does not establish that an organ is solid. - Yes, a lumen → go to Step 2.

Step 2 — What is the epithelium? - Stratified squamous → esophagus, oral cavity, vagina, anal canal, epidermis (no lumen). - Simple columnar epithelium → assess surface architecture: gastric pits in stomach; villi and intestinal crypts in small intestine; straight crypts without villi in colon; folds without true intestinal crypts in gallbladder or uterine tube. - Pseudostratified ciliated → respiratory tract. Pseudostratified with stereocilia → epididymis, vas deferens. - Transitional → ureter, bladder, renal pelvis. - Simple cuboidal/columnar tubules → kidney, salivary ducts, epididymis.

Step 3 — What surrounds it? - Cartilage rings → trachea. Cartilage plates → bronchus. No cartilage → bronchiole. - Three muscle layers → stomach. Taeniae coli → colon. Thick muscle wall → vas deferens, ureter. - Serosa indicates a mesothelial covering; adventitia indicates attachment to surrounding connective tissue. How to Read an Unfamiliar Histology Slide Without Guessing

First pass: orientation (low power). Decide whether you are looking at a hollow organ, a solid organ, a sheet of tissue, or a mixed specimen. Look for the boundary of the sample, connective-tissue septa, major vessels, a capsule if present, and any recognizable lumen. A single section may cut an organ obliquely, and a biopsy may omit diagnostic landmarks.

Second pass: tissue architecture (medium power). Identify epithelial layers, glands, follicles, ducts, tubules, muscle bundles, vascular channels, lymphoid aggregates and extracellular matrix. Ask which components repeat and how they are organized. For hollow organs, follow the wall from lumen outward; for solid organs, identify functional units rather than assuming every organ has a cortex and medulla.

Third pass: cellular evidence (high power). Compare nuclear shape, cytoplasmic staining, apical specialization, granules, extracellular fibers and cell arrangement. Recognize artifacts: folds may mimic thick epithelium, shrinkage can create false spaces, and tangential sections can make one layer look like several.

Fourth pass: test alternatives. State at least two observations that support your identification and one finding that would favor a competing organ. For example, both small intestine and colon have intestinal crypts, but villi strongly favor small intestine; Brunner glands in the submucosa support duodenum.

Fifth pass: communicate uncertainty. If the distinguishing feature is absent, identify the tissue at the highest defensible level (for example, intestinal mucosa rather than ileum). A confident but unsupported organ label is less useful than an accurate description.

Practice case A. You see simple columnar epithelium with many goblet cells, closely packed straight crypts and no villi. What is the best identification? Answer: colonic-type mucosa. The absence of villi and abundance of goblet-rich straight crypts favor large intestine; the precise segment may require additional landmarks.

Practice case B. You see a tube lined by urothelium with mucosal folds and a thick, irregular smooth-muscle wall. What alternatives remain? Answer: ureter and urinary bladder. A relatively small, stellate lumen and organized muscular layers favor ureter; a broad lumen and interlacing detrusor bundles favor bladder, but section orientation matters.

Practice case C. You see densely packed tubules and scattered round capillary tufts surrounded by a narrow space. What tissue is present? Answer: renal cortex, because renal corpuscles are present. Proximal and distal tubules should then be distinguished by their apical brush borders and staining patterns.

Retrieval rule: cover each answer and justify your choice from the visual evidence before reading the explanation. The purpose is to learn an observable decision process, not to memorize an organ name from a single photograph.

Eight common errors

  1. Saying the osteoclast is mesenchymal. It is hematopoietic.

Appendix D · An Original Schematic Pattern Atlas

How to use this atlas. These nine illustrations were drawn specifically for this book from simplified geometric shapes. They are not photomicrographs, are not drawn to scale, and do not show every cell or tissue boundary. Use each plate to learn the relationships among structures, then verify those relationships with real H&E, special-stain, or virtual-microscopy sections. Color here is instructional and is not a claim about staining.

Plate 1 — Epithelial organization

Look for: epithelial thickness, the number of true cell layers, cell shape at the free surface, and the position of the basal boundary. Microscope challenge: find pseudostratified respiratory epithelium and explain why its nuclei at different heights do not make it genuinely stratified.

Plate 2 — Cartilage matrix

Look for: chondrocytes in lacunae and the matrix between them. Smooth matrix favors hyaline cartilage; a branching elastic-fiber network supports elastic cartilage; prominent collagen bundles and aligned chondrocytes favor fibrocartilage. Important exception: articular hyaline cartilage lacks a perichondrium.

Plate 3 — Blood and lymphatic vessels

Look for: the thickness and composition of the wall, not just the apparent width of the lumen. An initial lymphatic capillary has a specialized, thin endothelial wall; larger collecting lymphatics have additional supporting tissue and valves. Microscope challenge: classify an artery–vein pair in one section and state two independent observations.

Plate 4 — Intestinal segments

Look for: villi, crypts and regional markers. Brunner glands in the submucosa support duodenum; aggregated Peyer patches support ileum; straight goblet-cell-rich crypts without villi favor colon. Sampling caveat: a mucosal biopsy may not contain the submucosa needed to identify Brunner glands.

Plate 5 — Renal cortex

Look for: a glomerular tuft enclosed by Bowman’s capsule and the surrounding tubular profiles. Proximal convoluted tubules tend to be strongly eosinophilic with an apical brush border and a less distinct lumen; distal profiles usually have a clearer lumen. Microscope challenge: explain why renal corpuscles establish cortical rather than medullary tissue.

Plate 6 — Liver architecture

Look for: a central vein, hepatocyte plates and sinusoids, with portal structures at the periphery of a classical lobule. Flow distinction: blood from portal venules and hepatic arterioles enters sinusoids and reaches a central vein; bile flows in the opposite direction toward bile ducts.

Plate 7 — Lymphoid organs

Look for: cortical–medullary organization and Hassall corpuscles in thymus; follicles, subcapsular sinus and medullary cords in lymph node; white pulp in a background of red pulp in spleen. Microscope challenge: identify a genuine thymic medulla without assuming that every round pink structure is a Hassall corpuscle.

Plate 8 — Bone and cartilage

Look for: osteonal concentric lamellae and central canals in compact bone, an interconnecting trabecular network in spongy bone, and chondrocyte lacunae in hyaline cartilage. Do not confuse: a cell in a bone lacuna is an osteocyte; an osteoclast is a larger resorbing cell on a bone surface.

Plate 9 — Skin

Look for: epidermal thickness, the stratum corneum and skin appendages. Hair follicles occur in thin skin, not thick palmar or plantar skin; eccrine sweat glands are present in both. Microscope challenge: distinguish a sweat-gland duct from the secretory coil by lumen size and epithelial appearance.

Final identification rule: these diagrams teach where to look, not what every real section must resemble. Confirm identifications using tissue relationships, stain, section orientation, and more than one diagnostic landmark.
