### The 60-second organ identification algorithm

**Step 1 — Is there a lumen?**
- **No obvious large lumen** → look for follicles, cords, sinusoids, lobules or lymphoid tissue. Small ducts and vessels may still be present; lack of a visible lumen does not establish that an organ is solid.
- **Yes, a lumen** → go to Step 2.

**Step 2 — What is the epithelium?**
- **Stratified squamous** → esophagus, oral cavity, vagina, anal canal, epidermis (no lumen).
- **Simple columnar epithelium** → assess surface architecture: gastric pits in stomach; villi and intestinal crypts in small intestine; straight crypts without villi in colon; folds without true intestinal crypts in gallbladder or uterine tube.
- **Pseudostratified ciliated** → respiratory tract. **Pseudostratified with stereocilia** → epididymis, vas deferens.
- **Transitional** → ureter, bladder, renal pelvis.
- **Simple cuboidal/columnar tubules** → kidney, salivary ducts, epididymis.

**Step 3 — What surrounds it?**
- **Cartilage rings** → trachea. **Cartilage plates** → bronchus. **No cartilage** → bronchiole.
- **Three muscle layers** → stomach. **Taeniae coli** → colon. **Thick muscle wall** → vas deferens, ureter.
- **Serosa** indicates a mesothelial covering; **adventitia** indicates attachment to surrounding connective tissue. These coverings depend on the organ segment and cannot alone determine its peritoneal position.

### How to Read an Unfamiliar Histology Slide Without Guessing

**First pass: orientation (low power).** Decide whether you are looking at a hollow organ, a solid organ, a sheet of tissue, or a mixed specimen. Look for the boundary of the sample, connective-tissue septa, major vessels, a capsule if present, and any recognizable lumen. A single section may cut an organ obliquely, and a biopsy may omit diagnostic landmarks.

**Second pass: tissue architecture (medium power).** Identify epithelial layers, glands, follicles, ducts, tubules, muscle bundles, vascular channels, lymphoid aggregates and extracellular matrix. Ask which components repeat and how they are organized. For hollow organs, follow the wall from lumen outward; for solid organs, identify functional units rather than assuming every organ has a cortex and medulla.

**Third pass: cellular evidence (high power).** Compare nuclear shape, cytoplasmic staining, apical specialization, granules, extracellular fibers and cell arrangement. Recognize artifacts: folds may mimic thick epithelium, shrinkage can create false spaces, and tangential sections can make one layer look like several.

**Fourth pass: test alternatives.** State at least two observations that support your identification and one finding that would favor a competing organ. For example, both small intestine and colon have intestinal crypts, but villi strongly favor small intestine; Brunner glands in the submucosa support duodenum.

**Fifth pass: communicate uncertainty.** If the distinguishing feature is absent, identify the tissue at the highest defensible level (for example, *intestinal mucosa* rather than *ileum*). A confident but unsupported organ label is less useful than an accurate description.

**Practice case A.** You see simple columnar epithelium with many goblet cells, closely packed straight crypts and no villi. What is the best identification? **Answer:** colonic-type mucosa. The absence of villi and abundance of goblet-rich straight crypts favor large intestine; the precise segment may require additional landmarks.

**Practice case B.** You see a tube lined by urothelium with mucosal folds and a thick, irregular smooth-muscle wall. What alternatives remain? **Answer:** ureter and urinary bladder. A relatively small, stellate lumen and organized muscular layers favor ureter; a broad lumen and interlacing detrusor bundles favor bladder, but section orientation matters.

**Practice case C.** You see densely packed tubules and scattered round capillary tufts surrounded by a narrow space. What tissue is present? **Answer:** renal cortex, because renal corpuscles are present. Proximal and distal tubules should then be distinguished by their apical brush borders and staining patterns.

**Retrieval rule:** cover each answer and justify your choice from the visual evidence before reading the explanation. The purpose is to learn an observable decision process, not to memorize an organ name from a single photograph.

### Eight common errors

1. Saying the osteoclast is mesenchymal. It is **hematopoietic**.
2. Saying the A band shortens. It **does not**.
3. Saying the thymus has follicles. It **does not**.
4. Saying the spleen has afferent lymphatics. It **does not**.
5. Saying the adrenal medulla makes steroids. It makes **catecholamines**.
6. Saying FSH acts on Leydig cells. It acts on **Sertoli** cells; LH acts on Leydig.
7. Saying rods are abundant at the foveal center. The central **foveola** is rod-free, but the broader fovea has a few rods at its periphery.
8. Saying the stomach has goblet cells. It has **surface mucous cells**, not goblet cells.

### The stains in detail

The table above is the quick-recall version; this one adds the chemistry behind each stain, so that you can predict what a stain will show even when you meet an unfamiliar one.

| Stain | Chemistry | What it stains | Why it is used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E)** | Hematoxylin is a **basic** dye; eosin is an **acidic** counterstain | Nuclei and rough ER blue-purple; collagen and most cytoplasmic proteins, especially mitochondria, pink-red | The default survey stain; the source of the basophilic/eosinophilic vocabulary |
| **Pararosaniline–toluidine blue (PT)** | Basic dye combination | Chromatin purple; cytoplasm and collagen lighter violet | Penetrates **plastic (resin) sections**, giving finer detail than H&E |
| **Mallory trichrome** | Sequential stain combination | Nuclei purple; cytoplasm, keratin, and erythrocytes red-orange; **collagen blue** | Demonstrates cells and small vessels of connective tissue. Masson and Gomori trichromes are equivalent, with collagen blue-green or green |
| **Picrosirius–hematoxylin (PSH)** | Sirius red in picric acid | Collagen red; cytoplasm pink-violet; nuclei purple | Collagen becomes **birefringent** under polarized light — a specific collagen stain |
| **Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS)** | Periodic acid oxidizes vicinal diols; Schiff reagent detects them | Carbohydrate-rich structures **magenta** | Mucin granules, glycogen, glycocalyx, basement membranes |
| **Wright–Giemsa** | Two related Romanowsky combinations | Nuclei purple; erythrocytes pink-orange; leukocyte granules differentially | Blood and bone-marrow smears |
| **Silver and gold impregnation** | Metal salts reduce on filaments | Nerve filaments and **reticulin (type III collagen)** dark brown or black | Largely replaced by immunohistochemistry |
| **Elastin stains** — Weigert resorcin fuchsin, aldehyde fuchsin, orcein | Dye binds elastin | Elastic fibers brown or purple | Distinguishing elastic from collagen (aorta, lung, skin) |
| **Lipid stains** — oil red O, Sudan black, osmium tetroxide | Lipophilic dye partitions into lipid | Lipid droplets and myelin | Requires frozen sections or special processing, because solvents used in paraffin embedding extract lipid |
| **Other aniline dyes** — azures, cresyl violet, brilliant cresyl blue, luxol fast blue, light green | Basic dyes | Various cellular and extracellular components | Permanence and color contrast in paraffin sections; many began as textile dyes |

> **MICRO** — Read the stain label before you interpret the section. "PT" tells you the section is plastic-embedded and shows fine detail; "Mallory" tells you collagen will be blue and cytoplasm red; "PSH" tells you collagen is red and can be examined under polarized light. The stain is part of the evidence.
