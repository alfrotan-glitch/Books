## 11 · The Circulatory System

**Book pages 215–236**<br/>Heart · Tissues of the Vascular Wall · Vasculature · Lymphatic Vascular System

### Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Name the three tunics of the heart and of a blood vessel, and describe what each contains.
2. Distinguish elastic artery, muscular artery, arteriole, capillary, venule, and vein in section.
3. Match each capillary type to the organ that needs it.
4. Trace the lymphatic system from blind-ended capillary to thoracic duct.

### The core idea

The circulatory system pumps and directs blood cells and substances to all tissues, and in an adult the total length of its vessels is estimated at **100,000–150,000 km**. Almost every vessel is a variation on one three-layered tube. What changes between an aorta and a capillary is **how much muscle and elastic tissue each layer contains** — decided entirely by the pressure the vessel must withstand and the job it must do.

### Heart

- **Endocardium** — endothelium + subendothelial connective tissue + a layer of Purkinje fibers; continuous with the intima of vessels.
- **Myocardium** — the thickest layer; cardiac muscle in complex spirals, atria thinner than ventricles, left ventricle thickest.
- **Epicardium** — connective tissue containing many **adipocytes**, covered by mesothelium; the visceral layer of the pericardium.
- **Cardiac skeleton** — dense irregular connective tissue rings that anchor the valves and electrically insulate atria from ventricles.
- **Valves** — endocardium folded over a core of dense connective tissue; **avascular**. The mitral and tricuspid have chordae tendineae and papillary muscles.

### Tissues of the Vascular Wall

| Layer | Contents | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Intima** | Endothelium, basal lamina, subendothelial connective tissue, **internal elastic lamina** (in arteries) | Non-thrombogenic surface, exchange, barrier |
| **Media** | **Smooth muscle** circularly arranged, elastic lamellae (in elastic arteries), **external elastic lamina** | Tone and compliance |
| **Adventitia** | Loose connective tissue, **vasa vasorum**, **nervi vascularis** | Anchorage and supply of the wall |

**The rule of the wall:** the thicker the media, the higher the pressure the vessel handles. Arteries have a thick media; veins a thin media and a thick adventitia. That single ratio lets you identify a vessel on any slide.

### Vasculature

| Vessel | Intima | Media | Adventitia | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Elastic artery** (aorta, pulmonary, common carotid) | Endothelium + subendothelial | **Many elastic lamellae** alternating with smooth muscle | Thin, with vasa vasorum | **Elastic lamellae** |
| **Muscular artery** | Prominent **internal elastic lamina** | Thick, 3–40 layers of smooth muscle | Moderate | **Well-defined IEL** |
| **Arteriole** | Endothelium + thin subendothelium | 1–2 layers of smooth muscle | Minimal | **The resistance vessel** |
| **Capillary** | Endothelium + basal lamina only | None | None | One cell thick |
| **Venule** | Endothelium | None or sparse pericytes | Thin | Site of **leukocyte diapedesis** |
| **Small/medium vein** | Endothelium | Thin | **Thickest layer** | Often collapsed on section |
| **Large vein** (vena cava) | Endothelium | Thin | **Thick, with longitudinal smooth muscle** | Valve-like intimal folds |

**Capillaries:**

| Type | Structure | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | Uninterrupted endothelium, tight junctions | Muscle, skin, lung, CNS | Regulated exchange; the **blood–brain barrier** is this type plus tight junctions and astrocyte feet |
| **Fenestrated** | Pores with diaphragms | Kidney glomerulus, intestine, endocrine glands | Rapid fluid and solute exchange |
| **Sinusoidal** | Discontinuous, large gaps, incomplete basal lamina | **Liver, spleen, bone marrow** | Free passage of cells and large molecules |

**Microcirculation.** Arteriole → metarteriole → capillary bed → venule, with **precapillary sphincters** regulating flow. **Arteriovenous anastomoses** shunt blood directly, important in skin thermoregulation.

### Lymphatic Vascular System

Begins as blind-ended **lymphatic capillaries** with overlapping endothelial flaps and **anchoring filaments**, which open when interstitial pressure rises. They converge into collecting vessels with valves, then nodes, trunks, and the **thoracic duct** and **right lymphatic duct**. Lymphatics have **no continuous basal lamina** and no fenestrations — which is exactly why tumor cells and bacteria enter them so easily.

> **CLINICAL** — **Atherosclerosis** begins with endothelial dysfunction: LDL crosses the endothelium, is oxidized, and provokes monocyte recruitment. Macrophages become **foam cells**, a fatty streak forms, smooth muscle migrates into the intima, and a fibrous cap covers a necrotic core. Rupture of a thin cap exposes thrombogenic material and produces the acute coronary syndrome. **Aneurysm** follows destruction of medial elastic fibers. **Varicose veins** follow valve failure. **Lymphedema** follows lymphatic obstruction — classically after axillary node clearance.

> **MICRO** — Arteries: **round lumen, thick wall, corrugated internal elastic lamina**. Veins: **irregular collapsed lumen, thin media**. In elastic arteries the media shows wavy pink bands with no orderly muscle coat; in muscular arteries the media is uniform smooth muscle.

> **TIP** — The three capillaries are distinguished by **continuity, not size**: continuous (tight), fenestrated (pores), sinusoidal (gaps wide enough for a red cell). Liver, spleen, marrow = sinusoidal. Kidney glomerulus and intestine = fenestrated. Brain = continuous with tight junctions.

### Chapter 11 — one-line answers

- Three tunics: intima (endothelium + subendothelial tissue), media (smooth muscle, elastic lamellae), adventitia (collagen).
- Elastic arteries have many concentric elastic lamellae; muscular arteries have a prominent internal elastic lamina.
- Arterioles have one or two smooth-muscle layers and control peripheral resistance.
- Continuous capillaries are the default; fenestrated ones serve endocrine glands, gut, and kidney; sinusoids serve liver, spleen, and marrow.
- Veins have thinner walls, larger lumens, and valves in medium-sized veins.
- The heart has endocardium, myocardium, and epicardium; Purkinje fibers conduct the impulse.
- Lymphatic capillaries are blind-ended and more permeable than blood capillaries; they drain to the thoracic duct.
- The thoracic duct empties into the left subclavian vein.

### Active recall

1. **Three layers of a vessel wall, outside in?** — Tunica adventitia, tunica media, tunica intima.
2. **Which vessel has the thickest media relative to its lumen?** — The muscular artery; an arteriole has only one or two smooth-muscle layers.
3. **Which capillary type is fenestrated, and where is it found?** — Fenestrated capillaries are in endocrine glands, gut mucosa, and kidney; they allow rapid exchange.
4. **What distinguishes a vein from an artery in section?** — Veins have a larger, often collapsed lumen and a thinner wall with less muscle; valves are present in medium veins.
5. **Where does lymph begin and end?** — Blind-ended lymphatic capillaries in tissue; it drains through nodes to the thoracic duct and into the subclavian vein.

## 12 · Blood

**Book pages 237–253**<br/>Composition of Plasma · Blood Cells

### Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Describe plasma and explain how serum differs from it.
2. Relate erythrocyte shape, size, and lifespan to gas transport.
3. Identify each leukocyte by nucleus and granules, and state its function.
4. Explain platelet structure and the steps of hemostasis.

### The core idea

Blood is connective tissue with a liquid matrix. Its cells all derive from one stem cell, and their morphology is a direct readout of their job: **red for oxygen, granular white for killing, agranular white for immunity, and fragments for clotting.**

### Composition of Plasma

About **55% of blood volume**. Water plus plasma proteins — **albumin, α- and β-globulins, complement proteins, and fibrinogen**, all secreted in the **liver**, plus the **immunoglobulins** — and electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste.

The book's Table 12–1 quantifies the mixture:

| Component | Share | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Water** | ~92% of plasma | Solvent in which formed elements are suspended |
| **Plasma proteins** | ~7% of plasma | Buffer pH; maintain osmotic pressure |
| — **Albumin** | ~58% of plasma protein | Osmotic pull (oncotic pressure); carries fatty acids, hormones, drugs |
| — **Globulins** | ~37% of plasma protein | α- and β-globulins transport lipids, metals, and iron; γ-globulins are antibodies |
| — **Fibrinogen** | ~4% of plasma protein | Precursor of fibrin for clotting |
| — **Regulatory proteins** | **>1%** of plasma protein | Enzymes, proenzymes, hormones, complement |
| **Electrolytes** | **~1%** of plasma (with nutrients) | Membrane potentials, pH, osmotic control |
| **Nutrients** | **~1%** of plasma (with electrolytes) | Glucose, amino acids, cholesterol, vitamins — energy and synthesis |

On clotting, some proteins are consumed and others are released from platelets, leaving **serum**, whose biological properties differ from plasma. **Serum is plasma minus fibrinogen** (i.e., after clotting).

### Blood Cells

**Erythrocytes**

- **Biconcave disc**, **7.5 µm** in diameter, **2.6 µm** thick at the rim but only **0.75 µm** at the center — flexible enough to pass small capillaries; **no nucleus, no organelles**, so the cell is packed with hemoglobin.
- Lifespan about **120 days**; removed by splenic macrophages; the globin is recycled and the heme converted to bilirubin.
- Shape maintained by **spectrin and ankyrin**; defects produce hereditary spherocytosis.
- **Hemoglobin** — four globin chains, each with a heme group carrying Fe²⁺. Adult HbA (α₂β₂) and fetal HbF (α₂γ₂, higher affinity for O₂).
- **Reticulocytes** are young red cells with residual RNA, appearing polychromatic; a raised count signals active marrow output.
- **Hematocrit** — the sedimented erythrocyte column — is about **44–45% of total blood volume** in healthy adults; the thin layer between cells and plasma is the **buffy coat** of leukocytes and platelets. An average adult has **5–6 L** of blood (supplementary standard value, not stated in the chapter).

**The leukocytes** — broadly grouped as **granulocytes** (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and **agranulocytes** (lymphocytes, monocytes)

| Cell | % of WBC | Size | Nucleus | Cytoplasm | Main function |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Neutrophil** | **60–70%** | 10–14 µm | **3–5 lobes**, dense | Pale; fine granules | **Phagocytosis of bacteria**; the acute responder |
| **Eosinophil** | **2–4%** | 10–14 µm | **Bilobed** | **Bright orange-red** granules | **Parasites and allergy**; major basic protein |
| **Basophil** | **0.5–1%** | 10–14 µm | **S-shaped**, obscured | **Large dark blue** granules | **Histamine and heparin** |
| **Lymphocyte** | **20–30%** | 7–12 µm | Round, dense | Pale rim | **T cells**, **B cells**, **NK cells** |
| **Monocyte** | **3–8%** | **14–20 µm** (largest) | **Kidney-shaped** | Abundant blue-gray | Become **macrophages** |

**Neutrophil granules:** **azurophilic (primary)** granules contain myeloperoxidase and defensins; **specific (secondary)** granules contain lysozyme and lactoferrin. The neutrophil dies after phagocytosis; the accumulation of dead neutrophils and debris is **pus**. Each **phagocyte** kills ingested bacteria inside a phagolysosome.

**Platelets** are **2–4 µm** anucleate fragments of **megakaryocyte** cytoplasm, with a peripheral clear zone (hyalomere) and a central granular zone (granulomere). They number **150,000–400,000/µL** and circulate for about **10 days**. They adhere, activate, and aggregate to form the primary hemostatic plug; **von Willebrand factor** links them to collagen.

**Hemostasis in four steps** (the standard physiology sequence; this chapter describes only the platelet plug and clotting): (1) **Vascular spasm** — the injured vessel constricts, slowing flow. (2) **Primary hemostasis** — platelets bind exposed collagen (directly and through von Willebrand factor), degranulate, and aggregate into a soft plug. (3) **Coagulation** — exposed tissue factor and platelet phospholipid trigger the clotting cascade, converting prothrombin to **thrombin**, which cleaves fibrinogen into **fibrin**; the fibrin mesh stabilizes the plug as a clot. (4) **Retraction and repair** — the clot contracts, and endothelial cells and fibroblasts rebuild the wall.

> **CLINICAL** — The differential count is a diagnostic instrument. **Neutrophilia** suggests bacterial infection or steroid use; **eosinophilia** parasites or allergy; **lymphocytosis** viral infection or chronic lymphocytic leukemia; **pancytopenia** marrow failure. In **sickle cell disease** a single glutamic acid → valine substitution in β-globin allows deoxygenated HbS to polymerize; the cell sickles, occludes small vessels, and is removed by the spleen.

> **MICRO** — Count lobes, not granules. **Neutrophil: 3–5 lobes. Eosinophil: exactly two, orange granules. Basophil: granules so dark the nucleus is hidden. Monocyte: kidney-shaped nucleus, largest cell. Lymphocyte: round dense nucleus, almost no cytoplasm.**

> **MNEMONIC** — **"Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas"** = **N**eutrophil, **L**ymphocyte, **M**onocyte, **E**osinophil, **B**asophil — in descending order of abundance.

> **TIP** — Eosinophil granules stain with eosin because they are **basic proteins** — the naming is the reverse of what students expect. The basophil's granules contain **heparin and histamine**, so the "basic" cell is the one full of allergy mediators.

### Chapter 12 — one-line answers

- Plasma is the fluid phase; serum is plasma after clotting and lacks fibrinogen.
- Erythrocytes are biconcave, 7.5 µm, anucleate, and live about 120 days.
- Hemoglobin is the gas carrier; the biconcave shape maximizes surface-to-volume ratio.
- Neutrophils are the most numerous leukocyte and the first responder to acute inflammation.
- Eosinophils have bilobed nuclei and orange-red granules (major basic protein); they attack parasites.
- Basophils carry histamine and heparin and mediate immediate hypersensitivity.
- Lymphocytes are the second most numerous; monocytes are the largest and become macrophages.
- Platelets are anucleate fragments of megakaryocytes and form the primary hemostatic plug.
- Hemostasis = vascular spasm → platelet plug → coagulation → clot retraction and repair.

### Active recall

1. **How does serum differ from plasma?** — Serum is plasma after clotting, so it lacks fibrinogen and consumed clotting factors.
2. **Normal erythrocyte dimensions and lifespan?** — About 7.5 µm diameter, biconcave with a 2.6-µm rim, circulating about 120 days.
3. **Which leukocyte is most numerous, and which is largest?** — Neutrophils (~60–70%); monocytes are the largest.
4. **What do eosinophil granules contain?** — Major basic protein and eosinophil cationic protein, active against parasites and in allergy.
5. **What are the four steps of hemostasis?** — Vascular spasm, primary hemostasis (platelet plug), coagulation (fibrin clot), and clot retraction with repair.

**Answer: B** — Bilobed nucleus plus large eosinophilic granules is the eosinophil.

## 13 · Hemopoiesis

**Book pages 254–266**<br/>Stem Cells, Growth Factors, & Differentiation · Bone Marrow · Maturation of Erythrocytes · Maturation of Granulocytes · Maturation of Agranulocytes · Origin of Platelets

### Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Trace the shift of hemopoietic sites from embryo to adult.
2. Describe the marrow niche and the stromal cells that support it.
3. Follow each lineage from stem cell to mature cell, naming the recognizable stages.
4. Match growth factors and colony-stimulating factors to the lineages they drive.

### The core idea

All blood cells descend from one **hematopoietic stem cell**, and the pathway from that cell to a mature erythrocyte, granulocyte, or platelet is a sequence of named stages you can recognize by **decreasing nucleus, increasing cytoplasm, and changing color**. It is the most examinable lineage in histology because every stage has a name.

### Sites of hemopoiesis

| Period | Site |
|---|---|
| **First trimester** | Yolk sac (mesoblastic phase) |
| **Second trimester** | **Liver and spleen** (hepatic phase) |
| **Third trimester** | **Bone marrow** — marrow becomes the major organ in the third trimester as skeletal elements ossify |

In the adult, active **red marrow** persists in the **sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis, skull, and proximal femur**. The rest is **yellow (fatty) marrow**, which can revert to red marrow under demand — why chronic anemia produces marrow expansion and frontal bossing.

### Stem Cells, Growth Factors, & Differentiation

The **hematopoietic stem cell** is CD34⁺, self-renewing, and pluripotent, sitting in a **niche** of stromal cells, other cells, and ECM. Committed **progenitor cells** — also called **CFUs** (colony-forming units) — proliferate and differentiate under growth factors, which are also called **CSFs** (colony-stimulating factors) or cytokines. Differentiation is driven by **growth factors**, examinable by name:

| Factor | Target |
|---|---|
| **Erythropoietin (EPO)** | Erythroid progenitors (kidney, some liver) |
| **Thrombopoietin (TPO)** | Megakaryocytes (liver) |
| **G-CSF** | Granulocyte production |
| **GM-CSF, M-CSF** | Granulocyte–monocyte and monocyte lineages |
| **IL-3** | Early multipotent progenitors |
| **IL-5** | Eosinophils |
| **SCF (stem cell factor)** | Stem cell maintenance |

### Bone Marrow

Between the trabeculae lie **hemopoietic cords** and **sinusoids** with a discontinuous endothelium, so mature cells can enter the circulation. The **myeloid:erythroid (M:E) ratio** is normally **2:1 to 4:1**; it shifts toward erythroid in hemolytic anemia and toward myeloid in infection or leukemia.

### Maturation of Erythrocytes

**Proerythroblast → basophilic → polychromatophilic → orthochromatophilic erythroblast → reticulocyte → erythrocyte**, developing in **erythropoietic islands or cords**. The names track the cytoplasm's transition from **RNA-rich to hemoglobin-filled**; at the last stage the nucleus is **extruded**, producing a **reticulocyte** that still contains some polyribosomes when released into the circulation.

The cell **shrinks**, the nucleus **condenses and is extruded**, hemoglobin accumulates so the cytoplasm shifts from blue to pink, and the nucleolus disappears. The sequence takes about **5–7 days**. EPO drives it; iron, vitamin B₁₂, and folate are required.

### Maturation of Granulocytes

**Myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band cell → segmented neutrophil (or eosinophil/basophil).**

The suffixes carry the meaning: **-blast** = dividing precursor (**myeloblast**, **lymphoblast**, **proerythroblast**), **-cyte** = the later, non-dividing cell (**myelocyte**, **lymphocyte**, **reticulocyte**); **thrombocyte** is another name for the platelet.

**Primary (azurophilic) granules** appear at the promyelocyte stage, **specific granules** at the myelocyte stage, the nucleus indents at the metamyelocyte stage, and becomes a **C-shaped band** before segmenting. A **left shift** — more band forms and younger cells in the peripheral blood — indicates accelerated production: immature neutrophils, chiefly **band (stab) cells**, are released prematurely when the circulating neutrophil pool is depleted by bacterial infection.

### Maturation of Agranulocytes

**Lymphopoiesis** begins in the marrow for B cells and in the thymus for T cells. **Monopoiesis** produces monocytes that become tissue macrophages. Both pass through stages in which the nucleus becomes less lobated, the cytoplasm more abundant, and the chromatin pattern characteristic of the mature cell.

### Origin of Platelets

**Megakaryoblasts → megakaryocytes**, which become polyploid and develop **demarcation membranes** that delineate future platelet territories; platelets bud from the cytoplasm under TPO control.

> **CLINICAL** — **Aplastic anemia** is marrow failure with an empty, fatty marrow and pancytopenia. **Leukemia** is the opposite: marrow packed with malignant blasts, crowding out normal lineages and producing anemia, infection, and bleeding. **Megaloblastic anemia** (B₁₂ or folate deficiency) shows nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony — the nucleus matures more slowly than the cytoplasm, so cells are large and hypersegmented. **Iron deficiency** produces microcytic hypochromic cells because hemoglobin synthesis lags behind cell division.

> **MICRO** — Identify the lineage by **nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and granule color**. Early cells have a large nucleus, a thin rim of basophilic cytoplasm, and a nucleolus. As cells mature, the ratio falls, granules appear, and the nucleus condenses. **Maturation moves in one direction: nucleus shrinks, cytoplasm grows, color shifts from blue to pink or from blue to granular.**

> **TIP** — Two facts that are easy to invert: **EPO comes from the kidney** (peritubular interstitial cells), and **TPO comes mostly from the liver**. Students routinely invert these.

### Chapter 13 — one-line answers

- Hemopoiesis shifts from yolk sac → liver and spleen → bone marrow; the adult site is red marrow of the axial skeleton.
- The marrow stroma (reticular cells, macrophages, adipocytes, endothelial cells) forms the hematopoietic niche.
- Erythropoiesis: proerythroblast → basophilic → polychromatophilic → orthochromatophilic normoblast → reticulocyte → erythrocyte.
- The nucleus is extruded at the reticulocyte stage; the reticulocyte still contains residual RNA.
- Granulopoiesis: myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band → segmented cell.
- Specific granules appear at the myelocyte stage; azurophilic granules at the promyelocyte stage.
- Megakaryocytes are polyploid and shed platelets from their cytoplasm.
- Erythropoietin from the kidney drives erythroid production; G-CSF drives granulocytes.
- The normal myeloid-to-erythroid ratio is about 2:1 to 4:1.

### Active recall

1. **Where does hemopoiesis occur in the adult?** — Red marrow of the axial skeleton (vertebrae, ribs, sternum, pelvis, proximal femur).
2. **What is the first recognizable erythroid precursor, and what is lost at the reticulocyte stage?** — Proerythroblast; the nucleus is extruded to form the reticulocyte.
3. **How do you recognize a megakaryocyte?** — A huge polyploid cell with a lobulated nucleus whose cytoplasm fragments into platelets.
4. **Which growth factor drives erythroid production?** — Erythropoietin, made by peritubular interstitial cells of the kidney.
5. **What does the M:E ratio describe?** — The ratio of myeloid (granulocytic) to erythroid precursors in marrow, normally about 2:1 to 4:1.

**Answer: A** — The megakaryocyte is the polyploid platelet factory; osteoclasts are multinucleated but resorb bone, not make platelets.

## 14 · The Immune System & Lymphoid Organs

**Book pages 267–294**<br/>Innate & Adaptive Immunity · Cytokines · Antigens & Antibodies · Antigen Presentation · Cells of Adaptive Immunity · Thymus · Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue · Lymph Nodes · Spleen

### Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Contrast innate and adaptive immunity in cells, speed, and memory.
2. Explain antigen presentation by MHC I and MHC II and the T-cell classes they activate.
3. Compare the architecture and function of thymus, lymph node, MALT, and spleen.
4. Explain how T and B cells are selected and activated, and what happens when selection fails.

### The core idea

The immune system is a set of organs arranged to **bring antigen to lymphocytes**. Each organ is a different solution to the same problem: how to filter a fluid (blood, lymph, or air/food), trap antigen, and present it to the right cell.

### Innate & Adaptive Immunity

| Feature | Innate | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | **Present from birth**, acts within minutes | **Develops more slowly**, improves with exposure |
| Specificity | Broad molecular patterns | **Exact antigen** |
| Memory | None | **Yes** |
| Cells | **Granulocytes** (mainly neutrophils), macrophages, dendritic cells, NK cells | **T and B lymphocytes** |
| Mediators | **Defensins, complement, lysozyme, interferons**, cytokines | Antibodies, cytotoxic granules |

The book's one-line definition is worth memorizing: innate immunity is present from birth and uses leukocytes (mainly granulocytes) plus soluble proteins; adaptive immunity develops more slowly and is **based on antigen presentation to lymphocytes**.

### Cytokines

**Polypeptide hormones of the immune system** — the book's phrase — secreted by immune cells to regulate one another. The ones worth naming: **interleukins** (IL-1, IL-2 for T-cell proliferation, IL-4 for IgE and Th2 responses, IL-5 for eosinophils, IL-6 for acute-phase response), **interferons** (antiviral; IFN-γ activates macrophages), **TNF-α** (inflammation, fever, cachexia), **chemokines** (directed leukocyte migration), and **colony-stimulating factors** (hemopoiesis, Chapter 13). Cytokines act locally (paracrine) or on the secreting cell (autocrine), and their excessive release produces a cytokine storm and the systemic inflammatory response.

### Antigens & Antibodies

**Antigens** are molecules recognized by the adaptive immune system; **epitopes** are the specific parts bound by a receptor. **Antibodies** are immunoglobulins:

| Class | Structure | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| **IgG** | Monomer | Most abundant; crosses the placenta; opsonizes |
| **IgM** | Pentamer | First responder; complement activation |
| **IgA** | Dimer with secretory component | Mucosal secretions |
| **IgE** | Monomer | Mast-cell binding; allergy and parasites |
| **IgD** | Monomer | B-cell receptor |

### Antigen Presentation

**T cells cannot see free antigen.** They recognize peptide displayed on **MHC** molecules:

- **MHC class I** — on the surface of **all nucleated cells**, bearing fragments of that cell's own proteins (and viral proteins if infected); recognized by **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells**.
- **MHC class II** — only on **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)**, mostly derived from monocytes; presents fragments of **endocytosed foreign** proteins to **CD4⁺ helper T cells**. This is the defining difference: MHC I says "here is what I am making"; MHC II says "here is what I have eaten".

This pairing — **CD4/MHC II, CD8/MHC I** — is the most examined immunology fact in the chapter.

### Cells of Adaptive Immunity

**T lymphocytes** mature in the thymus and are responsible for cell-mediated immunity. Their **T-cell receptor (TCR)** is usually an **αβ heterodimer**; each T cell also carries one CD marker that fixes its class:

| T-cell class | Marker | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Helper T cell** | **CD4⁺** | Recognizes MHC II; secretes cytokines that activate B cells and macrophages |
| **Cytotoxic T cell** | **CD8⁺** | Recognizes MHC I; kills infected or tumor cells |
| **Regulatory T cell** | **CD4⁺ CD25⁺** | Suppresses immune responses and prevents autoimmunity |
| **γδ T cell** | γδ TCR | Mainly in **epithelia**; early defense at body surfaces |

**B lymphocytes** mature in bone marrow and carry a **B-cell receptor (BCR)** — surface **IgM or IgD** — that binds antigen directly, without MHC. On activation they become **plasma cells** (antibody factories) and **memory cells**. **NK cells** kill without prior sensitization and without MHC restriction.

### How a Lymphoid Nodule Forms

This sequence explains every secondary lymphoid organ in the chapter:

1. Lymphocytes are packed in a meshwork of **reticulin** made by **fibroblastic reticular cells**; most APCs among them are **dendritic cells** with many processes.
2. In a secondary organ, **follicular dendritic cells (FDCs)** display antigen–antibody complexes on their large surface. A B cell whose BCR binds that antigen is held there.
3. With cytokines from helper T cells, that B cell **proliferates clonally**, producing a large transient nodule with a pale **germinal center**.
4. The cells disperse as **plasma cells, various T cells, and B and T memory cells** — which is why the same nodule can look "active" (large germinal center) or "resting" (dense primary follicle).

### Thymus

- **Capsule** with trabeculae dividing it into lobules, each with a **cortex** and a **medulla**.
- **No follicles.** The cortex is densely packed with developing **thymocytes** and epithelial reticular cells; the medulla is paler and contains **Hassall corpuscles** — concentric whorls of epithelial cells, unique to the thymus.
- **Blood–thymus barrier** — formed by **squamous thymic epithelial cells** joined by desmosomes and occluding junctions, together with vascular endothelium and pericytes — prevents unregulated exposure of thymocytes to antigens.
- **Cortex** is packed with **T lymphoblasts (thymocytes)**, macrophages, and **thymic epithelial cells (TECs)**. Stellate TECs form a **cytoreticulum** to which lymphocytes attach instead of reticulin fibers; these TECs are **antigen-presenting cells** expressing **MHC class II** and secreting cytokines — which is why the thymus is also counted among endocrine glands.
- **Hassall corpuscles** — aggregates of medullary TECs, concentrically arranged, **up to 100 µm**, **unique to the medulla**; their cells secrete cytokines that control local dendritic cells and promote regulatory T cells for peripheral tolerance.
- Function: **T-cell maturation and selection**. **Positive selection occurs in the cortex**: only thymocytes whose TCR recognizes the individual's own MHC molecules survive. **Negative selection occurs in the medulla**: thymocytes that bind self-antigen too tightly are deleted, leaving tolerance. Medullary TECs express **Aire**, which lets them display a wide sample of tissue self-antigens for this test.
- **Involutes** after puberty, replaced by adipose tissue.

### Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue

**Tonsils** (palatine, lingual, pharyngeal/adenoid, tubal) — a partial capsule (hemicapsule), no afferent lymphatics, epithelium invaginates into crypts. **Peyer's patches** in the ileum; **appendix**; **MALT** throughout the gut, respiratory tract, and genitourinary tract.

### Lymph Nodes

| Region | Contents | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Cortex** | **Lymphoid follicles** — primary (dense) or secondary (with pale **germinal center** and dark mantle) | **B-cell** zone |
| **Paracortex** | Diffuse lymphoid tissue, **high endothelial venules (HEV)** | **T-cell** zone; lymphocytes enter from blood here |
| **Medulla** | Cords and sinuses, macrophages and plasma cells | Filtration; antibody secretion |

**Flow:** afferent lymphatics → subcapsular sinus → trabecular sinuses → medullary sinuses → **efferent lymphatics at the hilum**. The one-way flow explains why nodal metastases lodge first in the subcapsular sinus.

### Spleen

- **No afferent lymphatics.** It filters **blood**.
- **White pulp** — lymphocytes around a central arteriole: the **periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS)** is the T-cell zone; follicles at its periphery are the B-cell zone.
- **Red pulp** — **cords of Billroth** and **sinusoids** whose elongated endothelial **stave cells** are separated by narrow slits.
- **Two routes through the red pulp.** In the **closed circulation**, capillaries from the penicillar arterioles connect directly to sinusoids. In the **open circulation** (about half of the penicillar arterioles), capillaries are open-ended and dump blood into the splenic cords, so plasma and cells must squeeze between stave cells to re-enter the vasculature. Platelets, motile leukocytes, and thin flexible erythrocytes pass; **stiff or swollen erythrocytes at the end of their 120-day life are blocked and removed by macrophages**.
- **Iron recycling.** Iron released from **hemoglobin** during erythrocyte degradation is stored by macrophages in **ferritin**, released bound to **transferrin**, and reused for erythropoiesis. Heme is carried by **hemopexin** and degraded to **bilirubin**, which is excreted in bile.
- **Functions:** blood filtration, removal of old erythrocytes, immune response to blood-borne antigen, and **extramedullary hemopoiesis** in fetal life and marrow failure.

| Organ | Capsule | Afferent lymphatics | Follicles | Unique feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Thymus** | Yes | None | **No** | **Hassall corpuscles** |
| **Lymph node** | Yes | **Yes** | Yes | Cortex/paracortex/medulla; HEV |
| **Spleen** | Yes | **None** | Yes | **White and red pulp** |
| **Tonsil** | Partial | None | Yes | Crypts |

> **CLINICAL** — **DiGeorge syndrome** is thymic aplasia (third and fourth pharyngeal pouches) with T-cell deficiency and hypocalcemia. **Severe combined immunodeficiency** is failure of both arms. **Hodgkin lymphoma** spreads contiguously through nodes and shows Reed–Sternberg cells. **Splenectomy** increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms — pneumococcus, meningococcus, *Haemophilus influenzae* — because the spleen's marginal zone is the first line against polysaccharide capsules.

> **MICRO** — Ask **"what is at the center of the nodule?"** Thymus: lobules with dark cortex and pale medulla plus pink whorls (Hassall corpuscles). Lymph node: capsule, subcapsular sinus, hilum. Spleen: **white pulp islands inside a red sea** of sinusoids and cords.

> **MNEMONIC** — **"Thymus Teaches T cells; Nodes Filter lymph; Spleen Filters blood."** And **"CD4 sees MHC II (2 × 2); CD8 sees MHC I (8 × 1)."**

> **TIP** — Two negative facts are worth more than most positive ones: the **thymus has no follicles**, and the **spleen has no afferent lymphatics**.

### Chapter 14 — one-line answers

- Innate immunity = present at birth, granulocytes and soluble proteins; adaptive = antigen presentation to lymphocytes.
- MHC I is on **all nucleated cells** and presents endogenous peptide to **CD8⁺** cells.
- MHC II is on **APCs** (mostly monocyte-derived) and presents endocytosed peptide to **CD4⁺** cells.
- BCR = surface **IgM or IgD**; TCR is usually **αβ** with a CD co-receptor.
- T cells: CD4 helper, CD8 cytotoxic, CD4⁺CD25⁺ regulatory, γδ in epithelia.
- B cells make antibodies (humoral); T cells mediate cell-mediated immunity.
- Plasma cells secrete antibody; memory B and T cells give the rapid second response.
- Primary lymphoid organs: **bone marrow (B)** and **thymus (T)**.
- Secondary: lymph nodes, MALT, spleen — where lymphocytes are activated.
- FDCs display antigen–antibody complexes; activated B cells form germinal centers.
- Thymus: cortex = positive selection, medulla = negative selection; **Aire** displays self-antigens.
- Thymus has **no follicles** and Hassall corpuscles in the medulla.
- Lymph node: cortex B cells, paracortex T cells and HEVs, medulla cords and sinuses.
- Spleen: white pulp (PALS = T zone, follicles = B zone) and red pulp (cords + sinusoids).
- Spleen filters blood; **open circulation** lets macrophages remove stiff 120-day erythrocytes.
- Iron is stored as **ferritin**, carried by **transferrin**, and heme iron binds **hemopexin**; the rest becomes bilirubin.

### Active recall

1. **Which MHC class is on all nucleated cells, and which T cell reads it?** — MHC I; CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells.
2. **Which cells present on MHC II?** — Antigen-presenting cells, mostly monocyte-derived: dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells.
3. **Where do T cells undergo positive and negative selection?** — Positive selection in the cortex; negative selection in the medulla.
4. **What is the difference between the thymus and the spleen in one line?** — The thymus makes and selects T cells and has no follicles; the spleen filters blood and has no afferent lymphatics.
5. **What does an FDC do?** — Follicular dendritic cells display antigen–antibody complexes to B cells and drive germinal-center formation.
