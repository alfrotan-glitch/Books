BEFORE YOU BEGIN

How to Use This Guide

Histology is the one preclinical subject where you cannot bluff. Everything is either there on the slide or it is not. But histology is also the most efficient subject in medicine, because a small number of structural rules generate almost every fact you will be asked.

This guide condenses Junqueira’s Basic Histology: Text & Atlas, 17th edition (Anthony L. Mescher, McGraw Hill, 2024; ISBN 978-1-264-93039-5) into the material that decides exam marks. It is built on the book’s own contents page: every chapter follows the same section sequence, in the same order, using the book’s own section titles, with printed page numbers so you can read a section here and then turn straight to the corresponding pages in your copy.

At the end of every chapter in the book there is a Summary of Key Points and an Assess Your Knowledge question set. This guide is designed to be read before those, so that when you reach them you already know the answers.

Three questions answer 80% of slide questions

  1. What tissue is this? — epithelium, connective tissue, muscle, nervous tissue, or a fluid.
  2. What organ or region? — the tissue arrangement identifies the organ, not the cell type.
  3. What is it doing here? — every structure you can see exists because of a function the cell must perform. Name the function and the marks follow.

TIP — Never memorise a slide as a picture. Memorise the rule that generates the picture (“stratified squamous with keratin on top = surface exposed to abrasion and drying”). The book’s atlas plates teach you the appearance; this guide teaches you the rule behind it.

Three study plans

If you have six weeks. One part of this guide per week. Read the corresponding chapter, then this section, then cover the tables and redraw them from memory. Do the book’s “Assess Your Knowledge” questions and this guide’s MCQs at the end of the week. Reserve the final week for the Rapid Review chapter only.

If you have ten days. Two to three chapters per day, tables only on the first pass. Spend 60% of your time on the six comparison tables that carry the most marks: epithelium types, connective tissue fibres, cartilage, muscle, leucocytes, and GI tract wall.

If you have 48 hours. Read Rapid Review (Chapter 24) end to end, then the 100 MCQs, then the six tables. Do not attempt prose. Histology rewards pattern recognition under time pressure, and patterns live in tables.

The Twelve Laws of Histology

These twelve statements are true across the whole book. When you are stuck in an exam, reason from them.

  1. Structure follows function, and function follows structure. Every visible feature is a solution to a physical problem — diffusion distance, mechanical stress, surface area, water loss, conduction speed.
  2. Form is set by the apical surface, the basal surface, and the junctions between cells. Look at all three before naming a tissue.
  3. Epithelium sits on something and is never crossed by a blood vessel. Avascularity plus a basement membrane defines it.
  4. Connective tissue is defined by its extracellular matrix, not its cells. The matrix tells you the mechanical job: tension, elasticity, compression, or filtration.
  5. Cells that make protein look basophilic; cells that store or secrete protein look eosinophilic. Rough ER is blue-purple with haematoxylin; secretory product and cytoplasm are pink with eosin.
  6. Cells that make steroid hormone are full of smooth ER and lipid droplets — hence foamy, pale, or vacuolated cytoplasm (adrenal cortex, Leydig cells, corpus luteum).
  7. Muscle is classified by nucleus position and striation, not by shape alone. Peripheral multiple nuclei = skeletal; central single nucleus with striation = cardiac; central single nucleus without striation = smooth.
  8. The number of cell layers tells you the mechanical job. One layer = exchange, absorption, secretion, or a thin barrier. Many layers = protection from abrasion.
  9. Surface area is bought with folds. Microvilli, villi, plicae circulares, basal infoldings, and cristae all exist for the same arithmetic reason.
  10. Tubes are built in concentric layers, and each layer has one job. In blood vessels: intima for exchange, media for tone, adventitia for anchorage. In the gut: mucosa for digestion, submucosa for supply, muscularis for movement.
  11. A capsule and a hilum identify a solid organ as lymph node, spleen, thymus, or gland. The route of fluid flow tells you which one.
  12. Every histology fact has a clinical twin. If you cannot name the disease, you do not yet own the fact.

Exam Technique

Answering a slide or image question

Never begin with a guess. Use this sequence every single time:

  1. Magnification and staining. H&E at low power versus PAS versus silver tells you what the examiner thinks is important.
  2. The boundary. Is there a free surface? A lumen? A capsule? Blood vessels inside the tissue? This immediately separates epithelium-lined organs from solid organs.
  3. The dominant tissue. Note the arrangement — cords, acini, tubules, follicles, layers, bundles.
  4. The one diagnostic cell. Parietal cell, Paneth cell, podocyte, Purkinje fibre, Clara cell, Sertoli cell, odontoblast. Examiners choose images that contain one.
  5. Name it, then justify it in the same sentence. “This is thyroid gland because the parenchyma is arranged in follicles of colloid lined by a single layer of cuboidal cells.” Justification earns the marks; the name alone earns half.

Answering a “describe and give the function of” essay

Use a fixed skeleton — it works for any organ:

Capsule → cortex → medulla → parenchyma arrangement → cell types → blood supply → ducts or outflow → function → clinical correlation.

Ten headings, ten marks, almost regardless of the organ. Write the headings first, then fill them. An examiner marking 200 scripts recognises structure instantly and rewards it.

Ten phrases that lose marks

  • “It looks like…” without saying why it looks that way.
  • Naming a cell without its product or function.
  • Confusing “basophilic” (blue, acidic structure) with “eosinophilic” (pink, basic protein).
  • Saying “simple squamous” for endothelium without noting it is a type of simple squamous epithelium.
  • Using “mucous” for “mucosa” — different words, different things.
  • Describing the adrenal medulla and cortex as one tissue; they are embryologically unrelated.
  • Calling transitional epithelium “stratified squamous”; the umbrella cells are unique.
  • Forgetting that the thymus has no follicles and the spleen has no afferent lymphatics.
  • Mixing up the two adrenal cortex zones that produce cortisol and aldosterone.
  • Writing “bone is dead tissue” — osteocytes are metabolically active and connected by canaliculi.

PEARL — In a viva or OSCE-style histology station, the examiner is usually testing whether you can connect a structure to a function and a disease. Practise every fact in a three-part sentence: “Structurally X, which allows Y, and when it fails you get Z.”

PART I — CELLS AND TECHNIQUES

1 · Histology & Its Methods of Study

pp. 1–16 · Part 1, pages 13–39 · Preparation of Tissues for Study · Light Microscopy · Electron Microscopy · Autoradiography · Cell & Tissue Culture · Enzyme Histochemistry · Visualizing Specific Molecules · Interpretation of Structures in Tissue Sections

The core idea

Histology explores the body’s tissues and how they are arranged to produce functional organs. Tissues have two interacting components: cells and extracellular matrix (ECM). The ECM is not packing material — it supports the cells, carries the fluid that brings nutrients and removes waste, and its molecules bind specific cell-surface receptors connected to structural components inside the cell, forming a continuum in which cells and matrix function together.

Organs form by an orderly combination of these tissues. Because cells and matrix components are small, histology depends on microscopes and molecular methods — and every image you will interpret has been fixed, dehydrated, cleared, embedded, sectioned, stained and mounted. Knowing what each step does tells you which appearances are real and which are artefacts.

Preparation of Tissues for Study

Step What the book says it does
Fixation Pieces of organ are placed as soon as possible in stabilising or cross-linking compounds. Formalin — a buffered isotonic solution of 37% formaldehyde — is widely used for light microscopy; glutaraldehyde for electron microscopy. Both react with the amine groups (NH₂) of proteins, preventing degradation by proteases; glutaraldehyde also cross-links adjacent proteins. Fixative must diffuse fully through the tissue, so organs are cut into small fragments, or fixed by vascular perfusion
Dehydration Water extracted gradually through increasing ethanol solutions, ending in 100% ethanol
Clearing Ethanol replaced by an organic solvent miscible with both alcohol and the embedding medium; the tissue becomes translucent
Infiltration Tissue placed in melted paraffin at 52–60 °C in an oven, which evaporates the clearing solvent
Embedding Paraffin allowed to harden in a small container at room temperature
Sectioning Block trimmed and cut on a microtome. Paraffin sections are typically 3–10 µm; EM requires sections less than 1 µm

The units the book expects you to handle: 1 µm = 1/1000 mm; 1 nm = 0.001 µm = 10⁻⁶ mm; 1 Å = 0.1 nm = 10⁻⁴ µm.

Plastic resins are used for both light and electron microscopy. They are infiltrated after dehydration and harden when cross-linking polymerisers are added; plastic embedding avoids the higher temperatures of paraffin and therefore avoids tissue distortion.

CLINICAL — Biopsies are tissue samples removed during surgery or routine procedures, fixed in vials of formalin. When the result is needed before the procedure ends — typically to know whether a growth is malignant before the patient is closed — the biopsy is frozen in liquid nitrogen, which preserves structure and hardens the tissue, and sectioned on a cryostat in a subfreezing cabinet. Freezing is also used for histochemistry of sensitive enzymes or small molecules, because freezing, unlike fixation, does not inactivate most enzymes; and because clearing solvents dissolve lipids, frozen sections are used whenever lipid-containing structures must be studied.

Staining

Most cells and extracellular material are completely colourless. Dyes behave like acidic or basic compounds and form electrostatic (salt) linkages with ionisable radicals of tissue macromolecules.

Term Meaning in the book Examples
Basophilic Anionic (net negative) components with affinity for basic dyes — nucleic acids, glycosaminoglycans Toluidine blue, alcian blue, methylene blue, haematoxylin
Acidophilic Cationic components with many ionised amino groups, staining with acidic dyes — mitochondria, secretory granules, collagen Eosin, orange G, acid fuchsin

H&E is the most common method. Haematoxylin stains DNA in the nucleus, RNA-rich cytoplasm, and cartilage matrix dark blue-purple; eosin stains other cytoplasmic structures and collagen pink and acts as the counterstain. Trichrome stains (eg, Masson) allow greater distinction among extracellular components.

The PAS reaction uses the hexose rings of polysaccharides and other carbohydrate-rich structures and stains them distinctly purple or magenta — the basis for demonstrating glycogen, mucin, and basement membranes. The Feulgen reaction is a modification of PAS that stains DNA specifically.

Enzyme digestion identifies what a stain is binding: pretreating a section with an enzyme that digests one substrate. Pretreatment with ribonuclease greatly reduces cytoplasmic basophilia with little effect on the nucleus, proving that the cytoplasmic staining depended on RNA.

Lipid-soluble dyes such as Sudan black reveal lipid-rich structures — provided the processing steps that remove lipids (heat and organic solvents) are avoided. Metal impregnation with silver salts visualises certain ECM fibres and specific elements in nervous tissue. The book’s Appendix lists the staining procedures used for most of its light micrographs.

Slide preparation, from fixation to observation, takes 12 hours to 2½ days, depending on tissue size, embedding medium, and staining method.

Light Microscopy

The bright-field microscope has three lens sets:

  • Condenser — collects and focuses a cone of light onto the slide.
  • Objective — enlarges and projects the image; routine objectives are ×4 (large field), ×10 (medium), ×40 (detail).
  • Eyepiece (ocular) — magnifies a further ×10, giving total magnifications of ×40, ×100, ×400. Total magnification = objective × ocular.

Resolving power — the smallest distance at which two structures are seen as separate — is the critical factor, and its maximum for a light microscope is about 0.2 µm, permitting clear images at 1000–1500×. Anything smaller or thinner than 0.2 µm (a single ribosome, a microfilament) cannot be distinguished, and two mitochondria closer than 0.2 µm appear as one. Resolution depends mainly on the objective lens; the ocular only enlarges and does not improve resolution. Magnification is of value only when accompanied by resolution.

Virtual microscopy converts a stained slide into high-resolution digital images captured in a grid at multiple magnifications, stored so a browser can navigate the original slide — now replacing glass slide collections in teaching laboratories.

Method Principle Use
Fluorescence Fluorophores emit longer-wavelength light when irradiated; bright on a dark background DAPI and Hoechst bind DNA (blue); acridine orange binds DNA and RNA and emits different colours, localising them separately; fluorescein-coupled probes and labelled antibodies
Phase-contrast Light changes speed through structures of different refractive index, producing contrast in transparent objects Living, unstained cultured cells; DIC (Nomarski optics) gives a more three-dimensional image
Confocal A laser point source and a pinhole aperture optically conjugated to the focal plane block out-of-focus light; the beam scans the specimen Thin optical sections, localisation with great precision, and 3-D reconstruction
Polarizing A polarising filter passes light vibrating in one direction; oriented macromolecules rotate it and appear bright against a dark background — birefringence Collagen, cellulose, microtubules, actin filaments; only material with repetitive periodic structure is seen

Digital cameras extend all of these methods: images can be quantified, enhanced, and analysed by software for features not visible through the eyepieces.

Electron Microscopy

The wavelength of an electron beam is far shorter than that of light, allowing a 1000-fold increase in resolution.

Transmission EM (TEM) resolves to about 3 nm — enough to view isolated particles magnified as much as 400,000×; tissue sections of 40–90 nm embedded in resin are routinely studied at up to about 120,000×. Electrons pass through the section to produce an image in black, white, and grey: areas that transmitted electrons appear electron lucent (bright), while denser areas or those binding heavy metal ions appear electron dense (dark). The column is configured like an upside-down light microscope, with electromagnetic lenses and a tungsten filament cathode accelerating electrons at 60–120 kV.

Scanning EM (SEM) images the surface in three dimensions.

Autoradiography

A radiolabelled precursor is incorporated into newly synthesised macromolecules and detected by a photographic emulsion applied over the section — so the silver grains mark the cellular sites where those macromolecules were made. The classic application is ³H-thymidine labelling of cells in S phase.

Cell & Tissue Culture

Living cells maintained outside the body, observed with phase-contrast or DIC microscopy, allow behaviour, differentiation, and drug responses to be studied directly — without fixation, which is precisely the advantage.

Enzyme Histochemistry

A substrate is converted by a specific enzyme into an insoluble, coloured product deposited at the site of the enzyme, localising enzyme activity within cells. Acid phosphatase for lysosomes is the standard example.

Visualizing Specific Molecules

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) depends on the specific reaction between an antigen and an antibody labelled with a visible marker — a fluorescent compound or peroxidase for light microscopy, gold particles for TEM.

Method How it works Why it is used
Direct IHC A labelled primary antibody binds the antigen directly Simple, fewer steps
Indirect IHC An unlabelled primary antibody is detected by labelled secondary antibodies More commonly used — the extra level of binding amplifies the signal and gives greater technical flexibility

In situ hybridisation (ISH) uses labelled cDNA probes to detect specific gene sequences or mRNAs microscopically.

Interpretation of Structures in Tissue Sections

Two rules from the book’s own section:

  1. Many processing, preparation, and staining steps introduce artefacts — spaces and precipitates not present in living tissue — and these must be recognised for what they are.
  2. Sections are essentially two-dimensional planes through three-dimensional structures, so what you see is a slice, not the whole object.

MICRO — Before identifying anything, decide whether it could be an artefact. A clear space around every cell is shrinkage; a straight edge through the tissue is a knife mark; granular debris is precipitated stain. True structure has irregular, biological boundaries.

TIP — Three numbers carry the chapter: 0.2 µm (light microscope resolution), 3–10 µm (paraffin section thickness), about 3 nm (TEM resolution). If a question asks why the electron microscope reveals organelles that the light microscope cannot, the answer is the shorter wavelength of the electron beam, not the magnification.

PEARL — The examiner’s favourite Chapter 1 chain is: dehydration precedes clearing, clearing makes the tissue translucent, and freezing avoids both — which is why frozen sections are used for rapid diagnosis, for enzymes, and for lipids.

2 · The Cytoplasm

pp. 17–52 · Part 1, pages 40–91 · Cell Differentiation · The Plasma Membrane · Cytoplasmic Organelles · The Cytoskeleton · Inclusions

The core idea

A cell is a compartmentalisation machine. Each organelle is a membrane-bound solution to a specific logistics problem: making protein, making lipid, generating ATP, degrading waste, or moving cargo. Learn the job, and the morphology follows automatically.

Cell Differentiation

The chapter opens with differentiation, because it explains why one genome produces hundreds of cell shapes. Cells become specialised by differential gene expression — the same DNA, different genes switched on. Differentiation is accompanied by changes in the amount and type of organelles, which is exactly what you recognise down a microscope: an erythroblast loses its nucleus, a plasma cell fills with rough ER, a muscle cell fills with contractile filaments.

Stem cells retain the capacity to divide and to produce differentiated progeny. They divide asymmetrically: one daughter renews the stem-cell pool, the other commits to differentiation. This mechanism explains tissue renewal in the gut, epidermis, and bone marrow (Chapter 3).

The Plasma Membrane

A 7.5–10 nm phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins — the fluid mosaic model. Amphipathic phospholipids form the bilayer spontaneously; cholesterol modulates fluidity; glycolipids and glycoproteins project outward as the glycocalyx, which mediates cell recognition, adhesion, and immune identity.

Protein class Position Function
Integral (transmembrane) Span the bilayer Channels, pumps, receptors, adhesion
Peripheral Attached to one face Signalling, cytoskeletal anchoring, enzymes

Lipid rafts are cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched microdomains that concentrate signalling molecules. Caveolae are small flask-shaped invaginations involved in uptake and signalling.

Transport across the membrane

  • Simple diffusion — small, non-polar molecules down a gradient.
  • Osmosis — water movement through the bilayer and aquaporins.
  • Facilitated diffusion — carrier or channel, down the gradient, no energy.
  • Active transport — against the gradient, ATP-dependent. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase exchanges 3 Na⁺ out for 2 K⁺ in and consumes a large fraction of the cell’s resting energy budget.
  • Endocytosis — phagocytosis (large particles, actin-driven), pinocytosis (fluid), receptor-mediated endocytosis (clathrin-coated pits; the classic example is LDL uptake, and its failure causes familial hypercholesterolaemia).
  • Exocytosis — vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane; requires SNARE proteins and calcium.
  • Transcytosis — endocytosis on one face plus exocytosis on the other; used by endothelial cells and antibodies crossing epithelia.

Cytoplasmic Organelles

Mitochondria. Double membrane; the inner membrane folds into cristae carrying the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. The matrix contains circular mitochondrial DNA, mitochondrial ribosomes, and calcium granules. Mitochondria are maternally inherited and semi-autonomous. Cells with huge energy demand (cardiac muscle, brown fat, proximal tubule, sperm midpiece) are packed with them. Cells that synthesise steroid hormone have mitochondria with tubular rather than lamellar cristae — a favourite EM question.

Ribosomes. 15–20 nm particles of rRNA plus protein, made of a large and small subunit. Free ribosomes make cytosolic protein for internal use; ribosomes bound to the rough ER make protein for secretion, membranes, or lysosomes. Polysomes are strings of ribosomes on one mRNA.

Rough ER (RER). Flattened cisternae studded with ribosomes. Sites of synthesis of secretory proteins, integral membrane proteins, and lysosomal enzymes. It is the structure responsible for basophilia — and in neurons, stacked RER plus polysomes form the Nissl bodies.

Smooth ER (SER). Tubular, ribosome-free. Three jobs: lipid and steroid synthesis, detoxification (hepatic cytochrome P450), and calcium storage (sarcoplasmic reticulum of muscle). Hepatocytes, adrenal cortical cells, Leydig cells, and the corpus luteum are rich in SER — which is why steroid-secreting cells look pale and foamy rather than blue.

Golgi apparatus. A stack of 4–8 flattened cisternae with a cis (forming) face receiving vesicles from the ER and a trans (maturing) face releasing vesicles. Functions: modification (glycosylation, sulfation, phosphorylation), sorting, and packaging. The trans-Golgi network is the dispatch centre routing proteins to secretory granules, lysosomes, or the membrane. Cells specialised for secretion (plasma cells, pancreatic acinar cells, goblet cells) have an obvious, often supra-nuclear Golgi.

Lysosomes. 0.05–0.5 µm vesicles of acid hydrolases at pH ≈ 5 maintained by a proton pump. Primary lysosomes are newly formed; secondary lysosomes are the product of fusion with a phagosome or autophagosome; residual bodies remain after digestion and accumulate as lipofuscin, the “wear-and-tear” pigment of long-lived cells such as cardiac myocytes and neurons.

Peroxisomes. Small vesicles containing catalase and oxidases. They perform β-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids, detoxify ethanol, and destroy H₂O₂.

Proteasomes. Cytosolic barrel-shaped complexes that degrade ubiquitin-tagged proteins.

The Cytoskeleton

Element Diameter Protein Main roles
Microfilament 6–8 nm Actin Microvilli core, cell cortex, stress fibres, cleavage furrow, muscle contraction
Intermediate filament 10 nm Keratin, vimentin, desmin, GFAP, neurofilament, lamin Mechanical strength; tissue-specific identity
Microtubule 25 nm Tubulin (α/β dimer) Spindle, cilia and flagella, intracellular transport

Intermediate filaments are the histologist’s best friend, because each cell type keeps its own: keratin in epithelium, vimentin in mesenchyme, desmin in muscle, GFAP in astrocytes, neurofilament in neurons, lamins in the nucleus. This is why immunohistochemistry can identify the origin of a tumour that looks undifferentiated.

Microtubules are polar. Kinesin walks cargo toward the plus end (anterograde, away from the cell body); dynein walks toward the minus end (retrograde, back to the cell body). Rabies and herpes viruses exploit dynein to travel to the neuronal soma.

Cilia and flagella have a 9 + 2 arrangement with dynein arms; the basal body has 9 + 0. Defective dynein causes primary ciliary dyskinesia, of which Kartagener syndrome (situs inversus, bronchiectasis, sinusitis, male infertility) is the classic form.

Inclusions

Non-membranous accumulations of metabolites or pigments: glycogen (PAS-positive, liver and muscle), lipid droplets (Sudan-positive), melanin, lipofuscin, haemosiderin, and crystals.

CLINICAL — Lysosomal storage diseases are the direct consequence of a missing acid hydrolase. In Tay–Sachs disease hexosaminidase A is absent, so GM2 ganglioside accumulates in neurons, producing developmental regression, a cherry-red macular spot, and death in early childhood. In I-cell disease the enzyme that tags hydrolases with mannose-6-phosphate is defective, so the enzymes are secreted instead of delivered to lysosomes.

MICRO — Learn to read a cell’s job from its EM. Abundant RER and a large Golgi = protein secretion. Abundant SER and lipid droplets, mitochondria with tubular cristae = steroid secretion. Many mitochondria and few other organelles = energy transduction. Numerous lysosomes and residual bodies = phagocytosis or long life. Bundles of filaments = mechanical work.

MNEMONIC — “Keratins Keep Epithelium; Vimentin is Versatile mesenchyme; Desmin Does muscle; GFAP Glues astrocytes; Neurofilaments are Neuronal.”

TIP — Two traps repeat every year. The Golgi modifies and sorts proteins; the RER makes them. And the mitochondrion is the powerhouse; the lysosome is the incinerator.

3 · The Nucleus

The core idea

The nucleus stores the genome, transcribes it, and duplicates it before division. Its morphology is a readout of transcriptional activity: dark, condensed chromatin means silent genes; pale, dispersed chromatin means active genes.

Components of the Nucleus

Nuclear envelope. Two membranes separated by a perinuclear cisterna, continuous with the rough ER. Beneath the inner membrane lies the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of lamins A, B, and C that supports the envelope and organises chromatin. Mutations in lamin A cause Hutchinson–Gilford progeria.

Nuclear pores. 3,000–4,000 per nucleus, each a nuclear pore complex regulating traffic. Small molecules diffuse; proteins and RNA require importins and exportins and a Ran-GTP gradient.

Chromatin. Each human cell holds about 2 metres of DNA — 3.2 billion base pairs — which must be packaged into a nucleus a few micrometres across. The DNA wraps around a core of eight histones (two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3, H4) with about 150 bp per turn, plus a larger H1 histone on the outside: that unit is the nucleosome, which in EM looks like 10-nm beads on a string. Further folding into 30-nm fibres and beyond involves nonhistone scaffold proteins and is less well understood. Heterochromatin is condensed and transcriptionally inactive; euchromatin is dispersed and active. Constitutive heterochromatin is permanently silent; facultative heterochromatin can be reactivated. The Barr body is a facultatively heterochromatic, inactivated X chromosome — one per somatic cell in a female.

Nucleolus. Not membrane-bound; a factory for ribosomal subunits, with a pars fibrosa (rRNA transcription) and a pars granulosa (ribosome assembly), organised around nucleolar organiser regions on the acrocentric chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22. Cells that make large amounts of protein have prominent nucleoli, and malignant cells often do too.

The Cell Cycle

Phase What happens Duration (typical cycling cell)
G1 Cell grows, checks nutrients and DNA integrity; restriction point ~10 h
S DNA replication; histones synthesised ~8 h
G2 Repair check, prepare for mitosis ~4 h
M Mitosis and cytokinesis ~1 h

Progression is driven by cyclin–CDK complexes. p53 halts the cycle at G1 to allow repair and triggers apoptosis if repair fails — hence “guardian of the genome”, and hence its mutation in more than half of human cancers. Rb is the G1/S brake lost in retinoblastoma. G0 is the quiescent state of cells that have left the cycle.

Mitosis

  • Prophase — chromosomes condense, centrioles move to opposite poles, the nuclear envelope begins to break down.
  • Metaphase — chromosomes align at the equatorial plate; the spindle attaches to kinetochores.
  • Anaphase — sister chromatids separate and are pulled poleward.
  • Telophase — nuclear envelopes re-form, chromosomes decondense.
  • Cytokinesis — an actin contractile ring pinches the cell in two.

Stem Cells & Tissue Renewal

  • Totipotent — can form the whole organism (zygote, early blastomeres).
  • Pluripotent — can form all three germ layers (inner cell mass; embryonic stem cells; induced pluripotent stem cells reprogrammed with the Yamanaka factors Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc).
  • Multipotent — form a restricted set of cell types (haematopoietic stem cell).
  • Unipotent — one cell type.

Tissues are labile (continuous turnover — epidermis, gut, marrow), stable (low turnover, can be triggered — liver, kidney, fibroblasts), or permanent (neurons, cardiac myocytes, lens fibres).

Meiosis

Two divisions produce four haploid cells from one diploid cell, with crossing over between homologous chromosomes during pachytene of prophase I, held together by the synaptonemal complex. Meiosis generates genetic diversity and halves the chromosome number; non-disjunction produces aneuploid gametes, the mechanism behind trisomy 21.

Apoptosis

Programmed cell death: caspases are activated, cytochrome c escapes mitochondria, the Bcl-2 family decides survival versus death, chromatin condenses, the cell shrinks and buds into apoptotic bodies that are phagocytosed without inflammation.

PART II — THE FOUR BASIC TISSUES

4 · Epithelial Tissue

pp. 71–95 · Part 1, pages 122–161 · Characteristic Features of Epithelial Cells · Specializations of the Apical Cell Surface · Types of Epithelia · Transport Across Epithelia · Renewal of Epithelial Cells

The core idea

Epithelial tissues are composed of closely aggregated polyhedral cells adhering strongly to one another and to a thin layer of ECM, forming cellular sheets that line the cavities of organs and cover the body surface. Epithelia (Gr. epi, upon + thele, nipple) line all external and internal surfaces of the body, and all substances that enter or leave an organ must cross this type of tissue. Everything else — the number of layers, the shape of the cells, the specialisations on the apex — is a solution to one of four problems: protection, absorption, secretion, or transport.

The four basic tissues (the book’s Table 4–1, and the framework for the whole subject):

Tissue Cells Extracellular matrix Main functions
Epithelial Aggregated polyhedral cells Small amount Lining of surface or body cavities; glandular secretion
Connective Several types of fixed and wandering cells Abundant amount Support and protection of tissues/organs
Muscle Elongated contractile cells Moderate amount Strong contraction; body movements
Nervous Elongated cells with extremely fine processes Very small amount Transmission of nerve impulses

Most organs are divided into the parenchyma — the cells responsible for the organ’s specialised functions — and the stroma, which plays a supporting role. Except in the brain and spinal cord, the stroma always consists of connective tissue. This single sentence answers a common question about why the CNS looks different from every other organ.

Characteristic Features of Epithelial Cells

The principal functions of epithelial tissues are covering, lining, and protecting surfaces (eg, epidermis), absorption (eg, the intestinal lining), and secretion (eg, parenchymal cells of glands). Specific cells of certain epithelia may be contractile (myoepithelial cells) or specialised sensory cells, such as those of taste buds or the olfactory epithelium.

Cell shape and nuclear shape correspond: columnar cells have elongated nuclei, squamous cells flattened nuclei, and cuboidal or pyramidal cells spherical nuclei. This is the fastest identification rule in the whole subject.

The defining features:

  1. Cells are closely apposed with minimal intercellular space.
  2. There is a free (apical) surface facing air, a lumen, or a cavity.
  3. Cells rest on a basement membrane.
  4. The tissue is avascular — nourishment arrives by diffusion from vessels in the underlying connective tissue.
  5. Cells are polarised: apical, lateral, and basal domains differ in structure and function.

Specializations of the Apical Cell Surface

Microvilli. Finger-like extensions with a core of actin filaments cross-linked by villin and fimbrin and anchored in a terminal web. They multiply surface area for absorption. In the proximal tubule and small intestine they form a brush border visible as a fuzzy eosinophilic rim. In the epididymis and inner ear, unusually long, stiff microvilli are called stereocilia — a misnomer, because they contain no microtubules.

Cilia. Motile, 9 + 2 microtubule axoneme with dynein arms, anchored to a basal body (9 + 0). They move mucus, cerebrospinal fluid, and the ovum. A primary cilium is a single non-motile 9 + 0 cilium acting as a sensory antenna — its failure underlies polycystic kidney disease.

Basal infoldings. Deep folds of the basal membrane packed with mitochondria — the “striated” appearance in the ducts of salivary glands and the distal tubule of the kidney. They are the morphological signature of active ion transport.

Two naming traps decide many marks. Endothelium and mesothelium are both simple squamous epithelium — one lines blood vessels, the other body cavities; they are not separate tissue types. And pseudostratified epithelium is truly a single layer in which every cell touches the basement membrane, even though the nuclei sit at different levels.

Transport Across Epithelia

An epithelium that moves material from one side to the other is built like a machine: entry at the apical face, exit at the basal face, with the tight junction preventing back-leak and mitochondrial-rich basal infoldings powering the pumps. In the intestinal enterocyte, glucose enters apically via Na⁺-glucose cotransport (SGLT1) and exits basolaterally via GLUT2; the Na⁺ gradient is maintained by basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. This is transcellular transport. Paracellular transport passes between cells and is limited by tight junctions.

Renewal of Epithelial Cells

Epithelia wear out. They are replaced from stem cells whose location varies with the tissue: the basal layer of stratified squamous epithelium, the crypt base of the intestine, the isthmus of the gastric gland, and the basal layer of the epidermis. Renewal rate is a diagnostic index: the intestinal epithelium turns over in days, the epidermis in weeks. Because the stem cells rest on the basement membrane, an epithelium can regenerate completely after superficial injury — but not after the basement membrane itself is destroyed.

Glands

  • Exocrine — connected to a surface by a duct. Endocrine — ductless, secreting into the blood.
  • Unicellular — the goblet cell (mucin; empty cup-shaped apex after secretion).
  • Simple — one unbranched duct (simple tubular = intestinal crypt; simple coiled tubular = eccrine sweat gland; simple branched acinar = sebaceous gland).
  • Compound — branched duct (salivary glands, pancreas, mammary gland).
  • Mechanism: merocrine (exocytosis — pancreas, sweat glands), apocrine (membrane and cytoplasm bud off — mammary gland), holocrine (whole cell disintegrates — sebaceous gland).

Myoepithelial cells sit between secretory cells and the basement membrane and contract to expel product — visible in mammary and salivary glands.

CLINICAL — Epithelial junctions are the target of autoimmune blistering. In pemphigus vulgaris, antibodies against desmoglein break desmosomes, so the epidermis detaches within the epithelium and thin-walled blisters form. In bullous pemphigoid, antibodies against hemidesmosomal antigens split the epidermis from the basement membrane and the blister is thick-walled and tense. The level of the split is dictated by which junction fails.

MICRO — Count nuclei, not cells. More than one nuclear level with a flat continuous surface = stratified. Then the top layer: flat and anucleate = keratinised; flat with nuclei = non-keratinised; dome-shaped and large = transitional; tall with cilia = respiratory.

MNEMONIC — “Simple for exchange, stratified for stress, transitional for stretch.” For cilia, remember 9 + 2 moves, 9 + 0 anchors.

TIP — Where is ciliated simple columnar epithelium found? The uterine tube — cilia move the ovum — and parts of the respiratory tract, where the epithelium is pseudostratified. Students often wrongly assign all ciliated epithelium to the lung.

The core idea

Connective tissue is the tissue whose defining feature is abundance of extracellular matrix. The cells are widely separated because the matrix — not the cells — does the mechanical work. Name the matrix and you have named the tissue’s job: collagen for tension, elastin for recoil, ground substance for compression and diffusion.

Collagen synthesis in five steps — a reliable structured-question answer:

  1. Translation on the RER as preprocollagen.
  2. Hydroxylation of proline and lysine — requires vitamin C.
  3. Glycosylation in the ER and Golgi.
  4. Procollagen is secreted and the propeptides cleaved → tropocollagen.
  5. Fibril assembly with cross-links formed by lysyl oxidase, which requires copper.

This pathway explains two diseases. Without vitamin C, hydroxylation fails and the result is scurvy — bleeding gums, poor wound healing, perifollicular haemorrhage. Without copper, cross-linking fails, as in Menkes disease. In Ehlers–Danlos syndrome the defect is in collagen processing or type; in Marfan syndrome the defect is in fibrillin, so the aorta dilates and the lens dislocates.

Ground Substance

An amorphous, hydrated gel of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), proteoglycans, and glycoproteins, which resists compression, lubricates, and controls diffusion.

  • GAGs are long unbranched polysaccharides with repeating disaccharides and many negative charges, which attract water: hyaluronic acid (unsulfated, not attached to protein, the backbone of the gel), chondroitin sulfate, keratan sulfate, dermatan sulfate, and heparan sulfate.
  • Proteoglycans are a core protein with GAG side chains — the classic is aggrecan, a very large molecule with a 250-kDa core protein heavily bound with chondroitin and keratan sulfate and joined to hyaluronan by a link protein. Its water-binding is why cartilage resists compression.
  • Perlecan is the key proteoglycan of all basal laminae — the one name that links ground substance to the basement membrane.
  • Glycoproteins — fibronectin (binds cells to matrix via integrins, important in wound healing) and laminin (basement membrane).

Types of Connective Tissue

  • Loose (areolar) — abundant ground substance, scattered fibres, many cells. Fills spaces, surrounds vessels, underlies epithelia.
  • Dense irregular — thick collagen bundles in many directions; dermis, organ capsules. Resists stress from all directions.
  • Dense regular — parallel collagen bundles; tendon and ligament. Resists stress in one direction.
  • Elastic — many elastic fibres; elastic ligament, vocal cord, large arteries.
  • Reticular — type III collagen network; lymphoid organs and marrow.
  • Mucous (Wharton’s jelly) — gelatinous, in the umbilical cord.
  • Embryonic — mesenchyme.

Repair. Inflammation → granulation tissue (proliferating capillaries, fibroblasts, macrophages) → collagen deposition → scar. Primary intention occurs in clean apposed wounds; secondary intention in larger, open wounds with more granulation and contraction.

CLINICAL — Scurvy is the purest histology-to-clinic link in the chapter: inadequate hydroxylation produces defective collagen, which produces fragile capillaries (bleeding gums, petechiae) and failed wound healing. Scleroderma is the opposite failure — excess collagen in skin and internal organs. Keloid is an overgrowth of dense collagen beyond the original wound margin, common in darker skin.

MICRO — Collagen is pink, wavy, and in bundles; elastin is sparse, often darker, and best shown by orcein. A plasma cell has an eccentric clock-face nucleus with a pale perinuclear halo; a mast cell’s granules stain metachromatically; the eosinophil’s bilobed nucleus is unmistakable.

MNEMONIC — “1 bone, 2 cartilage, 3 reticulin, 4 basement membrane.”

TIP — The most examined distinction here is dense regular versus dense irregular, and the answer is one word: direction. Regular = parallel = tendon = one axis of force. Irregular = random = dermis = many axes.

6 · Adipose Tissue

pp. 122–128 · Part 1, pages 197–207 · White Adipose Tissue · Brown Adipose Tissue

The core idea

Adipose tissue is an endocrine and metabolic organ disguised as a filling tissue. White fat stores energy; brown fat burns it for heat. Both are built from the same cell with a completely different internal architecture.

White Adipose Tissue

Unilocular. Each white adipocyte is a very large cell, 50–150 µm in diameter, containing one enormous lipid droplet that displaces the cytoplasm to a thin rim and flattens the nucleus against the plasmalemma — the signet-ring appearance. Adipose tissue is normally 15–20% of body weight in men and somewhat more in women. The lipid dissolves during routine processing, so on an H&E slide the cell appears as an empty circle with a thin border.

Functions: energy storage, insulation, cushioning of organs (retro-orbital, perirenal, mesenteric), and endocrine signalling.

Where the stored fat comes from — three sources, straight from the book: dietary fat carried as chylomicrons; triglycerides made in the liver and carried as VLDLs; and fatty acids and glycerol synthesised by the adipocytes themselves. Mobilisation is by hormone-sensitive lipase, activated by norepinephrine from the adrenal gland and sympathetic nerves, and by growth hormone; insulin inhibits that lipase and stimulates lipid synthesis. The cells are supported by reticular fibres, with connective tissue septa dividing the tissue into lobules.

Adipokines — the exam list:

  • Leptin — from adipocytes, acts on the hypothalamus to reduce appetite.
  • Adiponectin — increases insulin sensitivity; low in obesity.
  • Resistin, TNF-α, IL-6 — pro-inflammatory, part of why obesity is a chronic inflammatory state.
  • Aromatase — converts androgens to oestrogens; a peripheral oestrogen source in obesity.

Brown Adipose Tissue

Multilocular. Many small lipid droplets, a central round nucleus, and — the diagnostic feature — numerous mitochondria with abundant cristae containing uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1, thermogenin), which short-circuits the proton gradient so energy is released as heat instead of ATP.

Found in the fetus and newborn (interscapular, axillary, perirenal, along the great vessels), much reduced in adults. Sympathetic innervation drives it. “Beige” adipocytes appear within white fat after cold exposure.

CLINICAL — In lipodystrophy fat is absent or redistributed, and the metabolic consequences are paradoxical: severe insulin resistance with hypertriglyceridaemia, because the remaining fat cannot store lipid safely. In obesity the same storage limit is approached, so lipid spills into liver (steatosis), muscle, and pancreas — lipotoxicity. Brown fat is a therapeutic target: activating it increases energy expenditure, and PET imaging shows active depots in adults.

TIP — Do not confuse adipose tissue with steatosis. Adipocytes are a normal tissue with a capsule and vessels; steatosis is lipid accumulating inside hepatocytes or myocytes as vacuoles.

7 · Cartilage

pp. 129–137 · Part 1, pages 208–217 · Hyaline Cartilage · Elastic Cartilage · Fibrocartilage · Cartilage Formation, Growth, & Repair

The core idea

Cartilage is a tough, resilient connective tissue that structurally supports soft tissues — notably in the respiratory tract — and provides cushioned, low-friction surfaces in joints. Chondrocytes are a small percentage of the tissue’s mass, which is mainly a flexible extracellular matrix of collagen plus abundant proteoglycans, notably aggrecan, which binds a large amount of water. Cartilage always lacks blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves, but usually has a vascularised perichondrium of dense connective tissue. It is avascular and aneural, which explains both its durability under load and its disastrously poor healing.

The shared architecture

  • Cells: chondroblasts (immature, at the surface) and chondrocytes (mature, in lacunae).
  • Matrix: territorial matrix immediately around the lacuna (rich in proteoglycan, basophilic) and interterritorial matrix between cells (more collagen, eosinophilic).
  • Isogenous groups — clusters of 2–8 chondrocytes from one parent cell, indicating recent interstitial growth.
  • Perichondrium — fibrous outer layer plus chondrogenic inner layer; present on most cartilage, absent on fibrocartilage and on articular surfaces.

Hyaline Cartilage

The commonest type and the template for most of the fetal skeleton. Glassy, homogeneous matrix; collagen makes up 40% of hyaline cartilage’s dry weight, all of it embedded in a hydrated gel of proteoglycans and structural glycoproteins, with more proteoglycan and less collagen immediately around the lacunae (the territorial matrix). Perichondrium is present except on articular surfaces and the epiphyses of growing long bones, where it forms the smooth, low-friction bearing surface of joints. Found in the trachea, bronchi, nose, ribs, epiphyseal plate, and articular surfaces.

Elastic Cartilage

Type II collagen plus a network of elastic fibres, giving flexibility with recoil. Found in the epiglottis, pinna, external auditory canal, and Eustachian tube — that is, everywhere something must bend and spring back.

Fibrocartilage

Type I and type II collagen in coarse bundles, with chondrocytes arranged in parallel rows between them. No perichondrium. Found where compressive and tensile forces meet: intervertebral disc, symphysis pubis, menisci, temporomandibular joint. The disc is the perfect example, with an inner nucleus pulposus (notochord remnant, rich in hyaluronic acid) and an outer annulus fibrosus of concentric fibrocartilage lamellae.

Cartilage Formation, Growth, & Repair

  • Appositional growth — new cartilage added at the periphery by chondroblasts of the perichondrium. Increases width.
  • Interstitial growth — chondrocytes divide within lacunae and secrete matrix, pushing cells apart. Increases length; possible only in young cartilage.

Because cartilage is avascular, nutrition depends on diffusion from the perichondrium or synovial fluid. This limits repair: defects fill with fibrocartilage rather than hyaline cartilage, and that patch is mechanically inferior.

CLINICAL — Osteoarthritis is a failure of the chondrocyte to maintain matrix: proteoglycans are depleted, water content rises, collagen fibrils fragment, and the surface fissures. Because hyaline cartilage has no vessels and no nerves, pain comes late and from the underlying bone, capsule, and synovium. Rheumatoid arthritis is different: an autoimmune synovitis destroys cartilage from the surface inward, producing a pannus.

MICRO — Identify cartilage by the lacuna: a cell alone in a clear space within a solid matrix. Then the matrix: glassy purple with cells in clusters = hyaline; wavy dark fibres = elastic; thick parallel pink bundles with cells in rows = fibrocartilage.

TIP — Two marks are lost repeatedly: hyaline cartilage contains type II collagen, not type I, and fibrocartilage has no perichondrium, which is why disc injuries persist.

8 · Bone

The core idea

Bone is a living composite material. Its stiffness comes from mineral (hydroxyapatite), its toughness from collagen, and its capacity to adapt comes from four cell types that continuously demolish and rebuild it. Every skeletal disease is a failure of one of those four cells, or of the balance between them.

The osteoclast is the exception examiners always test. Every other bone cell is mesenchymal; the osteoclast is haematopoietic. It dissolves mineral by pumping H⁺ and digests collagen with cathepsin K.

The RANK–RANKL–OPG axis: osteoblasts express RANKL, which binds RANK on osteoclast precursors and drives differentiation; they also secrete osteoprotegerin (OPG), a decoy receptor that blocks RANKL. PTH shifts the balance toward resorption; oestrogen suppresses it.

Bone Matrix

  • Inorganic (~65%) — calcium phosphate as hydroxyapatite crystals; compressive strength.
  • Organic (~35%) — type I collagen (90% of the organic fraction), osteocalcin, osteonectin, osteopontin, proteoglycans, growth factors; tensile strength.

Remove the mineral and bone becomes flexible; remove the collagen and it becomes brittle. Bone is strong because it is both at once.

Periosteum & Endosteum

Periosteum — outer fibrous layer plus inner osteogenic (cambium) layer; absent on articular surfaces and at tendon insertions. Endosteum lines the marrow cavity. Both are sources of osteoprogenitor cells for growth and repair.

Types of Bone

Compact (cortical) bone — dense, on the surface; built of osteons (Haversian systems): concentric lamellae around a central Haversian canal containing vessels and nerves, with osteocytes in lacunae linked by canaliculi, and Volkmann canals running transversely.

Spongy (cancellous) bone — a network of trabeculae with marrow between them, oriented along lines of mechanical stress. No true osteons.

Woven versus lamellar. Woven bone is disorganised and produced rapidly in the fetus, fracture callus, and disease (Paget, osteosarcoma). Lamellar bone is ordered and mechanically superior; adult bone is lamellar but always being remodelled through basic multicellular units (BMUs).

Osteogenesis

Intramembranous ossification — mesenchyme condenses and differentiates directly into osteoblasts, depositing bone in a fibrous membrane. Produces the flat bones of the skull, mandible, and clavicle. No cartilage template.

Endochondral ossification — a hyaline cartilage model is replaced by bone. This is how long bones form and grow in length. The epiphyseal plate has five zones:

Zone (from epiphysis toward diaphysis) What happens
1. Reserve (resting) Small, inactive chondrocytes
2. Proliferative Chondrocytes divide in columns — the actual growth in length
3. Hypertrophic Chondrocytes enlarge and the matrix calcifies
4. Calcified cartilage Chondrocytes die; matrix calcifies
5. Ossification Osteoblasts deposit bone on the calcified spicules

Growth in width is appositional, by periosteal osteoblasts. The primary ossification centre appears in the diaphysis; secondary centres in the epiphyses. When the plate ossifies, an epiphyseal line remains and length growth stops.

Bone Remodeling & Repair

Fracture → haematoma → inflammation → soft callus (fibrocartilage) → hard callus (woven bone) → remodelling to lamellar bone. Immobilisation and blood supply decide the outcome. Wolff law — bone is laid down where it is loaded and resorbed where it is not — explains both trabecular architecture and the bone loss of bed rest.

Joints

Fibrous joints (synarthroses) — bones united by dense connective tissue, no cavity (sutures of the skull, gomphosis of a tooth).

Cartilaginous joints (amphiarthroses) — bones united by cartilage: primary by hyaline cartilage (epiphyseal plate, first sternocostal joint); secondary by fibrocartilage (intervertebral disc, symphysis pubis).

Synovial joints (diarthroses) — a joint cavity between hyaline articular cartilage on each bone, enclosed by a capsule of dense connective tissue lined by synovial membrane, which secretes synovial fluid for lubrication and nutrition of the avascular cartilage. Some joints add a meniscus (fibrocartilage), a labrum, or a fat pad. Because articular cartilage has no perichondrium and no vessels, it heals hardly at all — the anatomical basis of degenerative joint disease.

CLINICAL — Six to learn as a set. Osteoporosis — reduced bone mass with normal mineralisation; oestrogen deficiency drives it, trabeculae are lost first, fragility fractures follow. Rickets/osteomalacia — unmineralised osteoid; in children the growth plate widens and deforms (rachitic rosary, bowed legs). Paget disease — chaotic excessive remodelling with mosaic woven bone. Osteopetrosis — osteoclast failure, dense but brittle bone with a crowded marrow cavity. Achondroplasia — failure of chondrocyte proliferation in the plate, short limbs with normal trunk. Osteogenesis imperfecta — defective type I collagen, brittle bones, blue sclerae.

MICRO — On a ground section, concentric lamellae around a central canal = an osteon = compact bone. On a decalcified H&E section, osteoblasts are the row of cuboidal cells on the surface and osteocytes sit in lacunae. A multinucleated cell in a scalloped pit is an osteoclast resorbing.

MNEMONIC — “OsteoBlast Builds, OsteoClast Chews, OsteoCytes Communicate.” For the epiphyseal plate, “Rest, Proliferate, Hypertrophy, Calcify, Ossify” — read from the joint outward.

TIP — The commonest error is saying osteoclasts are mesenchymal. They are haematopoietic, from the monocyte–macrophage line. The second is reversing the epiphyseal zones: growth happens at the epiphyseal side, ossification at the diaphyseal side.

9 · Nerve Tissue & the Nervous System

pp. 161–192 · Development of Nerve Tissue · Neurons · Glial Cells & Neuronal Activity · Central Nervous System · Peripheral Nervous System · Neural Plasticity & Regeneration

The core idea

Nervous tissue is built for rapid signalling over distance. The neuron is a polarised cell with a receiving end (dendrites and soma), a conducting cable (axon), and a transmitting end (synapse). Everything else — myelin, glia, barriers — exists to make that signalling faster, safer, and better supplied.

Development of Nerve Tissue

The nervous system arises from the neural plate, which folds into the neural tube (giving the CNS) while neural crest cells migrate away to form the peripheral neurons, Schwann cells, satellite cells, adrenal medulla, melanocytes, and much of the facial skeleton. This embryology explains three exam facts: the CNS is lined by ependyma derived from the tube; Schwann cells are neural crest; and the adrenal medulla is neural crest while the cortex is mesoderm.

Neurons

  • Perikaryon (soma) — large, pale nucleus with a prominent nucleolus and abundant Nissl bodies (stacked RER and polysomes). Nissl substance is absent from the axon hillock — a useful landmark.
  • Dendrites — short, branched, receptive; contain Nissl bodies.
  • Axon — one per neuron, arises at the axon hillock, contains no Nissl bodies, specialised for conduction.
  • Axonal transport — kinesin anterograde; dynein retrograde. Rabies, herpes, and tetanus toxin exploit retrograde transport.

Classification: multipolar (most CNS neurons and motor neurons), bipolar (retina, olfactory epithelium), pseudounipolar (sensory ganglia), anaxonic. Also by function — sensory, motor, interneuron.

The synapse. A chemical synapse has a presynaptic terminal with synaptic vesicles and mitochondria, a synaptic cleft (~20–30 nm), and a postsynaptic membrane with receptors. Transmission is unidirectional and modifiable. Electrical synapses (gap junctions) are fast and bidirectional.

Myelination. In the PNS a Schwann cell wraps one axon segment; in the CNS an oligodendrocyte wraps several. The gaps are nodes of Ranvier, where voltage-gated Na⁺ channels cluster, so the impulse jumps by saltatory conduction. Velocity rises with axon diameter and myelin thickness.

Central Nervous System

  • Cerebral cortex — six layers; pyramidal cells are the output neurons; grey matter outside.
  • Cerebellar cortex — three layers: molecular, Purkinje (large flask-shaped cells, the only output), and granular.
  • Spinal cord — grey matter in an H-shaped core, white matter outside; anterior horn motor, posterior horn sensory. This is the reverse of the cerebral cortex.
  • Meninges — dura, arachnoid, pia; CSF in the subarachnoid space.
  • Choroid plexus — produces CSF.
  • Blood–brain barrier — tight junctions between endothelial cells, reinforced by astrocyte end-feet and pericytes. Absent in the circumventricular organs (area postrema, median eminence, pineal, posterior pituitary), which is how circulating hormones reach the brain.

Peripheral Nervous System

  • Sensory ganglia — pseudounipolar neurons with satellite cells; no synapses inside the ganglion.
  • Autonomic ganglia — multipolar neurons, synapses present.
  • Peripheral nerve — axons in fascicles surrounded by perineurium, the whole nerve by epineurium, individual fibres by endoneurium.

Neural Plasticity & Regeneration

After axotomy the distal stump undergoes Wallerian degeneration; the soma shows central chromatolysis; Schwann cells form bands of Büngner to guide regrowth; regeneration proceeds at about 1 mm/day. CNS axons regenerate poorly because of inhibitory molecules (Nogo, MAG) and glial scarring. Plasticity — the remodelling of synapses with use — is the basis of learning and of rehabilitation after injury.

CLINICAL — Multiple sclerosis is autoimmune demyelination in the CNS. Guillain–Barré syndrome is demyelination in the PNS, ascending from the legs. Parkinson disease is loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra with Lewy bodies. Alzheimer disease shows neurofibrillary tangles and amyloid plaques. Poliomyelitis destroys anterior horn motor neurons and produces flaccid paralysis.

MICRO — Identify neurons by the large pale nucleus, prominent nucleolus, and clumped Nissl bodies. The axon hillock is the pale cone where Nissl substance stops. In peripheral nerve, look for wavy bundles of axons with myelin rings and connective tissue sheaths. In the cerebellar cortex, the single row of enormous Purkinje cells is unmistakable.

TIP — Oligodendrocyte = CNS, several axons. Schwann cell = PNS, one segment of one axon. And microglia are the only glia of mesodermal origin.

10 · Muscle Tissue

pp. 193–214 · Skeletal Muscle · Cardiac Muscle · Smooth Muscle · Regeneration of Muscle Tissue

The core idea

Muscle converts chemical energy into directed force. All three types use the same actin–myosin interaction, but arrange it differently: striated and voluntary (skeletal), striated and involuntary (cardiac), unstriated and involuntary (smooth).

Skeletal Muscle

Organisation, large to small: muscle → fascicles → fibres → myofibrils → myofilaments. Each fibre is a long, cylindrical, multinucleated syncytium with peripheral nuclei, produced by fusion of myoblasts. Satellite cells persist beneath the basal lamina and mediate limited regeneration.

The sarcomere, bounded by two Z lines:

A band Thick filaments (myosin) Constant length
I band Thin filaments (actin) only Shortens
H band Thick filaments only, no overlap Shortens
Z line Anchor for actin Moves closer
M line Centre of the A band —

Sliding filament mechanism: myosin heads bind actin, hydrolyse ATP, and pivot, pulling thin filaments toward the M line. The A band never changes length.

Control. The sarcoplasmic reticulum stores Ca²⁺; T-tubules invaginate at the A–I junction, forming triads (one T-tubule + two terminal cisternae). An action potential releases Ca²⁺, which binds troponin C, moves tropomyosin off the myosin-binding site, and contraction begins. Relaxation requires Ca²⁺ reuptake.

Innervation. The motor end plate — acetylcholine binds nicotinic receptors, depolarises the sarcolemma, and triggers contraction. One motor neuron plus all the fibres it supplies is a motor unit.

Fibre types:

I (slow, red) Slow Oxidative Resistant Red
IIa (fast, oxidative-glycolytic) Fast Mixed Intermediate Intermediate
IIx/IIb (fast, glycolytic) Fast Glycolytic Fast fatigue White

Sensory receptors. Muscle spindles (intrafusal fibres, Ia afferents, gamma motor neurons) sense length and stretch; Golgi tendon organs sense tension.

Cardiac Muscle

  • Striated, branched fibres, one or two central nuclei.
  • Intercalated discs — the diagnostic feature — contain fascia adherens (anchors actin), desmosomes (mechanical strength), and gap junctions (electrical coupling), making the heart a functional syncytium.
  • Diads at the Z line (one T-tubule + one cisterna) rather than triads at the A–I junction.
  • Abundant mitochondria and lipid droplets; highly oxidative; resistant to fatigue.
  • Purkinje fibres — specialised conducting cells, larger and paler, with fewer myofibrils; they conduct rapidly through the ventricles.
  • Regeneration is essentially absent; damage is repaired by fibrous scar.

Smooth Muscle

  • Spindle-shaped cells with a single central nucleus and no striations.
  • Contractile apparatus: dense bodies (analogous to Z lines) linked by desmin and actin.
  • No troponin. Calcium binds calmodulin, which activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK).
  • Caveolae replace T-tubules; the SR is sparse and Ca²⁺ entry across the membrane matters more.
  • Unitary (single-unit) smooth muscle — gap junctions, spontaneous rhythmicity, in gut, uterus, bladder. Multiunit — each cell separately innervated, in the iris and vas deferens.
  • Capable of hypertrophy and hyperplasia (uterine myometrium in pregnancy).

Regeneration of Muscle Tissue

Skeletal muscle regenerates weakly, from satellite cells, and only if the basal lamina survives; otherwise the defect fills with fibrous tissue. Cardiac muscle does not regenerate — infarcted myocardium is replaced by scar. Smooth muscle can both hypertrophy and, in the uterus, proliferate.

CLINICAL — Duchenne muscular dystrophy is an X-linked defect of dystrophin, which links the sarcolemma to the extracellular matrix; without it, contraction tears the membrane, fibres die, and fat and fibrous tissue replace muscle (pseudohypertrophy). Myasthenia gravis is autoantibody blockade of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, producing fatigable weakness. Cardiac hypertrophy is an adaptation to pressure overload; beyond a point the myocytes outgrow their blood supply and fail.

MICRO — Look first for striations; if present, look at the nuclei. Many nuclei at the edge = skeletal. One or two central nuclei with dark transverse bands = cardiac (intercalated discs). No striations and central nuclei in spindle-shaped cells = smooth.

MNEMONIC — “Skeletal = Surface nuclei. Cardiac = Central nucleus, Cross-striations, interCalated discs. Smooth = Spindle, Single central nucleus, no Striations.”

TIP — The A band does not shorten. This is asked more often than any other muscle fact: I and H shorten, A stays constant, Z lines approach.

11 · The Circulatory System

pp. 215–236 · Heart · Tissues of the Vascular Wall · Vasculature · Lymphatic Vascular System

The core idea

Every vessel in the body is a variation on one three-layered tube. What changes between an aorta and a capillary is how much muscle and elastic tissue each layer contains — decided entirely by the pressure the vessel must withstand and the job it must do.

Heart

  • Endocardium — endothelium + subendothelial connective tissue + a layer of Purkinje fibres; continuous with the intima of vessels.
  • Myocardium — the thickest layer; cardiac muscle in complex spirals, atria thinner than ventricles, left ventricle thickest.
  • Epicardium — mesothelium + connective tissue + adipose; the visceral layer of the pericardium.
  • Cardiac skeleton — dense irregular connective tissue rings that anchor the valves and electrically insulate atria from ventricles.
  • Valves — endocardium folded over a core of dense connective tissue; avascular. The mitral and tricuspid have chordae tendineae and papillary muscles.

Tissues of the Vascular Wall

Layer Contents Function
Intima Endothelium, basal lamina, subendothelial connective tissue, internal elastic lamina (in arteries) Non-thrombogenic surface, exchange, barrier
Media Smooth muscle circularly arranged, elastic lamellae (in elastic arteries), external elastic lamina Tone and compliance
Adventitia Loose connective tissue, vasa vasorum, nervi vascularis Anchorage and supply of the wall

The rule of the wall: the thicker the media, the higher the pressure the vessel handles. Arteries have a thick media; veins a thin media and a thick adventitia. That single ratio lets you identify a vessel on any slide.

Microcirculation. Arteriole → metarteriole → capillary bed → venule, with precapillary sphincters regulating flow. Arteriovenous anastomoses shunt blood directly, important in skin thermoregulation.

Lymphatic Vascular System

Begins as blind-ended lymphatic capillaries with overlapping endothelial flaps and anchoring filaments, which open when interstitial pressure rises. They converge into collecting vessels with valves, then nodes, trunks, and the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct. Lymphatics have no continuous basal lamina and no fenestrations — which is exactly why tumour cells and bacteria enter them so easily.

CLINICAL — Atherosclerosis begins with endothelial dysfunction: LDL crosses the endothelium, is oxidised, and provokes monocyte recruitment. Macrophages become foam cells, a fatty streak forms, smooth muscle migrates into the intima, and a fibrous cap covers a necrotic core. Rupture of a thin cap exposes thrombogenic material and produces the acute coronary syndrome. Aneurysm follows destruction of medial elastic fibres. Varicose veins follow valve failure. Lymphoedema follows lymphatic obstruction — classically after axillary node clearance.

MICRO — Arteries: round lumen, thick wall, corrugated internal elastic lamina. Veins: irregular collapsed lumen, thin media. In elastic arteries the media shows wavy pink bands with no orderly muscle coat; in muscular arteries the media is uniform smooth muscle.

TIP — The three capillaries are distinguished by continuity, not size: continuous (tight), fenestrated (pores), sinusoidal (gaps wide enough for a red cell). Liver, spleen, marrow = sinusoidal. Kidney glomerulus and intestine = fenestrated. Brain = continuous with tight junctions.

12 · Blood

pp. 237–253 · Composition of Plasma · Blood Cells

The core idea

Blood is connective tissue with a liquid matrix. Its cells all derive from one stem cell, and their morphology is a direct readout of their job: red for oxygen, granular white for killing, agranular white for immunity, and fragments for clotting.

Composition of Plasma

About 55% of blood volume. Water (90%), plasma proteins — albumin (osmotic pressure, carrier), globulins (transport, immunoglobulins), fibrinogen (clotting) — plus electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste. Serum is plasma minus fibrinogen (i.e., after clotting).

Blood Cells

Erythrocytes

  • Biconcave disc, 7–8 µm, no nucleus, no organelles — maximum haemoglobin per cell.
  • 120-day lifespan; removed by splenic macrophages; the globin is recycled and the haem converted to bilirubin.
  • Shape maintained by spectrin and ankyrin; defects produce hereditary spherocytosis.
  • Haemoglobin — four globin chains, each with a haem group carrying Fe²⁺. Adult HbA (α₂β₂) and fetal HbF (α₂γ₂, higher affinity for O₂).
  • Reticulocytes are young red cells with residual RNA, appearing polychromatic; a raised count signals active marrow output.
  • Normal values: Hb 13–17 g/dL (male), 12–15 g/dL (female); haematocrit ~40–50%; MCV 80–100 fL.

Eosinophil 2–4% 10–14 µm Bilobed Bright orange-red granules Parasites and allergy; major basic protein

Neutrophil granules: azurophilic (primary) granules contain myeloperoxidase and defensins; specific (secondary) granules contain lysozyme and lactoferrin. The neutrophil dies after phagocytosis; the accumulation of dead neutrophils and debris is pus.

Platelets are 2–3 µm anucleate fragments of megakaryocyte cytoplasm, with a peripheral clear zone (hyalomere) and a central granular zone (granulomere). They adhere, activate, and aggregate to form the primary haemostatic plug; von Willebrand factor links them to collagen.

CLINICAL — The differential count is a diagnostic instrument. Neutrophilia suggests bacterial infection or steroid use; eosinophilia parasites or allergy; lymphocytosis viral infection or chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; pancytopenia marrow failure. In sickle cell disease a single glutamic acid → valine substitution in β-globin allows deoxygenated HbS to polymerise; the cell sickles, occludes small vessels, and is removed by the spleen.

MICRO — Count lobes, not granules. Neutrophil: 3–5 lobes. Eosinophil: exactly two, orange granules. Basophil: granules so dark the nucleus is hidden. Monocyte: kidney-shaped nucleus, largest cell. Lymphocyte: round dense nucleus, almost no cytoplasm.

MNEMONIC — “Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas” = Neutrophil, Lymphocyte, Monocyte, Eosinophil, Basophil — in descending order of abundance.

TIP — Eosinophil granules stain with eosin because they are basic proteins — the naming is the reverse of what students expect. The basophil’s granules contain heparin and histamine, so the “basic” cell is the one full of allergy mediators.

13 · Hemopoiesis

pp. 254–266 · Stem Cells, Growth Factors, & Differentiation · Bone Marrow · Maturation of Erythrocytes · Maturation of Granulocytes · Maturation of Agranulocytes · Origin of Platelets

The core idea

All blood cells descend from one haematopoietic stem cell, and the pathway from that cell to a mature erythrocyte, granulocyte, or platelet is a sequence of named stages you can recognise by decreasing nucleus, increasing cytoplasm, and changing colour. It is the most examinable lineage in histology because every stage has a name.

Sites of haemopoiesis

Period Site
First trimester Yolk sac (mesoblastic phase)
Second trimester Liver and spleen (hepatic phase)
Third trimester and after birth Bone marrow (myeloid phase)

In the adult, active red marrow persists in the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis, skull, and proximal femur. The rest is yellow (fatty) marrow, which can revert to red marrow under demand — why chronic anaemia produces marrow expansion and frontal bossing.

Stem Cells, Growth Factors, & Differentiation

The haematopoietic stem cell is CD34⁺, self-renewing, and multipotent, sitting in a niche of stromal cells, osteoblasts, and sinusoids. Differentiation is driven by growth factors, examinable by name:

Factor Target
Erythropoietin (EPO) Erythroid progenitors (kidney, some liver)
Thrombopoietin (TPO) Megakaryocytes (liver)
G-CSF Granulocyte production
GM-CSF, M-CSF Granulocyte–monocyte and monocyte lineages
IL-3 Early multipotent progenitors
IL-5 Eosinophils
SCF (stem cell factor) Stem cell maintenance

Bone Marrow

Between the trabeculae lie haemopoietic cords and sinusoids with a discontinuous endothelium, so mature cells can enter the circulation. The myeloid:erythroid (M:E) ratio is normally 2:1 to 4:1; it shifts toward erythroid in haemolytic anaemia and toward myeloid in infection or leukaemia.

Maturation of Erythrocytes

Proerythroblast → basophilic erythroblast → polychromatophilic erythroblast → orthochromatic normoblast → reticulocyte → erythrocyte.

The cell shrinks, the nucleus condenses and is extruded, haemoglobin accumulates so the cytoplasm shifts from blue to pink, and the nucleolus disappears. The sequence takes about 5–7 days. EPO drives it; iron, vitamin B₁₂, and folate are required.

Maturation of Granulocytes

Myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band cell → segmented neutrophil (or eosinophil/basophil).

Primary (azurophilic) granules appear at the promyelocyte stage, specific granules at the myelocyte stage, the nucleus indents at the metamyelocyte stage, and becomes a C-shaped band before segmenting. A left shift — more bands and younger forms in the peripheral blood — indicates accelerated production, typically in infection.

Maturation of Agranulocytes

Lymphopoiesis begins in the marrow for B cells and in the thymus for T cells. Monopoiesis produces monocytes that become tissue macrophages. Both pass through stages in which the nucleus becomes less lobated, the cytoplasm more abundant, and the chromatin pattern characteristic of the mature cell.

Origin of Platelets

Megakaryoblasts → megakaryocytes, which become polyploid and develop demarcation membranes that delineate future platelet territories; platelets bud from the cytoplasm under TPO control.

CLINICAL — Aplastic anaemia is marrow failure with an empty, fatty marrow and pancytopenia. Leukaemia is the opposite: marrow packed with malignant blasts, crowding out normal lineages and producing anaemia, infection, and bleeding. Megaloblastic anaemia (B₁₂ or folate deficiency) shows nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony — the nucleus matures more slowly than the cytoplasm, so cells are large and hypersegmented. Iron deficiency produces microcytic hypochromic cells because haemoglobin synthesis lags behind cell division.

MICRO — Identify the lineage by nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and granule colour. Early cells have a large nucleus, a thin rim of basophilic cytoplasm, and a nucleolus. As cells mature, the ratio falls, granules appear, and the nucleus condenses. Maturation moves in one direction: nucleus shrinks, cytoplasm grows, colour shifts from blue to pink or from blue to granular.

TIP — Two facts carry disproportionate marks: EPO comes from the kidney (peritubular interstitial cells), and TPO comes mostly from the liver. Students routinely invert these.

14 · The Immune System & Lymphoid Organs

The core idea

The immune system is a set of organs arranged to bring antigen to lymphocytes. Each organ is a different solution to the same problem: how to filter a fluid (blood, lymph, or air/food), trap antigen, and present it to the right cell.

Cytokines

Small signalling proteins secreted by immune cells that coordinate the response. The ones worth naming: interleukins (IL-1, IL-2 for T-cell proliferation, IL-4 for IgE and Th2 responses, IL-5 for eosinophils, IL-6 for acute-phase response), interferons (antiviral; IFN-γ activates macrophages), TNF-α (inflammation, fever, cachexia), chemokines (directed leucocyte migration), and colony-stimulating factors (haemopoiesis, Chapter 13). Cytokines act locally (paracrine) or on the secreting cell (autocrine), and their Storm-like excess produces the systemic inflammatory response.

Antigens & Antibodies

Antigens are molecules recognised by the adaptive immune system; epitopes are the specific parts bound by a receptor. Antibodies are immunoglobulins:

Class Structure Main role
IgG Monomer Most abundant; crosses the placenta; opsonises
IgM Pentamer First responder; complement activation
IgA Dimer with secretory component Mucosal secretions
IgE Monomer Mast-cell binding; allergy and parasites
IgD Monomer B-cell receptor

Antigen Presentation

T cells cannot see free antigen. They recognise peptide displayed on MHC molecules:

  • MHC class I — on all nucleated cells; presents endogenous peptide (from intracellular proteins, including viral) to CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells.
  • MHC class II — on antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells); presents exogenous peptide taken up by endocytosis to CD4⁺ helper T cells.

This pairing — CD4/MHC II, CD8/MHC I — is the most examined immunology fact in the chapter.

Cells of Adaptive Immunity

T lymphocytes mature in the thymus and are responsible for cell-mediated immunity: CD4⁺ helpers orchestrate B cells and macrophages; CD8⁺ cytotoxic cells kill infected cells. B lymphocytes mature in bone marrow, carry surface immunoglobulin, and differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibody and memory cells. NK cells kill without prior sensitisation and without MHC restriction.

Thymus

  • Capsule with trabeculae dividing it into lobules, each with a cortex and a medulla.
  • No follicles. The cortex is densely packed with developing thymocytes and epithelial reticular cells; the medulla is paler and contains Hassall corpuscles — concentric whorls of epithelial cells, unique to the thymus.
  • Blood–thymus barrier in the cortex prevents antigens from reaching developing T cells.
  • Function: T-cell maturation and selection (positive and negative selection, establishing self-tolerance).
  • Involutes after puberty, replaced by adipose tissue.

Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue

Tonsils (palatine, lingual, pharyngeal/adenoid, tubal) — no capsule, no afferent lymphatics, epithelium invaginates into crypts. Peyer’s patches in the ileum; appendix; MALT throughout the gut, respiratory tract, and genitourinary tract.

  • Red pulp — cords of Billroth and sinusoids lined by elongated endothelial cells; removes aged or damaged red cells and platelets.

CLINICAL — DiGeorge syndrome is thymic aplasia (third and fourth pharyngeal pouches) with T-cell deficiency and hypocalcaemia. Severe combined immunodeficiency is failure of both arms. Hodgkin lymphoma spreads contiguously through nodes and shows Reed–Sternberg cells. Splenectomy increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms — pneumococcus, meningococcus, Haemophilus influenzae — because the spleen’s marginal zone is the first line against polysaccharide capsules.

The core idea

The gut is one tube with four concentric layers, and every region is a modification of those four layers for a specific job: grinding, acidifying, absorbing, or dehydrating. Learn the four layers and their regional variations and you can describe any segment from oesophagus to anus.

Ileum Peyer’s patches; shortest villi

Large Intestine

No villi, straight tubular crypts, abundant goblet cells, and a muscularis externa whose outer longitudinal layer is gathered into three taeniae coli, producing haustra and appendices epiploicae. Function: water and electrolyte absorption, mucus secretion, bacterial fermentation. Appendix — a blind tube with abundant lymphoid tissue in the lamina propria and submucosa.

Anal canal — the transition from columnar to stratified squamous epithelium at the dentate (pectinate) line, an important clinical zone for haemorrhoids and anal cancer.

CLINICAL — Peptic ulcer — H. pylori breaches the mucous barrier; acid and pepsin digest the wall, classically in the duodenal bulb or gastric antrum. Pernicious anaemia — loss of parietal cells means no intrinsic factor, so B₁₂ cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum. Coeliac disease — villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and intraepithelial lymphocytes in the proximal small intestine, driven by gluten. Barrett oesophagus — chronic acid reflux causes metaplasia from stratified squamous to intestinal-type columnar epithelium with goblet cells, a premalignant change.

MICRO — Identify the segment by the surface, not the glands. Villi + crypts = small intestine. No villi + deep pits + thick gland mass = stomach. No villi + straight crypts + many goblet cells = colon. Then find the regional marker: Brunner glands = duodenum; Peyer’s patches = ileum; three muscle layers = stomach.

MNEMONIC — “Duodenum has Brunner Below; Ileum has Peyer’s Patches.” And “Filiform = Feel only; Circumvallate = Carry most taste.”

TIP — The stomach has no goblet cells and no villi; the intestine has both. This single contrast answers many identification questions.

16 · Organs Associated with the Digestive Tract

pp. 329–348 · Salivary Glands · Pancreas · Liver · Biliary Tract & Gallbladder

The core idea

Three gland systems share one duct system and one embryological origin: the salivary glands lubricate and begin digestion, the pancreas digests, the liver processes, and the gallbladder stores. Each is built around its job.

Salivary Glands

Parotid — serous only. Submandibular — mixed, predominantly serous. Sublingual — mixed, predominantly mucous.

The duct system: intercalated → striated (basal infoldings, ion transport) → interlobular. Myoepithelial cells surround the acini and compress them. The striated duct is the histological signature of a salivary gland and the reason saliva is hypotonic.

Pancreas

A compound acinar gland, entirely exocrine except for the scattered islets of Langerhans.

  • Exocrine acini — serous cells, intensely basophilic basal cytoplasm (RER) and eosinophilic apical zymogen granules. Centroacinar cells sit inside the acinus and begin the duct.
  • Duct system — intercalated → intralobular → interlobular → main pancreatic duct.
  • Endocrine islets — paler, round clusters between acini:

β (beta) Insulin ~60–70%
α (alpha) Glucagon ~20–25%
δ (delta) Somatostatin ~5–10%
PP Pancreatic polypeptide Small
ε Ghrelin Very small

The pancreas has no capsule separating lobules, which is why acini appear packed continuously.

Liver

Cords of hepatocytes radiate from a central vein, separated by sinusoids, with portal triads at the periphery of each lobule.

  • Capsule — Glisson capsule, dense connective tissue, with a mesothelial surface.
  • Classic lobule — a hexagon with a central vein at the centre and portal triads at the corners.
  • Portal triad — hepatic artery (supply), portal vein (nutrients), bile duct (drainage). Three structures with three different flow directions side by side — the single most examined arrangement in the chapter.
  • Hepatocytes — polygonal, eosinophilic, with abundant SER, RER, glycogen, and peroxisomes; arranged in plates one or two cells thick.
  • Sinusoids — discontinuous endothelium with Kupffer cells (macrophages) and Ito (stellate) cells (store vitamin A; become myofibroblasts in fibrosis).
  • Space of Disse — between endothelium and hepatocyte; where plasma exchanges with hepatocytes and where lymph forms.
  • Bile canaliculi — channels between adjacent hepatocytes, draining toward the portal triad. Bile flows toward the triad while blood flows away from it — opposite directions, and a favourite question.

Hepatic acinus and zonation. Zone 1 (periportal) receives the most oxygen and performs oxidative metabolism; zone 3 (pericentral) receives the least and performs biotransformation. Zone 3 dies first in ischaemia and is the site of alcoholic injury.

Functions: carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism; albumin and clotting factor synthesis; detoxification; bile production; bilirubin conjugation; storage of glycogen, iron, and vitamins A, D, and B₁₂.

Biliary Tract & Gallbladder

A sac with a folded mucosa of simple columnar epithelium, no muscularis mucosae and no submucosa, and a single muscular layer. It concentrates bile by absorbing water and secretes mucus. Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses are mucosal invaginations into the muscle wall — normal, but diagnostically useful in cholecystitis. The biliary tree itself (intrahepatic ducts → common hepatic duct → cystic duct → common bile duct) is lined by simple cuboidal to columnar epithelium.

CLINICAL — Cirrhosis is the end result of hepatocyte injury: fibrosis with regenerative nodules, portal hypertension, and synthetic failure. Acute pancreatitis is autodigestion by prematurely activated enzymes. Chronic pancreatitis destroys acini and islets, producing diabetes and steatorrhoea. Carcinoma of the head of the pancreas obstructs the common bile duct and produces painless obstructive jaundice — an anatomy lesson delivered as a symptom.

MICRO — The liver is recognised by cords of eosinophilic cells with central veins and portal triads. The pancreas by dark purple acini with pink apical granules and pale islets. Pancreas versus parotid: the pancreas has islets and no striated ducts; the parotid has striated ducts and no islets.

TIP — Bile flows toward the portal triad; blood flows away from it. Students who memorise “everything flows to the centre” lose this mark every year.

17 · The Respiratory System

pp. 349–370 · Nasal Cavities · Pharynx · Larynx · Trachea · Bronchial Tree & Lung · Lung Vasculature & Nerves · Pleural Membranes · Respiratory Movements

The core idea

The respiratory tract is a conducting series (nose to terminal bronchiole) followed by a respiratory series (respiratory bronchiole to alveolus), and the transition is marked by the disappearance of cartilage and the appearance of alveoli. Each level down the tree loses one structure and gains another.

Nasal Cavities

Vestibule (stratified squamous with hairs) → respiratory region (pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells, over a vascular lamina propria that warms and humidifies) → olfactory region (bipolar olfactory neurons, supporting cells, and Bowman glands; no goblet cells). Paranasal sinuses are lined by thinner respiratory epithelium.

Pharynx

Nasopharynx — respiratory epithelium with lymphoid tissue (pharyngeal tonsil) in the lamina propria. Oropharynx and laryngopharynx — stratified squamous epithelium, continuous with the oral cavity and oesophagus. The change from respiratory to stratified squamous epithelium marks the point where the airway is exposed to abrasion from food.

Larynx

Epiglottis — elastic cartilage with stratified squamous on one surface and respiratory epithelium on the other; the switch is abrupt. True vocal cords — stratified squamous epithelium, avascular, with the vocal ligament and skeletal muscle. False cords — respiratory epithelium, glandular.

Trachea

  • Epithelium — pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells (respiratory epithelium).
  • Lamina propria — elastic fibres, lymphocytes.
  • Submucosa — seromucous glands.
  • Cartilage — C-shaped hyaline rings, incomplete posteriorly.
  • Trachealis muscle — smooth muscle bridging the gap posteriorly, allowing the oesophagus to expand.

Bronchial Tree & Lung

Bronchi — cartilage in irregular plates rather than rings, a complete ring of smooth muscle, fewer glands.

Bronchioles — no cartilage, no glands, no goblet cells. Epithelium becomes simple ciliated columnar to cuboidal, with Clara (club) cells that secrete surfactant-like material and detoxify. Smooth muscle is relatively prominent — why bronchioles are the site of asthmatic bronchoconstriction.

Terminal bronchiole — the last conducting airway. Respiratory bronchiole → alveolar duct → alveolar sac → alveolus. Gas exchange begins at the respiratory bronchiole.

Cell Shape Function Proportion of surface
Type I pneumocyte Squamous, thin Gas exchange; forms the blood–air barrier ~95% of surface area
Type II pneumocyte Cuboidal, lamellar bodies Surfactant; progenitor for type I ~5%, but more numerous
Alveolar macrophage Large, free Phagocytosis —

Blood–air barrier = surfactant + type I pneumocyte + fused basement membranes + capillary endothelium, about 0.2–0.6 µm thick — the thinnest barrier in the body and the single most important determinant of diffusion. Pores of Kohn connect adjacent alveoli. Surfactant (dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine plus proteins) lowers surface tension and prevents collapse at end-expiration.

Lung Vasculature & Nerves

The lung has a double blood supply: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood for gas exchange and forms a dense capillary network around alveoli, while the bronchial arteries (from the aorta) supply the conducting airways and pleura. Pulmonary vessels are unusual in being arteries that carry deoxygenated blood and in having thin walls suited to low pressure. Autonomic nerves control bronchial smooth muscle and gland secretion — parasympathetic (vagal) constriction, sympathetic relaxation.

Pleural Membranes

A serous membrane of simple squamous mesothelium over connective tissue — visceral and parietal layers with a thin fluid film between them. The fluid allows the layers to slide and holds them together by surface tension, which is how the lungs follow the chest wall.

Respiratory Movements

Ventilation is a pressure-driven event: the diaphragm and intercostal muscles enlarge the thoracic cavity, pleural pressure falls, the lung expands passively, and air enters. Elastic recoil of the lung and surface tension at the alveolar air–liquid interface provide the force for expiration — which is precisely why surfactant deficiency causes collapse.

CLINICAL — Respiratory distress syndrome of the newborn — immature type II pneumocytes produce insufficient surfactant, so alveoli collapse; treatment is exogenous surfactant and antenatal steroids. Emphysema — destruction of alveolar walls enlarges air spaces and reduces surface area. Asthma — bronchoconstriction, mucosal oedema, eosinophilic inflammation, thickened basement membrane and smooth muscle hypertrophy. Chronic bronchitis — mucus hypersecretion and gland hypertrophy. Cystic fibrosis — defective CFTR thickens secretions, causing bronchiectasis and recurrent infection.

MICRO — Walk down the tree and count what disappears. Cartilage rings + glands = trachea. Cartilage plates + no glands = bronchus. No cartilage, no glands, muscular wall = bronchiole. Then look for alveoli in the wall: if present, it is a respiratory bronchiole.

MNEMONIC — “Rings, Plates, None” for cartilage from trachea → bronchus → bronchiole. And “Type I for gas, Type II for surfactant, macrophage for garbage.”

TIP — Clara cells are found in bronchioles, not alveoli, and are the progenitor cells of the bronchiolar epithelium. A question that places them in the alveolus is testing whether you know the level of the airway.

The core idea

Skin is a stratified, keratinising, self-renewing barrier with appendages embedded in it. The epidermis is a factory that moves cells outward and turns them into keratin; the dermis provides mechanical and nutritional support; the appendages provide temperature control, sensation, and protection.

Dermis

  • Papillary dermis — loose connective tissue forming dermal papillae that interdigitate with the epidermis; capillaries and Meissner corpuscles.
  • Reticular dermis — dense irregular connective tissue with thick collagen bundles and elastic fibres.

Thick versus thin skin. Thick skin (palms, soles) has a thick stratum corneum, a stratum lucidum, and no hair follicles; it has many sweat glands. Thin skin covers most of the body, has hair follicles and sebaceous glands, and a thin epidermis.

Subcutaneous Tissue

The hypodermis — loose connective tissue and adipose tissue (panniculus adiposus) attaching skin to deep fascia. It insulates, cushions, and stores energy, and it is the layer that determines how a wound behaves and how an injection is absorbed.

Hair

A downgrowth of epidermis; the bulb contains the papilla (blood supply) and matrix (proliferation); arrector pili smooth muscle raises the hair; a root plexus provides sensation. Hair growth cycles through anagen (growth), catagen (involution), and telogen (rest).

Nails

A plate of keratinised cells produced by the nail matrix; the lunula is the visible part of the matrix; the hyponychium is the undersurface.

Skin Glands

Sebaceous glands — holocrine, secreting sebum into the hair follicle; absent on palms and soles.

Eccrine sweat glands — merocrine, coiled tubular, with a secretory portion of cuboidal cells and a duct of stratified cuboidal cells; abundant on palms and soles; watery sweat for thermoregulation.

Apocrine sweat glands — axilla, areola, and perianal region; larger lumens, odour after bacterial action.

Skin Repair

First intention — clean, apposed edges; minimal granulation. Second intention — open wounds; abundant granulation tissue, contraction, and a larger scar. Burns are classified by depth: first-degree (epidermis), second-degree (into dermis, with blistering), third-degree (full thickness, including appendages, so it cannot re-epithelialise).

CLINICAL — Psoriasis — accelerated keratinocyte turnover with parakeratosis, acanthosis, and Munro microabscesses. Melanoma — malignant melanocytes; the Breslow thickness (depth of invasion) predicts survival, which is why the dermis matters. Vitiligo — autoimmune destruction of melanocytes. Basal cell carcinoma — from basal keratinocytes, locally invasive but rarely metastasises. Squamous cell carcinoma — from spinous cells, can metastasise. Pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid are the blistering diseases of desmosomes and hemidesmosomes described in Chapter 4.

MICRO — Count layers and look for appendages. Thick skin: five layers, thick keratin, no hair. Thin skin: four layers, hair follicles and sebaceous glands. A dark condensed nucleus at the basal layer with a clear halo suggests a melanocyte.

TIP — “Melanocytes are more numerous in dark skin” is false. Melanocyte number is similar across races; melanin production and transfer differ.

MNEMONIC — “Come Let’s Get Some Beer” — Corneum, Lucidum, Granulosum, Spinosum, Basale (superficial to deep) — and remember lucidum exists only in thick skin.

19 · The Urinary System

pp. 393–412 · Kidneys · Blood Circulation · Renal Function: Filtration, Secretion, & Reabsorption · Ureters, Bladder, & Urethra

The core idea

The kidney is a countercurrent filtration and reabsorption machine built from millions of identical tubes. Each segment of the tubule has a distinct epithelium matched to a distinct transport job, so identifying the segment from its histology is the same as naming its function.

Kidneys

  • Capsule → cortex (renal corpuscles + convoluted tubules) → medulla (loops of Henle, collecting ducts, vasa recta) organised into pyramids.
  • Renal columns (of Bertin) — cortical tissue between pyramids.
  • Lobe = pyramid + overlying cortex; lobule = a medullary ray + the cortical tissue around it.
  • Papilla → minor calyx → major calyx → renal pelvis → ureter.

The nephron begins with the renal corpuscle: the glomerulus (a tuft of fenestrated capillaries) inside Bowman’s capsule.

  • Parietal layer — simple squamous epithelium.
  • Visceral layer — podocytes with primary and secondary processes (foot processes, pedicels) that embrace the capillaries; the filtration slits between pedicels are bridged by the slit diaphragm containing nephrin.
  • Mesangial cells — between capillaries; contractile, phagocytic, structural.

The filtration barrier has three layers: fenestrated endothelium (no diaphragms) → glomerular basement membrane (type IV collagen, laminin, heparan sulfate; the charge barrier) → podocyte slit diaphragms (the size barrier). Small molecules pass; cells and large proteins do not. Loss of the charge barrier produces albuminuria before loss of the size barrier — which explains early diabetic proteinuria.

Juxtaglomerular apparatus — at the vascular pole: juxtaglomerular (JG) cells in the afferent arteriole (modified smooth muscle, contain renin), the macula densa (a plaque of tall, closely packed cells in the distal tubule sensing NaCl), and extraglomerular mesangial (lacis) cells.

Blood Circulation

Renal artery → segmental → interlobar (between pyramids) → arcuate (at the corticomedullary junction) → interlobular (radiating into the cortex) → afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries in the cortex and vasa recta in the medulla. The vasa recta run in a hairpin alongside the loop of Henle and are the anatomical basis of the countercurrent multiplier that creates the medullary osmotic gradient.

The visual contrast that answers most questions: the proximal tubule is eosinophilic with a ragged brush border and a star-shaped lumen; the distal tubule is pale with a clean lumen and no brush border.

Ureters, Bladder, & Urethra

Ureter — transitional epithelium, a star-shaped lumen, and a muscular wall of two layers (inner longitudinal, outer circular) in the upper part, three in the lower. Bladder — transitional epithelium, a thick detrusor muscle in interlacing bundles, and a trigone where the epithelium is firmly attached. Urethra — male: prostatic (transitional), membranous (stratified columnar), spongy (pseudostratified/stratified squamous); female: short, lined by stratified squamous near the external meatus.

CLINICAL — Diabetic nephropathy thickens the glomerular basement membrane and later produces mesangial expansion (Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules), with proteinuria. Nephrotic syndrome — heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia; podocyte injury is central. Acute tubular necrosis — ischaemic or toxic injury to the proximal tubule, the most metabolically demanding segment. Polycystic kidney disease — the primary cilium and its signalling fail. Renal cell carcinoma arises from proximal tubule cells; transitional cell carcinoma from the urothelium.

MICRO — Start at the renal corpuscle: the glomerulus is a tuft of capillaries inside a capsule. Then compare tubules: eosinophilic + brush border = proximal; pale + clean = distal; large pale cells in a row = collecting duct. The macula densa is the patch of tall cells on the distal tubule facing the vascular pole.

TIP — The proximal tubule is the most eosinophilic structure in the renal cortex, packed with mitochondria for active transport. Students often call it “basophilic” because the brush border looks fuzzy — the cytoplasm is pink.

20 · Endocrine Glands

pp. 413–438 · Pituitary Gland (Hypophysis) · Adrenal Glands · Pancreatic Islets · Diffuse Neuroendocrine System · Thyroid Gland · Parathyroid Glands · Pineal Gland

The core idea

Endocrine glands release hormone into the blood, so their histology is organised around storage and release rather than ducts. The three patterns to recognise are cords (pituitary, adrenal, parathyroid), follicles (thyroid), and clusters (pancreatic islets). Each pattern is dictated by how the hormone is stored.

Pituitary Gland (Hypophysis)

Two embryologically distinct parts in one sella turcica.

Adenohypophysis (oral ectoderm — Rathke’s pouch):

Region Cells Hormones
Pars distalis Acidophils GH, prolactin
Basophils ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH
Chromophobes Inactive or degranulated
Pars intermedia Basophils, colloid cysts MSH (rudimentary in humans)
Pars tuberalis — Wraps the infundibulum

Neurohypophysis (neuroectoderm): the pars nervosa stores oxytocin and ADH synthesised in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus and transported along axons. Herring bodies are dilatations of these axons containing hormone; pituicytes are the glial cells of the lobe.

Control: the hypothalamus releases releasing and inhibiting hormones into the hypophyseal portal system, carrying them directly to the pars distalis — which is why a tiny hypothalamic signal controls a large pituitary output.

Adrenal Glands

Two glands in one organ, with entirely different embryology and function:

Region Cells Hormone Control
Zona glomerulosa Small clusters under the capsule Aldosterone Angiotensin II, K⁺
Zona fasciculata Long cords of pale, foamy cells Cortisol ACTH
Zona reticularis Irregular cords, pigmented cells Androgens (DHEA) ACTH
Medulla Chromaffin cells (neural crest) Epinephrine, norepinephrine Sympathetic splanchnic nerve

The fasciculata is the thickest zone and its cells are pale and vacuolated because they are full of lipid and smooth ER for steroid synthesis. The medulla is functionally a sympathetic ganglion without axons. Mnemonic for the zones: “GFR — Salt, Sugar, Sex” from outside in.

Pancreatic Islets

Pale, round clusters between the acini of the exocrine pancreas (Chapter 16): β (insulin, 60–70%), α (glucagon, 20–25%), δ (somatostatin), PP, ε (ghrelin). Islets are richly vascularised, and their hormones pass directly into the bloodstream rather than into ducts.

Diffuse Neuroendocrine System

Scattered hormone-secreting cells throughout the body — gut enteroendocrine cells, respiratory neuroendocrine cells, and similar — sharing the ability to take up amine precursors (APUD cells). They are the reason a “pure” endocrine organ is not the only source of hormones: the gut alone produces more regulatory peptides than any single gland.

Thyroid Gland

  • Follicles of varying size filled with colloid (thyroglobulin), lined by simple cuboidal epithelium.
  • Follicular cells — synthesise T3 and T4. Activity is read from the epithelium: active gland = tall columnar cells with scalloped colloid; inactive gland = flat cells with dense colloid.
  • Parafollicular (C) cells — between follicles, paler and larger, of neural crest origin, secreting calcitonin.
  • Folliculogenesis: thyroglobulin is synthesised, iodinated at the apical surface, stored as colloid, taken back by endocytosis, and digested to release T3/T4.

Parathyroid Glands

  • Chief cells — small, pale, secretory cells producing PTH.
  • Oxyphil cells — larger, eosinophilic, abundant mitochondria, function uncertain; increase with age.
  • Arranged in cords with fat and capillaries; no follicles.

Pineal Gland

Pinealocytes (modified neurons) in cords, with corpora arenacea (brain sand) — calcified concretions visible on X-ray. Secretes melatonin in darkness under sympathetic control; a biological clock component, and a favourite “identify this organ” image because of the concretions.

CLINICAL — Graves disease — diffuse hyperthyroidism with tall columnar follicular cells, scalloped colloid, and lymphoid infiltration. Hashimoto thyroiditis — autoimmune destruction with Hürthle cells and germinal centres. Cushing syndrome — excess cortisol, with fasciculata hyperplasia or an adrenal adenoma. Conn syndrome — aldosterone excess from a glomerulosa adenoma. Pheochromocytoma — a chromaffin-cell tumour producing episodic hypertension. Diabetes mellitus — progressive β-cell loss. Pituitary adenoma — bitemporal hemianopia from compression of the optic chiasm plus a hormone syndrome.

MICRO — Three patterns, three organs. Follicles of colloid = thyroid. Cords of pale foamy cells under a capsule = adrenal cortex; the medulla is paler and central. Small pale chief cells in dense cords = parathyroid. Pituitary: acidophils and basophils in sheets (anterior); pale fibrillary tissue without epithelial cells (posterior).

TIP — The adrenal cortex has three zones and the medulla has none of the cortex’s function. Catecholamines are medullary.

21 · The Male Reproductive System

pp. 439–459 · Testes · Intratesticular Ducts · Excretory Genital Ducts · Accessory Glands · Penis

The core idea

The testis does two jobs that require opposite conditions: producing sperm, which needs a temperature below core (hence the scrotum), and producing testosterone, which needs a rich blood supply. The architecture keeps them apart by separating the seminiferous tubules from the interstitium.

Testes

  • Tunica albuginea — thick capsule; the mediastinum testis sends septa dividing the gland into lobules.
  • Seminiferous tubules — lined by a stratified epithelium of germ cells plus Sertoli cells, on a basal lamina, surrounded by peritubular myoid cells.
  • Interstitium — Leydig cells, blood vessels, connective tissue.

Sertoli cells extend from base to lumen; their nuclei are pale, oval, and have a prominent nucleolus. They are not germ-line cells. Functions:

  • Physical support and nutrition of developing germ cells.
  • Blood–testis barrier — tight junctions between adjacent Sertoli cells near the base, creating basal and adluminal compartments. This is why the immune system does not reject sperm.
  • Phagocytosis of residual bodies.
  • Secretion of androgen-binding protein (ABP), inhibin, anti-Müllerian hormone, transferrin, and plasminogen activator.
  • Response to FSH — which acts on Sertoli cells, not on germ cells.

Spermatogenesis: spermatogonia (type A dark/pale → type B) → primary spermatocyte → secondary spermatocyte → spermatid → spermatozoon. Meiosis I halves the chromosome number and meiosis II separates chromatids, giving four spermatids per primary spermatocyte. Spermiogenesis is the metamorphosis into a sperm: acrosome from the Golgi, nuclear condensation, flagellum growth, shedding of residual cytoplasm. Spermiation releases the sperm into the lumen.

Because the barrier makes the adluminal compartment immunologically privileged, sperm antigens are never tolerised — which is why vasectomy can produce anti-sperm antibodies.

Leydig cells — large, polygonal, eosinophilic cells with abundant SER and lipid droplets, sometimes containing Reinke crystals; they secrete testosterone under LH stimulation.

Intratesticular Ducts

Rete testis (simple cuboidal) collects sperm from the seminiferous tubules and delivers them through the efferent ductules (ciliated and absorptive columnar) to the epididymis.

Excretory Genital Ducts

Segment Epithelium Function
Epididymis Pseudostratified columnar with stereocilia Maturation and storage of sperm
Ductus (vas) deferens Pseudostratified columnar Transport; very thick muscular wall (inner longitudinal, middle circular, outer longitudinal)
Ejaculatory duct Columnar Delivers into the prostatic urethra

Accessory Glands

  • Seminal vesicles — folded mucosa, secretory cells rich in fructose and prostaglandins; about 60% of semen volume.
  • Prostate — tubuloalveolar glands in a fibromuscular stroma; corpora amylacea are characteristic. Glands are arranged in zones: the peripheral zone is where most carcinomas arise; the transition zone is where BPH arises.
  • Bulbourethral (Cowper) glands — mucin for lubrication.

Penis

Three erectile bodies: two corpora cavernosa and one corpus spongiosum containing the urethra. Each is a sponge of endothelium-lined cavernous spaces surrounded by smooth muscle and a tunica albuginea. Erection is a vascular event: parasympathetic nitric oxide dilates the helicine arteries, filling the spaces and compressing venous outflow.

CLINICAL — Cryptorchidism — an undescended testis is exposed to core temperature, so spermatogenesis fails and malignancy risk rises; orchidopexy is performed early. Varicocele — dilation of the pampiniform plexus raises testicular temperature and impairs spermatogenesis. Prostatic carcinoma — arises in the peripheral zone, is palpable on rectal examination, and metastasises to bone. BPH — nodular hyperplasia of the transition zone, compressing the urethra. Testicular tumours — germ-cell tumours in young men; a painless mass is malignant until proven otherwise.

MICRO — Identify the two compartments instantly. Inside the tubule: Sertoli cells (pale, tall) and germ cells at various stages. Outside the tubule: Leydig cells (eosinophilic, polygonal, in clusters). If asked for the cell that forms the blood–testis barrier, the answer is Sertoli.

TIP — FSH acts on Sertoli cells; LH acts on Leydig cells. One sentence, and it accounts for many marks.

22 · The Female Reproductive System

The core idea

The ovary is the only adult structure that must rebuild itself through a monthly cycle. Every histological feature of the ovary, tube, uterus, and vagina is a stage in one of two cycles — ovarian (follicle to corpus luteum) and endometrial (proliferative to secretory) — locked together by the same four hormones.

Two-cell, two-gonadotropin theory: LH stimulates theca interna cells to produce androgens; FSH stimulates granulosa cells to aromatise them into oestrogen. This division of labour explains why both gonadotropins are needed for normal oestrogen production.

Uterine Tubes

Four parts — infundibulum with fimbriae, ampulla, isthmus, intramural portion. The mucosa is folded and lined by ciliated columnar cells (move the ovum) and peg (secretory) cells; the muscular wall is thin.

Major Events of Fertilization

Fertilisation normally occurs in the ampulla. The sperm penetrates the corona radiata and zona pellucida, the membranes fuse, the oocyte completes meiosis II, and the zygote forms. The zygote divides as it travels toward the uterus, reaching the blastocyst stage by the time it arrives — about five days.

Uterus

  • Perimetrium (serosa), myometrium (thick smooth muscle in interlacing bundles), endometrium.
  • Endometrium — stratum functionalis (shed each month) and stratum basalis (retained, regenerates it). Spiral arteries supply the functionalis and constrict to cause shedding.
Phase Days Hormone Histology
Menstrual 1–4 Falling progesterone Shedding, haemorrhage, neutrophilic infiltrate
Proliferative 5–14 Oestrogen Glands straight and narrow, mitoses, dense stroma
Secretory 15–28 Progesterone Glands tortuous and dilated with secretion, oedematous stroma, coiled spiral arteries

The single best discriminator: straight, narrow glands = proliferative; saw-toothed, dilated, secreting glands = secretory.

Embryonic Implantation, Decidua, & the Placenta

The blastocyst implants around day 6–7. The trophoblast differentiates into cytotrophoblast (mitotic, inner) and syncytiotrophoblast (invasive, outer). Chorionic villi project into maternal blood in the intervillous space.

Decidua — the modified endometrium of pregnancy: decidua basalis (under the embryo, forms the maternal placenta), decidua capsularis (over it), decidua parietalis (the rest).

Placental hormones: hCG (maintains the corpus luteum early; the basis of pregnancy tests), human placental lactogen, progesterone, and oestrogen.

Cervix

Endocervical canal lined by simple columnar mucus-secreting cells; the ectocervix by stratified squamous epithelium. They meet at the squamocolumnar junction, and the zone between original and current junction is the transformation zone — the site of HPV-related dysplasia and the target of the Pap smear.

Vagina

Stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium rich in glycogen, no glands (lubrication comes from cervical and Bartholin glands), and a lamina propria with elastic fibres and venous plexuses. Lactobacillus metabolises glycogen to lactic acid, maintaining an acidic pH.

External Genitalia

The vulva: the labia majora (skin with hair and sebaceous glands, containing the homologue of the scrotum), labia minora (thin skin without hair, with sebaceous glands), the clitoris (erectile tissue homologous to the penis), and the vestibule containing the urethral and vaginal openings and the openings of the greater vestibular (Bartholin) glands, which provide lubrication.

Mammary Glands

A modified apocrine sweat gland. In the non-lactating state the gland is mostly dense connective tissue and adipose tissue with small ducts; during pregnancy lobules proliferate; during lactation alveoli are distended with secretion. The lactiferous ducts converge on the nipple, with lactiferous sinuses beneath it.

Hormonal control: oestrogen for ductal growth, progesterone for lobuloalveolar development, prolactin for milk synthesis, oxytocin for milk ejection.

CLINICAL — Polycystic ovary syndrome — multiple arrested antral follicles, anovulation, hyperandrogenism. Endometriosis — functional endometrial tissue outside the uterus, bleeding cyclically. Leiomyoma (fibroid) — a benign smooth-muscle tumour of the myometrium. Endometrial carcinoma — unopposed oestrogen, with atypical hyperplasia. Cervical carcinoma — HPV in the transformation zone. Ectopic pregnancy — implantation outside the uterine cavity, usually in the ampulla. Breast carcinoma — arises from ductal or lobular epithelium; the terminal duct lobular unit is the functional unit to remember.

MICRO — Place the ovary in the cycle by the dominant structure: many small primordial follicles = inactive/early; a large antral follicle = late follicular; a corpus luteum with folded, pale, luteinised cells = secretory phase; a hyaline scar = post-ovulatory. In the uterus, look at the glands, not the stroma.

MNEMONIC — “Proliferative = Pencil-thin glands; Secretory = Saw-tooth and swollen.”

TIP — The corpus luteum secretes progesterone, and it is maintained by LH (and, in pregnancy, by hCG). Do not say oestrogen maintains the corpus luteum.

23 · The Eye & Ear: Special Sense Organs

The core idea

Both organs convert a physical signal into a nerve impulse through a specialised epithelium — the retina for light, the organ of Corti for sound. Their histology is therefore about transduction, not merely structure.

Eyes: The Photoreceptor System

Three tunics:

Tunic Components Function
Fibrous Cornea and sclera Protection and support; the cornea also refracts
Vascular (uvea) Choroid, ciliary body, iris Nutrition, accommodation, pupil control
Nervous Retina Phototransduction

The cornea has five layers, and the order is a standard question:

  1. Corneal epithelium — stratified squamous, non-keratinised.
  2. Bowman’s membrane — acellular collagen.
  3. Stroma — regular lamellae of type I collagen and keratocytes; avascular.
  4. Descemet’s membrane — thick basal lamina.
  5. Corneal endothelium — simple squamous; pumps water out to maintain transparency.

The cornea is transparent because it is avascular and its collagen is regularly arranged. When it becomes oedematous or scarred, it loses transparency — the anatomical explanation for corneal blindness.

  • Choroid — pigmented, vascular; nourishes the outer retina.
  • Ciliary body — ciliary muscle (accommodation) and ciliary processes (aqueous humour secretion) with zonular fibres suspending the lens.
  • Iris — pigment epithelium and a stroma with sphincter pupillae (constricts, parasympathetic) and dilator pupillae (dilates, sympathetic).

Aqueous humour flows from the posterior chamber through the pupil into the anterior chamber and drains through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm. Blockage raises intraocular pressure — glaucoma.

The lens: capsule → subcapsular epithelium → lens fibres (elongated cells packed with crystallins, without organelles). New fibres are added at the equator throughout life, so the lens grows and its nucleus becomes denser. Loss of transparency is cataract.

The retina — five of its ten layers matter for exams:

Layer Contents
Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) Pigmented; absorbs stray light, phagocytoses outer segments, regenerates visual pigment
Photoreceptor layer Rods (rhodopsin, dim light, ~120 million) and cones (iodopsin, colour, ~6 million)
Outer nuclear layer Photoreceptor nuclei
Inner nuclear layer Bipolar, horizontal, amacrine, and Müller cell nuclei
Ganglion cell layer Ganglion cells whose axons form the optic nerve

The retina is inverted: light passes through the ganglion cell and inner layers before reaching the photoreceptors, which sit against the pigment epithelium. The macula lutea — and its centre, the fovea centralis — contains only cones, has the highest acuity, and is the site of central vision. The optic disc is the blind spot, because photoreceptors are absent there. Müller cells are the principal glia.

Ears: The Vestibuloauditory System

External ear — auricle of elastic cartilage covered by skin; external acoustic meatus lined by skin with ceruminous glands and hairs; tympanic membrane with a fibrous core.

Middle ear — air-filled tympanic cavity with the ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes); the auditory tube equalises pressure; the oval window and round window transmit and relieve pressure waves.

Inner ear — bony labyrinth containing the membranous labyrinth:

  • Cochlea — the organ of Corti on the basilar membrane, with inner and outer hair cells (the transducers), a tectorial membrane that deflects their stereocilia, supporting cells, and the spiral ganglion. The stria vascularis secretes endolymph.
  • Vestibule — utricle and saccule, each with a macula containing hair cells and otoliths (gravity and linear acceleration).
  • Semicircular canals — cristae ampullares with hair cells in a cupula (rotational acceleration).

Endolymph (high K⁺) fills the membranous labyrinth; perilymph (high Na⁺) fills the bony labyrinth. The ionic difference is the battery that drives transduction.

CLINICAL — Glaucoma — raised intraocular pressure from impaired drainage through the trabecular meshwork, damaging the optic nerve. Cataract — lens opacity from crystallin denaturation. Age-related macular degeneration — degeneration of the RPE and photoreceptors at the macula. Retinal detachment — separation of the neural retina from the RPE, which starves the photoreceptors. Diabetic retinopathy — microvascular damage with haemorrhage and neovascularisation. Otosclerosis — ossification of the stapes footplate, producing conductive deafness. Presbycusis — age-related loss of hair cells and neurons. Ménière disease — endolymphatic hydrops with vertigo, tinnitus, and fluctuating deafness.

MICRO — The cornea is identified by five layers and a regular, avascular stroma. The retina by its ten layers with a pigmented layer at the outer border and ganglion cells at the inner border. The organ of Corti by the tunnel of Corti between inner and outer hair cells on the basilar membrane, with the tectorial membrane above.

TIP — Rods detect dim light, cones detect colour and detail; the fovea has only cones, which is why acuity is highest there and colour vision is lost first in the periphery.
