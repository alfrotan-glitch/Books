# Medical & Scientific Accuracy Review

This is not copyediting. The manuscript can be grammatically perfect and
still be medically wrong — actively look for that, don't just assume
correctness because the prose reads confidently.

## What to actively hunt for

Outdated medical information; incorrect anatomy, physiology, or
pathology; incorrect pharmacology; incorrect diagnostic criteria;
incorrect or outdated clinical recommendations; obsolete classifications
or terminology; incorrect lab values or units; incorrect epidemiological
claims; internal contradictions; misleading oversimplifications; unsafe
clinical statements; and unsupported claims presented as settled fact.

For every clinically important claim, verify against current
authoritative sources when external research is available (web search).
Don't silently "modernize" a claim without verifying — and don't
fabricate the appearance of having verified something you didn't.

## Evidence tiers — keep these distinct in your own reasoning

- Established medical fact
- Current guideline recommendation
- Textbook explanation / teaching simplification
- Expert consensus
- Emerging evidence
- Uncertain evidence
- Outdated information (flag for replacement or removal)

When you correct something, know which tier the correction rests on, and
say so in the change log if it's not obvious (e.g. "updated per current
guideline X" vs. "textbook convention, unchanged").

## References and citations

- Preserve and improve existing references where the manuscript already
  has them.
- Where an important claim lacks a citation, flag it for verification
  rather than either leaving it unsupported or inventing one.
- **Never invent citations, DOIs, study names, authors, or journals.**
  This is an absolute rule, not a style preference — a fabricated
  citation is worse than no citation, because it looks verified.
- When external research is available, prefer authoritative sources:
  WHO, CDC, major professional medical societies, recognized clinical
  guidelines, peer-reviewed/PubMed-indexed literature, major medical
  textbooks, and official government health authorities.
- For rapidly evolving topics (e.g. active outbreak guidance, recently
  revised diagnostic criteria), do an explicit currency check — state
  the date/version of the guidance you're relying on if it matters.

## Medical safety layer — stop-the-line issues

These get flagged immediately, ahead of general editing, because getting
them wrong in a published clinical education book has real-world stakes:

- Potentially dangerous clinical instructions
- Incorrect doses
- Contraindication errors
- Drug interaction errors
- Diagnostic errors
- Unsafe treatment claims
- Outdated emergency recommendations
- Misleading patient-management instructions

**Never "improve" dangerous content for stylistic elegance.** If a
sentence is unsafe, the fix is medical correctness first; a beautifully
written unsafe sentence is still unsafe.

## When storytelling/narrative framing is involved

For books that teach through clinical narrative or case-based scenes
(rather than a plain textbook register): storytelling may simplify
*language and presentation* — pacing, dialogue, how a concept is
revealed — but must never simplify anatomy, physiology, pathophysiology,
clinical reasoning, or evidence-based practice in a way that becomes
inaccurate. Drama does not get a pass on accuracy. If a scene's dramatic
version and its medically accurate version conflict, accuracy wins, and
you adjust the scene, not the medicine.

## Recording corrections

Classify every substantive scientific/clinical correction (see
`references/editorial-workflow.md` for the full classification scheme)
and, where practical, preserve the original wording in the audit trail so
the author can see exactly what changed and why. Don't silently
overwrite a clinical claim — that removes the author's ability to
disagree or double-check your correction.
